About

Method

This book compares how school (and a few near-school) history textbooks narrate the same modern events. It is not a survey of what “really happened.” It is a survey of classrooms: eleven national textbooks, eight languages, read side by side, one event at a time.

Who wrote this

The chapters were drafted and revised by an AI — Claude, a language model — which read all eleven textbooks in the corpus and is, in effect, the book’s twelfth narrator: the only one in the room without a country, a ministry, or a childhood classroom. That is a stance, not a neutrality claim. An AI is made of human text and carries its own tilt, which is why nothing in this book asks for trust in the narrator: every claim about a textbook ends in a passage, an edition, and a page number the reader can check.

One human read every chapter as it was written — choosing the threads worth following, rejecting what did not land, and holding the book to a single standard: a reader who finished school long ago should come away understanding something real about the history their own family lived through. Where the book works, that collaboration is why.

Rules

  • Quotes or nothing — no claim about a textbook without a passage, edition, and page.
  • Machine translation is a map — English research extracts locate pages; chapter quotes from non-English books use the original line plus a reading translation.
  • Absence is graded — a missing topic is avoidance only when most in-scope books cover it; shared silence is curriculum norm. The rule is symmetric: a topic only a few books make room for is a signature, and what it does for them is the finding.
  • Causal precision — a precondition is never promoted into a cause; the prose says exactly as much as the receipts support.
  • No contempt register — the coda asks the reader to check their own book, which fails if the prose has already taught them to sneer.

Ancestors

The nearest ancestor is Marc Ferro’s Comment on raconte l’histoire aux enfants (1981; in English, The Use and Abuse of History: Or How the Past Is Taught to Children) — a French historian reading the world’s classrooms one country at a time, by hand. Ferro showed that children in different countries inherit different pasts; his twelve years of Histoire parallèle on television put two countries’ newsreels of the same week side by side and let the gap speak. What he described, this book tries to measure — with the quote ladders, and with page-space counts across one shared spine of events. The other debts are to the Annales school’s habit of following matter (grain, silver, nitrogen) underneath politics, and to Ernest Renan’s old observation that a nation is partly made of what it agrees to forget.

The instruments

The measurements stay out of the chapters on purpose — a reader should never be handed arithmetic where a sentence will do. They live here instead: the topic × book space matrix records how much of each book’s page-space each event receives, measured mechanically from page extents and confirmed by a second blind pass. Chapter Receipts sections carry the per-claim counts (“in the corpus: five of eleven classrooms…”), with editions and pages, so any sentence in the reading prose can be traced to its evidence.

Form

Each episode chapter follows a thread — usually a physical thing: a leaf, a bark, a bar of gold, a punch card — and keeps a fixed spine of furniture: a ladder of tellings where the rows earn their place, credit where due, a limits passage, receipts, and the same coda every time. The introduction teaches the rubrics once; the chapters then stay out of the reader’s way.
