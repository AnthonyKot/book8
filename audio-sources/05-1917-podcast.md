# 1917

On the night of 25 October 1917 — 7 November on the calendar you use — armed men in Petrograd take the telegraph offices, the railway station, the bridges and the power station. Then they walk into the Winter Palace and arrest the ministers sitting round a table inside. The streetcars are running while they do it. The theatres and restaurants are open.  Deaths at the palace that night are counted on one hand.  Almost nobody carries that picture. The picture almost everybody carries — a vast crowd pouring over iron gates in the dark, under fire — was made later, on purpose, twice.

### 1 The picture

The war of 1914 reached Petrograd first as bread queues and then as a garrison that would not fire on them. In February, shortages and strikes grew into mass mutinies. The Tsar ordered force, lost the garrison and abdicated. A Provisional Government then shared power with the Petrograd Soviet and kept Russia in the war.

Lenin returned from exile across Germany in April, promising power to the Soviets and an exit from the fighting. The July Days demonstrations failed, and he went into hiding. In August, General Kornilov's march on Petrograd collapsed after Kerensky released and armed Bolshevik factory workers as Red Guards. The government survived each immediate crisis. By October, the men moving through the city had a practical list: stations, bridges, telegraph, power, palace.

The two dates attached to that night are not rival claims. Russia still used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar used almost everywhere else. The October Revolution therefore happened in November on the calendar now used in a phone.

It was a checklist executed by a few thousand people in a city of over two million.  The palace defenders were cadets and a women's battalion. The ministers were arrested in a dining room. The cruiser *Aurora* fired a blank.  Outside, the streetcars ran. Nothing in that description looks large enough to become the founding scene of a state that would organise a third of the planet's politics for seventy years. The outcome was immense. Its opening night had produced almost no spectacle.

So the spectacle was built, in public, at anniversaries. On 7 November 1920, the third anniversary, the director Nikolai Evreinov staged *The Storming of the Winter Palace* on Palace Square itself. Around eight thousand performers, many of them Red Army soldiers, moved under searchlights to artillery sound, with the *Aurora* in the river and an audience estimated at a hundred thousand.  The government made on the quiet night had held power for three years. Now the night acquired a crowd big enough to resemble what followed.

For the tenth anniversary, the state commissioned Sergei Eisenstein to film it again. *October* was shot during 1927 in and around the real palace and released in 1928. The crowd swarms the wrought-iron gates, pours through the courtyard and floods the staterooms.  Frames from the sequence have been reprinted and rebroadcast as though a camera had been present in 1917. No camera was.

The re-enactment and film did not make the revolution or make it succeed. What the anniversaries produced was the visual record that the small night had not generated for itself: evidence manufactured afterwards at the scale the outcome seemed to require, then absorbed into the common stock of things people have seen. The event was over. The picture was the part still being decided.

A phone now makes this mechanism ordinary. People enter a government building; a few seconds of footage arrive before their meaning does. The caption soon chooses among the same three nouns: revolution, insurrection, coup. The picture is not sufficient. The noun tells the viewer whether a people, an organised minority or a small group at the top is supposed to be acting. That is not an analogy laid over 1917. It is the same gap between an image and the story attached to it, running with better cameras.

Go back, then, to the streetcar. The men taking the city had the power station on their checklist, and the current stayed on. Three years later somebody built a more memorable version on the real square with eight thousand performers, and a film crew built it again a decade after the night. The night underneath it remained the size of an ordinary evening: theatres open, restaurants open, trams keeping to their routes while the ministers were arrested indoors. Whatever noun belongs to the event, that is the traffic it happened in.
