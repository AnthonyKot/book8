# The Great Depression: Coffee in the Firebox

In 1931, men in overcoats and hats queued on a Chicago pavement for free food. The soup kitchen had been funded by Al Capone. In the same years, across the Atlantic, Brazil was buying coffee in order to destroy it: burning it in the open, dumping it at sea, and shovelling it into locomotive fireboxes.

The connection between the queue and the fire began with a collapse in share prices. On 24 October 1929, thirteen million shares changed hands on Wall Street. The following Tuesday, sixteen million did. By mid-November, quoted prices were roughly half their September level. Nothing had been shelled and no border had moved, yet the shock reached a crop thousands of miles from the New York exchange.

Coffee supplied roughly seven-tenths of Brazil’s export earnings in 1929. For a generation, the state had protected the world price by warehousing surplus beans and borrowing abroad against the stock. In an ordinary bad harvest or weak season, the arrangement could work: buy the surplus, wait for demand to recover, and repay the loan when the price rose.

The crash broke both sides of that calculation. American demand collapsed, and New York banks called in foreign loans. Brazil could neither sell the crop nor keep borrowing against it. Between 1931 and 1944, it destroyed roughly seventy-eight million sixty-kilo bags of coffee. The fires were not an act of madness; they were the last lever available in a system designed to defend a price.

The political order moved with the market. In October 1930, the coffee oligarchy lost its hold on Brazil’s presidency and Getúlio Vargas took power. A state coffee department was created to run the destruction as policy. A country whose farmers owned no shares in New York had its national income cut in half by a fall there, then used the instrument it still controlled: the supply of its own crop.

Trade policy carried the crisis further. In June 1930, President Herbert Hoover signed the Smoot–Hawley tariff despite a public appeal from 1,028 economists urging a veto. Other countries retaliated. The value of world trade fell by roughly two-thirds in three years.

The tariff did not cause the Depression. Banks failed, the money supply contracted, and governments tied to the gold standard often tightened policy when they needed room to spend. Countries that left gold earlier recovered earlier: Britain did so in 1931, while France held on until 1936. But tariffs made the slump travel. They narrowed the route through which other countries earned dollars to buy American goods or pay American debts. Retaliation made the same choice rational elsewhere. The contraction moved through customs houses as well as banks, reaching a cotton budget in Khartoum and a coffee warehouse in Santos.

The pattern remains recognisable in a bag of coffee. It names a country and a roaster, but neither fixes the price behind it. That price can be set on an exchange in New York or London. The people who grow the beans have to live with it much as everyone else lives with the weather. In the 1930s, a crop became worth more in a firebox than in a cup.

Eight textbooks in this comparison cover the crisis, but they give it different shapes. The Indian, Belarusian, and Russian accounts write the crash almost like weather: an exchange crashes, a fall occurs, a Depression follows. The Indian account gives it a timeline entry and a passing clause. The Belarusian account describes state regulation in the Western democracies without naming the New Deal. The Russian account names Hoover only to say that he did nothing.

The British, American, and Italian textbooks move from weather to policy. The British account calls the crash a symptom and gives the Republican Party part of the blame. The American account refuses a single cause, then names Smoot–Hawley and its damage to world trade. The Italian account says the United States defended its own production instead of accepting the responsibilities of an economic hegemon. These accounts name a party, a law, and a state. None follows coffee from the warehouse to the fire, or connects the later postwar trade settlement to the tariff it has just described.

The Sudanese and Chinese accounts start where the financial story lands. The Sudanese textbook does not begin with Wall Street. It begins with falling cotton prices and a government cutting jobs and salaries. Its sharpest line is: “But the government did not reduce the salaries of the British and the foreigners.” The Depression becomes a choice between identifiable groups.

The Chinese textbook makes the crisis a motive for Japanese expansion into China. It says that the worldwide economic crisis gravely affected Japan, and that Japan’s rulers, seeking to escape their predicament, hurried to launch a war of aggression against China. There is no unemployment figure, bank failure, tariff, or Roosevelt. In that classroom, the Depression is not primarily an economic story. It is part of the road to invasion.

None of these perspectives is useless. Each follows a different consequence: a government’s policy, a worker’s pay, or an invasion. But all eight leave the machinery thin. They name damage, blame, policy, or consequence; none explains the whole route from monetary contraction and gold, through tariffs and retaliation, to the price of cotton or coffee. Three mention coffee. None says what Brazil did with it.

The Sudanese account deserves particular credit for turning a global downturn into a choice between named groups rather than an injury to an abstract population. Still, this is a comparison of eight school accounts whose syllabuses reach 1929, not eight complete national histories. None is an economic history. The shared silence about the machinery beneath the slump is real, but it is also a limit of the genre.
