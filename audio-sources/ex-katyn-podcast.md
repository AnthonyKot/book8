# Katyn

### 1 The forest and the file

In April 1943, German soldiers dig into a pine forest near Katyn, twelve miles west of Smolensk. They uncover Polish officers in uniform, stacked in layers and shot through the back of the head. The forest will supply the name by which the killings are remembered. It contains only part of them.

The story begins with a border crossing. On 17 September 1939, the Red Army entered eastern Poland, after the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and sixteen days after Germany invaded from the west. Approximately 250,000 Polish soldiers and officers were captured or interned by Soviet forces. Most would go home. A selected group would pass from military defeat into a system of camps, prisons and orders.

Among the captives were officers, border guards, policemen and educated reservists. Some were held at Kozelsk, Starobelsk and Ostashkov; others were in prisons in Smolensk, Kharkiv, Kalinin, Kyiv and Kherson. These place names matter because they prevent one famous forest from swallowing the scale of what happened. Katyn was an execution site and a discovery site. It was not the whole operation.

Before the graves came a file. On 5 March 1940, People’s Commissar for Internal Affairs Lavrentiy Beria submitted proposal No. 794/B to Joseph Stalin and the Soviet Politburo. It proposed executing 25,700 Polish captives. Stalin, Voroshilov, Molotov and Mikoyan signed the order. The act that would later be denied entered the record first as an administrative decision with a number and four signatures.

The killings followed in April and May. The NKVD executed 21,857 Polish prisoners of war and detainees across five main locations. Around 4,500 were killed in the Katyn forest. The name on the map is therefore smaller than the event for which it stands.

That difference is easy to lose. A grave can be photographed; a network of camps, prisons and execution sites has to be reconstructed. The bodies near Smolensk made one part of the operation visible in 1943. The signed proposal and the counts make the larger system visible. Read together, the forest and the file answer different questions: where some of the dead were found, and how so many people came to be there.

The discovery also created a second separation, between the army that exposed the graves and the state whose police had filled them. German radio announced the find on 13 April 1943. The USSR denied responsibility and attributed the massacre to German troops; the Burdenko Commission repeated that account in 1944. The German role in discovering the bodies could now be made to blur the Soviet role in killing them. Two distinct acts occupied the same sentence, and the order of the nouns could decide which one a reader remembered.

The denial lasted beyond the war. On 13 April 1990, TASS published an official Soviet statement acknowledging NKVD responsibility under Mikhail Gorbachev. In 1992, under Boris Yeltsin, key documents from Package No. 1 were released to Poland. The story had travelled from an order kept in a state file, to bodies exposed by an enemy army, to an official denial, and finally back to the documents.

This is why Katyn is difficult to compress honestly. The sequence has several scales: approximately 250,000 captured or interned; 25,700 proposed for execution; 21,857 executed; around 4,500 at the forest that gave the event its familiar name. Each number answers a different question. Treat them as alternatives and the story looks inconsistent. Put them in order and the mechanism appears.

It also has several verbs. Soldiers are captured. A commissar proposes. Political leaders sign. Secret police execute. An army discovers. A government denies. An official statement acknowledges. Archives release. Remove the subjects and only the bodies remain, as if they arrived in the ground without the decisions that connected one stage to the next.

For a reader now, the ordinary object in this story is not a weapon but a place-name. “Katyn” can fit on a map label, a search box or a textbook index. Yet the word asks the reader to hold a forest and an operation together: one site that made the dead visible, and a chain of decisions spread across camps, prisons, offices and years. The little-known history is not hidden behind the name. It is folded inside it.
