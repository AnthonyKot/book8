# Spain, 1936–1939: The Bombers and the Bill

“Guernica had no anti-aircraft guns; there were no guns in the town at all, not even a machine gun was to be found.”

That is a priest listing what his town lacked. Guernica was a Basque town about thirty kilometres east of Bilbao. On the afternoon of Monday, 26 April 1937—a market day—aircraft came over the town centre in waves for more than three hours.

The aircraft had entered the war before they bombed the town. On 17 and 18 July 1936, part of the Spanish army rose against the government, beginning in Spanish Morocco. From late July, German and Italian transport planes carried the Army of Africa across the Strait of Gibraltar. The German Condor Legion followed in November. Aircraft first moved an army into the war; then they became part of the war.

Spain had also signed a Non-Intervention Agreement. France proposed it in August 1936, and a committee began meeting in London the following month. Germany, Italy, and the Soviet Union all adhered to the agreement. All three supplied the war. The question was not only who would send aircraft, tanks, troops, and advisers. It was how a country split in two would pay for supplies that officially were not supposed to arrive.

The Republic paid first. In 1936, the Bank of Spain held the world’s fourth-largest gold reserve. Spain had been neutral in the First World War and sold goods to both sides; now it held more than seven hundred tonnes of gold, much of it in coin, bars, and foreign holdings. Non-intervention meant that a vast reserve could buy little in Paris or London. The reserve was enormous, and the shops were shut.

In September, on the authority of a decree signed by finance minister Juan Negrín, about 510 tonnes—roughly three-quarters of the vault—went by truck to the naval base at Cartagena. Four Soviet ships carried it to Odessa in late October. Much of the remainder had already been sent or sold through France. In Moscow, metal became an account.

The Soviet side held the pen over the other column: the price of aircraft, freight, and advisers, and the exchange rates between pesetas, roubles, and dollars. The Republic knew what it had deposited. It could not see what it was being charged. By 1938 the account was spent and deliveries were slowing. Yet the gold bought real aircraft. The fighters and tanks it paid for helped hold Madrid through the winter of 1936. The gold did not lose the Republic the war. It made the Republic the customer of one supplier, at prices only that supplier could see.

Francisco Franco’s side paid later. Because the reserve remained with the government, it bought on credit. Berlin channelled the trade through two special companies, one in Spain and one in Germany. They billed aircraft, fuel, and the Condor Legion’s upkeep, then took repayment in materials German rearmament needed: iron ore from Vizcaya and pyrites from the Huelva mines. Rome extended the larger credit line and sent the larger army.

The debts outlasted the alliances. Spain was still paying instalments on its civil-war debt to Rome in the 1960s, a generation after the Italian state that extended the credit had vanished. One side paid cash into an account it could not audit. The other signed for goods against ore it had not yet dug. Spain’s savings and minerals financed both ends of Spain’s war.

The financial pattern is familiar even at household scale: payment made up front and difficult to follow, or a purchase made against future wages. The bombing is what remains in photographs. Behind the aircraft were two ordinary financial arrangements enlarged until a country’s gold, mines, and future payments were inside them.

Six textbooks in this comparison cover the war: Belarusian, Russian, Italian, American, British, and German accounts. All describe foreign intervention as aid, help, or support. The word is not false. It is remarkably roomy. It can hold a transport plane, an air raid, a Soviet account, and a German mining arrangement without requiring a student to see any of them.

The Italian textbook says that Nazi Germany sent aircraft and pilots and “made use of the war to test the efficiency of its air force.” It identifies Spain as a testing ground, but does not name Guernica or the Condor Legion. The Belarusian account names neither aircraft nor target, unit, date, or sender. The Russian account includes air strikes but does not land them on a named target; Guernica appears elsewhere only as an artwork. The American account names Madrid and Guernica but not the unit, date, or person who authorised the raid.

The British and German accounts provide all five pieces: aircraft, target, unit, date, and sender. Yet they give Guernica death tolls an order of magnitude apart. The British account gives 1,600; the German account says several hundred. The toll is disputed, but neither tells pupils that. The German account goes furthest by placing the commander of its own air force beside the priest who watched the bombing from below.

This is not a ladder from dishonest to honest. The Italian account names the experiment and loses the town. The American account names the town and loses the people who sent the aircraft. At one end, balanced verbs make German and Italian aid to the Nationalists resemble Soviet support for the Republic, with no cargo attached. At the other, named units and active senders make the act visible.

Then comes the larger gap. None of the six accounts says what the aid cost, who paid for it, or what payment looked like. None of the eleven textbooks in the wider comparison mentions the Bank of Spain’s reserve. The classroom story follows the bombers and leaves the bill outside.

The German account deserves credit for putting an air-force commander’s explanation next to the priest’s account from the ground, and for calling Spain a military dress rehearsal without pretending that Guernica caused the next war. The comparison still has limits: only six accounts cover the episode, none is a history of Spain from inside its institutions or villages, and their estimates of Italian troops differ sharply.
