# What Hiroshima Is For

### 1 A city in the plural

In February 1989, in the village of Karaul on the Kazakh steppe, a public meeting adopted an appeal about the nuclear test site next door — the meeting that began the Nevada–Semipalatinsk movement, which closed the site two years later.  Kazakhstan's eleventh-grade history textbook reproduces the appeal. One of its sentences is this:

“Over 40 years, thousands of Hiroshimas have exploded here.”

Nothing in that sentence is about Japan. Roughly four and a half thousand kilometres of Asia lie between that village and Hiroshima, and forty-four years lie between the meeting and the morning the word acquired its meaning.  Nobody on the page stops to explain the unit, because by 1989 nobody needed it explained. A city had become a quantity, and a plural one.

The word first acquired that meaning on 6 August 1945. Japanese cities were already burning before August; the difference was not that civilians had suddenly entered the war. A uranium bomb called Little Boy fell on Hiroshima from the B-29 *Enola Gay*, releasing the energy of roughly fifteen thousand tons of TNT. On 8 August the Soviet Union declared war on Japan. On 9 August Soviet forces entered Manchuria and a plutonium bomb called Fat Man fell on Nagasaki. The emperor broadcast Japan's surrender on 15 August; the document was signed aboard the USS *Missouri* on 2 September.

The bomb gave Hiroshima a second life as a ruler. Seven years later the first thermonuclear test in the Pacific released something like seven hundred times as much energy; nine years after that, the largest device ever detonated released about fifty megatons, the equivalent of more than three thousand Little Boys.  As weapons grew, their technical units moved further from ordinary experience.

Kilotons and megatons are honest units, but no one can picture a kiloton. A city is different. It has streets, schools and a name. That made Hiroshima useful whenever a speaker needed to turn a quantity of explosive energy into an imaginable loss. By the time the people of Karaul wrote their appeal, the name could cross thousands of kilometres and stand in the plural without explanation.

The same unit still turns up when arsenals are compared in a newsfeed — a new warhead, a stockpile total, a treaty lapsing — as the equivalent of so many Hiroshimas. It travels across languages without translation and usually without a footnote. As popular measures go, it is unusually durable and unusually strange: the name of a place where people were killed, doing arithmetic.

The human count is different. Figures commonly cited today run to roughly 90,000–140,000 at Hiroshima and 60,000–80,000 at Nagasaki by the end of 1945, with further deaths for decades afterwards. Those are ranges, not conversions. A death at the moment of the blast, by the end of 1945 and years later from radiation belongs to a different total. The interval chosen changes the answer.

That makes the familiar comparison less precise than it sounds. One Hiroshima may mean explosive yield, people killed immediately, people dead by the end of the year, or a city's entire destruction. The multiplier can be announced to three significant figures while the thing being multiplied varies by nearly half. The name supplies an image exactly where the number refuses to settle.

The city also became part of a chain of announcements. One interpretation made the bomb a demonstration of American power to the Soviet Union. A Soviet test followed in 1949, then four decades of arsenals priced in cities.  The nine days in August do not move. What moves is the arrow drawn through them: bomb to surrender, Soviet entry to surrender, bomb to Cold War.

The sentence from Karaul completes the transformation. Hiroshima begins as a place, becomes an event, then becomes the unit by which another landscape describes what happened to it. The unit works because everyone recognises the city. It remains unstable because recognition is not the same as agreement about what, exactly, is being counted.
