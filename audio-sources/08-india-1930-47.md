# India, 1930–1947: Salt, Rice, and Independence

On 6 April 1930, at Dandi on India’s Gujarat coast, Mohandas Gandhi walked into the shallows and lifted a lump of natural salt from the mud. He had walked for twenty-four days with seventy-eight companions. By the end, crowds filled the road behind them.

Picking up salt was illegal. In British India, only the colonial state could produce and sell it. Gandhi had chosen an everyday necessity: an object small enough to hold up, but essential to everyone. His act made a system of control visible.

Thirteen years later, the same empire faced a different crisis in Bengal. There was no salt monopoly to point to. Rice could still be found, sometimes only a district away. But people arriving from villages could no longer afford it.

Amartya Sen was nine years old, at school in Santiniketan near Calcutta, when he watched villagers come to the city to die in 1943. Later, he built a way of explaining famines around what he saw. The crucial question, he argued, is not only whether food exists. It is whether people have an *entitlement* to it: whether a day’s work, a catch of fish, or a haircut can still be exchanged for food.

In Bengal, several pressures came together. Japan captured Rangoon and then Burma in 1942, cutting off an important source of rice for eastern India. Fearing a Japanese advance, the colonial administration removed rice stocks above a household allowance from coastal areas and confiscated or destroyed tens of thousands of boats. Those boats were how fishermen made a living and how rice moved between districts. War needs were given priority: Calcutta’s industries, the army, and forces beyond India were provisioned before many Bengal households. Refugees arrived from Burma, and a cyclone struck the delta.

No single factor explains the deaths. But the result was devastating. Between two and three million people died, most of them in 1943. This was neither a simple harvest failure nor a planned massacre. In a rising market, people with cash could still lose the practical claim that let them buy food. The rice could be present, and the eater absent from it.

That distinction survives in ordinary life. Rationing reached Calcutta in 1943 and remained after independence. India’s Public Distribution System still connects ration cards and fair-price shops to government buffer stocks and fixed prices. Elsewhere the system is less visible, sitting behind the price printed on a bag of rice. When India restricted most rice exports in July 2023, world prices rose sharply. The problem Gandhi made visible with salt—the control of a necessity—could also appear in a market where the food was physically there.

This is the background against which school textbooks tell the story of independence. Seven of the books in this comparison reach India. Four make Britain the grammatical giver: the American account says Britain “granted” independence; the Russian account says Britain “took the decision to grant” it; the Ukrainian account has the colonial administration announce the granting; the British account makes Bevin and Attlee decide to give it. The Belarusian account says India and Pakistan “attained” independence, while an Indian timeline says they “become independent.” The Italian account reverses the movement: Congress wrests the promise from English hands.

The British textbook contains the argument inside its own account. It presents a response from Indian historians: “Independence was not a gift from the British, it was ‘the hard-won fruit of struggle and sacrifice’.” Yet that same textbook does not cover Amritsar, the Rowlatt Act, the Salt March, or Quit India. It names the struggle while leaving out much of the road that would let a pupil follow it.

The other accounts leave different shapes. The Russian textbook describes Amritsar directly and follows methods of civil resistance, then returns the final decision to London. The American textbook explains the salt monopoly, the march, the arrests, the wartime famine, and the 1946 naval mutiny, yet still makes Britain the giver. The Italian textbook gives Amritsar its own heading and makes Congress the active party. The Ukrainian account gives an estimate of about one hundred thousand deaths in the population exchange during partition; the Belarusian account gives neither the place of the massacre nor a partition toll. Only the American and British accounts mention the Bengal famine, and neither places it within the independence story.

The Indian world-history textbook makes its boundary explicit. Its introduction says that South Asian history belongs to the following year. Its brief timeline leaves out Amritsar, the Salt March, Jinnah, Mountbatten, partition, and the Bengal famine. But it also offers an unusually clear instruction for listening to all of these accounts: “So we need to read each timeline critically, see what it tells us and what it does not. Timelines frame history in particular ways.”

That does not make every omission a deliberate refusal. Four books in the wider comparison are outside Indian history altogether. Only two of the seven in-scope accounts mention the Bengal famine, so its absence is better understood as a shared curriculum limit than as evidence of one country’s special silence. No short world-history textbook can settle the disputed death toll of partition.

But the verbs still matter. One set of accounts tells a story of Britain giving independence. Another lets India take it. And around those verbs lie the roads, rifles, borders, salt, and rice that determine what independence had to overcome.
