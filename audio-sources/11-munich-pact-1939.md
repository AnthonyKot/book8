# Munich, the Pact, and September 1939: A Paper That Became a Warning

On 30 September 1938, at Heston aerodrome west of London, Neville Chamberlain stepped down from an aeroplane and held a sheet of paper above his head for the cameras. It was not the Munich Agreement he had signed the night before. It was a separate one-page declaration that he had asked Adolf Hitler to sign that morning. That evening, from a window at Downing Street, Chamberlain read the line that outlived him: “peace for our time.”

The larger agreement had been made by Chamberlain, Édouard Daladier, Hitler, and Benito Mussolini. It transferred the Sudetenland to Germany. Czechoslovakia was not at the table, and the Soviet Union was not invited. The transfer took place between 1 and 10 October.

That paper carried a decision out of a room, but it did not settle what the decision meant. It could be read as peace purchased under pressure, as a betrayal of a country the signatories were meant to defend, or as the point at which Moscow learned that collective security would not hold.

Events changed the price. German troops occupied Bohemia and Moravia on 15 March 1939, and Slovakia became a separate state under German protection. Britain publicly guaranteed Poland on 31 March, followed by France. British, French, and Soviet representatives talked in Moscow through August without reaching an agreement. On 23 August, Joachim von Ribbentrop and Vyacheslav Molotov signed a non-aggression treaty with a secret additional protocol dividing eastern Europe into spheres of interest.

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September. Britain and France declared war on Germany two days later, but not on the Soviet Union. On 17 September, the Red Army crossed into eastern Poland. A German–Soviet treaty of 28 September fixed a line between the two powers. Organised Polish resistance ended in early October. The chain was complete: Munich, failed talks, the Pact, and two armies entering Poland.

Munich did not remain one link in that chain. It became a word that could be taken into later crises. In June 1950, after the first reports from Korea, President Harry Truman recalled the 1930s: Manchuria, Ethiopia, Austria, and what followed when each was allowed to pass. “No more Munichs” appeared during the Suez crisis, in arguments over Vietnam, and repeatedly since.

The analogy turns a comparison into an instruction. If a present aggressor is another Hitler, delay becomes appeasement. If the resemblance is wrong, the word can push a government toward a war that the earlier event does not explain. An analogy travels lighter than the event from which it comes. The Munich in a speech need not carry Czechoslovakia’s empty chair, Moscow’s exclusion, lost fortifications, or the secret agreement signed eleven months later. It needs a threatening state, a concession, and a known ending. Once those pieces are matched, the rest of the past can seem to have matched too.

This is why *appeasement* still enters arguments about present wars. The history does not decide whether Munich fits a new crisis. It explains why the word can make negotiation look like surrender before the negotiation has begun. The agreement failed to secure the peace Chamberlain announced. Its name survived as advice about how peace should be defended.

Six textbooks in this comparison teach the sequence, but they do not give pupils the same chain of causes. The Belarusian and Russian accounts point backward from the Pact to Munich: exclusion, failed collective security, and Western appeasement make Stalin’s agreement with Germany a consequence. The German and American accounts point forward, from the secret protocol into Poland. The British and Italian accounts keep both sides in view: exclusion before the Pact, secret clauses after it. All six include 23 August. None treats it as an accident.

The Russian textbook says: “The Munich collusion became a synonym for betrayal and disgrace, the crown of unprincipled concessions on the part of England and France.” The phrase makes a particular arrow bright: Munich leads to the Pact. Other accounts light different arrows. The British account alone calculates Munich in territory, population, industry, and fortifications, then gives four rival answers to the question of blame. The German account alone prints an abandoned plan by German officers to overthrow Hitler if war came over the Sudetenland. The Italian account notes that the supposedly Italian proposal followed German demands almost word for word. The Belarusian account alone reproduces the secret spheres of interest with Lithuania on the German side.

The sharpest difference comes on 17 September. The Belarusian account describes a liberation campaign that reunites peoples. The Russian account names troops crossing a border and an order, but not the person who gave it. The British account puts the invasion in a subordinate clause. The Italian account makes the Russians the subject of a seizure. The American account says Poland was crushed from two sides. The German account names the Soviet leadership, the order, and annexation. The grammar becomes more active as the narrator moves away from Moscow.

The American textbook explicitly names the “Munich Analogy” and carries it into the present. The British account shows the argument at work later over Korea and Suez without giving it that name. The other four accounts stop the word in 1939. The textbooks therefore do more than disagree about what Munich caused. Some offer a verdict on the past; two offer a tool that can shape the next decision.

The British account deserves credit for giving pupils four argued answers instead of one verdict. The Belarusian, Russian, and German accounts each preserve an inconvenient detail that their wider framing might have omitted. This is still a small set of school accounts, not a military history of September 1939 or a legal history of the secret protocol. Differences between the printed accounts are part of the evidence; they should not be silently repaired into one classroom story.
