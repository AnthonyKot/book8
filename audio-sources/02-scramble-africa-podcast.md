# Scramble for Africa

### 1 The tree that opened a continent

In the summer of 1841, three iron steamers went up the Niger with about a hundred and fifty Europeans aboard. They were there to sign treaties against the slave trade and plant a model farm. Within two months more than forty of them were dead of fever, and the ships had turned back for the sea.

European traders had worked that coast since the 1480s. They had wanted markets and raw materials for centuries, yet they had almost never gone inland. The expedition showed why. A river that looked like a road into the continent was also a place where a European crew could be ruined before its plans had properly begun. The desire to enter Africa was old. The ability to survive the journey was not.

Thirteen years later, William Balfour Baikie took a steamer up the same Niger and into the Benue. His expedition remained on the rivers for four months. Every European aboard received a daily dose before falling ill, and every one of them came home. One river, two expeditions, and the difference between them was quinine.

The drug came from the bark of the cinchona tree. Europeans had used it to treat malaria for years; in the 1840s the British found that taking it before exposure could prevent the disease. But cinchona grew only in the Andes, and the supply did not meet European demand. A medicine can work perfectly and still change little if there is not enough of it.

So cinchona seed left the Andes. Attempts to establish the tree elsewhere often failed, but plantations took hold in Dutch Java and British India. By the 1870s, quinine was cheap enough to issue to a garrison rather than reserve for a governor. This is the same move Britain made with tea: a plant under somebody else’s sovereignty becomes a supply problem, and an empire answers by moving the plant.

Quinine did not cause the Scramble for Africa. It did not create the appetite for territory, markets or raw materials. It altered the cost and practical reach of acting on that appetite. So did the shallow-draught steamer, able to enter rivers a sailing ship could not. Then the Berlin Conference added a rule: a claim had to be occupied to count. Survival had become more manageable just as possession on paper began to demand a presence on the ground.

The chain has four links. Plantation production made preventive dosing available in quantity. Preventive dosing lowered the danger of river travel for European expeditions. Steamers widened the routes they could use. The Berlin rule then rewarded the powers that converted travel into occupation. No one link explains the partition. Together they help explain why an old ambition suddenly acquired a new timetable.

Nor did those links make conquest automatic. While the delegates met in Berlin, Mahdist forces took Khartoum, and a Mahdist state ruled from Omdurman until 1898. In 1896, Menelik II’s army defeated an Italian force at Adwa, and Ethiopia’s independence was confirmed that October. The new tools changed what European states could attempt and sustain. They did not write every outcome in advance.

That is why the calendar matters. In 1841, an expedition with iron hulls and official backing could be driven out by fever within weeks. In 1854, preventive dosing brought another expedition home. By the 1870s, plantation production had widened the supply. In 1884, delegates of fourteen states could sit in Berlin beneath a wall map and divide the interior among themselves, with no African state in the room. The ambition had been waiting on the coast. What changed was the mortality, the transport and the rule for turning a claim into control.

A trace of that machinery is still on a kitchen shelf. Quinine is what makes tonic water bitter. The tonic was drunk because a daily dose had to go down; gin and sugar made it easier to swallow. Under a black light, a glass of tonic water glows faint blue. That is the quinine. The label may still say *Indian*, naming the colony where the habit formed. A drink now mixed for pleasure carries the residue of a medical constraint that once helped Europeans move inland.
