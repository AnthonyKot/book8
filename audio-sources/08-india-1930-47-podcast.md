# India, 1930–1947: Salt, Rice, and Independence

On 6 April 1930, at Dandi on India’s Gujarat coast, Mohandas Gandhi walked into the shallows and lifted a lump of natural salt from the mud. He had walked for twenty-four days with seventy-eight companions. By the end, crowds filled the road behind them.

Picking up salt was illegal. In British India, only the colonial state could produce and sell it. Gandhi had chosen an everyday necessity: an object small enough to hold up, but essential to everyone. His act made a system of control visible.

Thirteen years later, the same empire faced a different crisis in Bengal. There was no salt monopoly to point to. Rice could still be found, sometimes only a district away. But people arriving from villages could no longer afford it.

Amartya Sen was nine years old, at school in Santiniketan near Calcutta, when he watched villagers come to the city to die in 1943. Later, he built a way of explaining famines around what he saw. The crucial question, he argued, is not only whether food exists. It is whether people have an *entitlement* to it: whether a day’s work, a catch of fish, or a haircut can still be exchanged for food.

In Bengal, several pressures came together. Japan captured Rangoon and then Burma in 1942, cutting off an important source of rice for eastern India. Fearing a Japanese advance, the colonial administration removed rice stocks above a household allowance from coastal areas and confiscated or destroyed tens of thousands of boats. Those boats were how fishermen made a living and how rice moved between districts. War needs were given priority: Calcutta’s industries, the army, and forces beyond India were provisioned before many Bengal households. Refugees arrived from Burma, and a cyclone struck the delta.

No single factor explains the deaths. But the result was devastating. Between two and three million people died, most of them in 1943. This was neither a simple harvest failure nor a planned massacre. In a rising market, people with cash could still lose the practical claim that let them buy food. The rice could be present, and the eater absent from it.

That distinction survives in ordinary life. Rationing reached Calcutta in 1943 and remained after independence. India’s Public Distribution System still connects ration cards and fair-price shops to government buffer stocks and fixed prices. Elsewhere the system is less visible, sitting behind the price printed on a bag of rice. When India restricted most rice exports in July 2023, world prices rose sharply. The problem Gandhi made visible with salt—the control of a necessity—could also appear in a market where the food was physically there.
