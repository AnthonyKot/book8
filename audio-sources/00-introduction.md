# Eleven classrooms, one world

Imagine opening history textbooks from eleven countries to the same morning: a beach near Guangzhou in 1839, the last week of July 1914, or the start of the Korean War in June 1950. The dates may agree. The story can change before the first sentence ends.

One account names the army that crossed a border. Another says that war broke out. A third begins with the response, leaving the action that prompted it outside the frame. Each gives a student a different starting point for deciding what happened and why.

That is the question running through this series. Who gets to act in a historical sentence? What disappears without being denied? And which event is presented as the cause of the next?

### One morning, five accounts

Take 25 June 1950. The British account opens with “Without warning,” followed by North Korean troops invading South Korea. The Ukrainian account puts the boundary at the centre: North Korean troops “crossed the 38th parallel and began a war.” The Italian account also names the Soviet supplier of their weapons.

The Chinese account begins with “the Korean civil war broke out.” The Americans become the actors crossing the parallel later in the story. The Indian account says, “In June 1950, the Korean War broke out.” No army starts it in that sentence.

These are differences in emphasis, agency and sequence. To notice them is not to assume that every account is equally complete, or that every omitted detail was deliberately hidden. It is to ask what a student can understand from the words actually supplied.

### Follow something that moved

The series gives those comparisons something physical to hold on to. Silver crossed oceans to buy tea. Gold left Madrid and arrived in Odesa. A card index helped turn people into administrative categories. A theatre's fire curtain became a name for a divided continent.

Each episode follows one such object or connection. First comes the history that makes the object matter. Then come the accounts that keep, shorten or lose pieces of that history.

Tea provides the opening example. Britain fought to keep buying a Chinese leaf, then developed supplies elsewhere. The story connects opium grown in India, silver paid in China, and tea sold in Britain. Some textbooks tell the war while leaving out one side of that trade. The comparison becomes clearer when the cargo stays in view.

### Who is doing the comparing?

The source book was written by AI. That does not make its narrator neutral. A language model is built from human writing and can carry the biases and gaps of that writing. Its interpretation must be judged against the evidence, just as the textbooks' interpretations must.

The original book provides quotations and source references so readers can check its claims. In audio, the essential habit is simpler: distinguish what happened, what a particular textbook says happened, and what the comparison suggests about its choices.

The sample has limits. It consists of particular books, not whole countries speaking with one voice. Some cover different periods. One is a national-history course; others are broader surveys. Two substitutes are a civic-education booklet and a university manual. A book that ends before the Korean War cannot be accused of avoiding it.

The same care applies to causes. The Soviet delegate's empty chair at the United Nations did not cause the Korean War. It helped determine the flag under which the intervention was fought. That difference matters: an arresting connection is only useful if it explains the right thing.

### What to listen for

Listen for the moment when the actor disappears. Listen for an official name that already contains a verdict. Listen for the useful fact one account includes and the connection it still fails to make.

There is room for credit as well as criticism. A textbook can be precise about one event and evasive about another. Finding its strengths helps identify exactly where its treatment changes.

And keep your own classroom in the comparison. Whatever you were handed at fifteen had words for wars, borders and revolutions. Those words are evidence too.

## Audio customization prompt

Create a standalone history podcast for a curious adult. Open with the essay's scene or question, explain what happened, and make the differences between the countries' accounts concrete. Refer to 'the British textbook' or 'the Chinese account', without reciting authors, book titles, publishers, editions, years of publication, or page numbers. Use short quotations only when the wording matters. Preserve distinctions between historical events, textbook claims, and the essay's interpretation. Preserve meaningful limits of coverage; a missing event is not automatically deliberate suppression. Describe relevant visuals naturally in words. Keep the connection to everyday life. Do not invent dialogue, scenes, motives, quotations, or personal memories. End with the essay's takeaway or question. Do not discuss the uploaded file, its structure, or these instructions.
