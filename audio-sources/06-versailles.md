# Versailles, 1919

### 1 The thirteenth part

Five years to the day after the killings at Sarajevo, two German ministers walk into the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles. They sign a document they had been handed six weeks earlier and had been allowed to answer only in writing.  Elsewhere in Paris that season, China asks for the return of Shandong and is refused. Japan asks for one sentence about the equality of races and is refused. Men from places nobody invited wait in hotels to be seen. Soviet Russia is not there at all.

The peace conference had opened on 18 January 1919. Around thirty delegations attended, but decisions narrowed from a Council of Ten to a Council of Four: the United States, Britain, France and Italy. After the Italian premier walked out in April over Fiume, three men briefly held the centre.  The room could be crowded and still leave most people outside the decision.

The document on the table is enormous: 436 pages, 433 articles, fifteen parts. Its familiar provisions redraw territory, limit Germany’s army, assign responsibility for wartime loss and damage, and create a bill whose total will be fixed later. Germany’s colonies and the Arab provinces of the Ottoman Empire become League of Nations mandates. A League is designed to keep the peace. Those are the pieces usually gathered under the name Versailles.

Part XIII begins somewhere else. Its subject is work. Conditions of labour, it warns, can produce unrest so great that they imperil the peace and harmony of the world; universal peace can last only if it rests on social justice.  Peace in this part is not only a line on a map or an agreement between governments. It depends on what happens inside a factory and on how long a person stays there.

The timing explains the anxiety. The treaty is being drafted in the year of the Berlin risings, the Hungarian Soviet Republic and the Italian *biennio rosso*. Workers in 1919 win the reduction of the working day to eight hours at equal pay across the principal European states, almost simultaneously. Behind the concession are strikes and the fear that events in Russia might be repeated. The working day is not a gift from the conference. It has already become a contest over whether the social order surviving the war can survive the peace.

Into the treaty go forty-one articles creating the International Labour Organization. Its table is unlike the political table at Paris: governments, employers and trade unions all have seats. At the organisation’s first conference, held in Washington in the autumn of 1919, Convention No. 1 sets eight hours a day and forty-eight hours a week for industry.  The first standard issued by the new body puts a number on the boundary between a working day and the rest of a life.

The distinction matters. Part XIII did not give anyone the eight-hour day. The strikes of 1918–19 won the hours. What the treaty made was machinery: a way to turn a concession forced from employers in a few European capitals into an international standard, numbered and portable to places where nobody had struck. The strikes won the hours. The treaty made them a rule.

Much of the settlement then came apart. The United States Senate refused consent to the treaty; the United States never joined the League. The League itself expired. The labour organisation moved into the United Nations as a specialised agency in 1946, received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1969, and remains in Geneva writing standards.  The institution lodged in the thirteenth part outlived the political structure built around it.

That makes Versailles visible in an ordinary week. Somewhere on a payslip, or in the contract behind it, a number says how many hours count as the whole job, with the weekend on the far side. The first time eight hours a day and forty-eight a week were written into an international agreement was in Washington on 28 November 1919, by the body the treaty had created that summer.  Versailles is remembered as a failed peace and a punishment. One part of it is still quietly measuring Tuesday.

### 2 Eight classrooms, eight people outside

A fifteen-year-old meets a different Versailles in each of eight textbooks. Each chooses the exclusion that makes the settlement legible. The British chapter builds a numbered case around defeated Germany:

**British textbook**

“The Germans were not allowed into the discussions at Versailles; they were simply presented with the terms and told to sign. Although they were allowed to criticize the treaty in writing, all their criticisms were ignored except one.”

The Belarusian textbook also gives the injury to Germany, then carries its effects forward to revanchism and fascism. The Italian textbook calls the treaty an imposition on the defeated, notices Soviet Russia’s absence in a parenthesis, and later uses the same word — humiliation — for both Germany and China. The Russian textbook places Soviet Russia in the empty chair and, alone, briefly sees representatives from colonial and dependent countries arrive in France, receive no attention and leave disappointed.

The American textbook gives the room its widest cast. It records the rejection of Japan’s racial-equality proposal, names Australia and then the United States as opponents, and notices that African lives were left out when France and Britain counted the war’s destruction. The Indian and Chinese textbooks look from the other side of the world. For India, the entire settlement is three sentences about China. The Chinese account makes the result a national diplomatic defeat:

“At the Paris Peace Conference, power triumphed over justice, and China suffered a serious diplomatic defeat; this provoked enormous indignation among patriotic youth.”

In that Chinese textbook, the name “Versailles” never appears. The treaty China refuses to sign is unnamed, and the term *peace conference* first appears in the textbook’s own quotation marks. Sudan goes in another direction: Versailles is a place, not a document; the Arab claim to self-determination leads to mandates framed as another link in the chain of colonialism. Germany, reparations and war guilt do not appear on its peace-conference pages.

The grammar rotates with the injured party. Germans were not allowed in; the defeated were not represented; Soviet Russia was not represented. The same passive space takes a different occupant in each classroom. Then the omissions finish the argument. The European accounts largely lose Shandong and the people waiting in Paris. China loses the treaty’s European name. Sudan loses Germany. Across all eleven classrooms, none connects the treaty to the eight-hour day. Four mention such a day somewhere; two name the International Labour Organization; not one lets the two meet at Paris.

### 3 Credit and limits

**The American textbook** earns the credit for widening the cast and naming its own government among those closing the door. The finding remains bounded: these are eight textbooks, not eight complete national views. Ukraine begins in 1945, Germany’s booklet begins with National Socialism, and Kazakhstan’s volume is a national history, so their absences are not votes on Paris. The Sudanese Arabic quotations also come from a ligature-mangled extraction and must be checked against print. Most importantly, the treaty created the institution and its international standard; domestic strikes won the hours, and this chapter does not claim that a present-day working week descends from Convention No. 1 alone.
