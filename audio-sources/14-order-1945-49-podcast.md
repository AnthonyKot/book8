# The Cold War’s First Sentence

### 1 The borrowed object

On the evening of 8 December 1881, shortly before the curtain rose on Offenbach’s *Tales of Hoffmann*, the Ringtheater in Vienna caught fire. Several hundred people in the auditorium did not get out. Within a few years, Austrian and German building codes required theatres to hang a sheet of iron on a counterweight between the stage and the house. Its ordinary name was *eiserner Vorhang*, or iron curtain. Drury Lane had advertised one since 1794. The Cold War’s great political image began as fire-safety equipment.

An iron curtain had one clear job. A theatre contained two rooms that depended on each other: a stage full of scenery, ropes and lights, and an auditorium full of people. If fire took one, the sheet came down to stop it reaching the other. The object did not explain how the fire began. It drew a hard line through a single building.

On 5 March 1946, out of office, with the President of the United States sitting on the platform behind him, Winston Churchill spoke at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri. He said that an iron curtain had descended across Europe.  The borrowed object did exactly what it had done in a theatre: it turned one space into two, put danger on the other side and made separation sound protective.

Two years later, Berlin supplied the physical version. On 24 June 1948, the road, rail and canal links into its western sectors closed, and the power station in the Soviet sector stopped feeding the western grid. Rather more than two million people were inside the line.  Two days later, the first transport aircraft landed at Tempelhof. They kept landing for eleven months.  The line across Europe was no longer only something said at a lectern. It controlled food, fuel and the routes by which both could arrive.

The rescue acquired another mechanical metaphor. In English, it was an *airlift*: the cargo goes up. In Ukrainian it is called an air bridge, in Italian a *ponte aereo*, and in German a *Luftbrücke*. The cargo goes across. A bridge implies two banks and something between them that has to be spanned. Curtain and bridge became a pair: one image drops a barrier; the other crosses it.

Churchill had not invented his phrase. In 1920, Ethel Snowden, a British Labour delegate visiting Soviet Russia, wrote of crossing into it: *“We were behind the ‘iron curtain’ at last!”*  On 25 February 1945, Joseph Goebbels wrote in *Das Reich* that an iron curtain would fall over territory taken by the Soviet Union if Germany surrendered.  On 12 May, four days after the German surrender, Churchill cabled Truman that “an iron curtain is drawn down upon their front. We do not know what is going on behind.”  The image had travelled from theatre architecture to travel writing, then into Nazi warning and private diplomacy, before Fulton made it the name of an age.

That journey matters because the phrase appears to explain the history. It does not. No curtain divided Europe. Armies divided it, and treaties wrote the division down. No bridge ended the division either; aircraft kept one part of one city supplied across it. The metaphors made those arrangements sayable in one breath. They also quietly assigned roles: someone lowers a curtain, someone builds a bridge, somebody is kept out and somebody is carried through.

The era’s other name travelled too. George Orwell used *cold war* in October 1945; Bernard Baruch used it officially in April 1947; Walter Lippmann gave the words further life that year. The name described a conflict that was neither peace nor the war just ended. Like the curtain, it did not settle who made the first move. It made the dispute compact enough to fit in a heading.

The first object is still working. In traditional theatres across Europe the iron curtain is lowered and checked as a matter of licence. At the Vienna State Opera — along the same ring road from where the Ringtheater stood — the panel has carried a different commissioned artwork every season since the late 1990s, raised before the music starts.  Anyone who has watched that great slab go up has watched the Cold War’s name lift out of the way.
