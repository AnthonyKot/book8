# Meet the narrators

A history book can be precise in nine places and evasive in the tenth. Knowing what it does well makes the change easier to hear.

This series compares eleven particular accounts. They are not eleven countries speaking unanimously. They have different audiences, different periods to cover, and different rules for approval. The German source is a civic-education booklet. The Italian source is a university manual. The Kazakh source teaches national history. Those differences matter whenever an event goes missing.

Here are the voices, and the habits to listen for.

### Belarus: the result without the actor

The Belarusian survey has a long reach, including the nineteenth-century events that some other books barely touch. Its weakness is often the sentence that delivers an outcome without naming who produced it.

Wars are won, territories seized, countries opened. The result arrives, but the person or institution responsible can disappear.

Its account of the Soviet entry into eastern Poland describes protection and reunification. The comparison asks what those words keep in view, and what happens to the decisions and victims outside them. The book's broad coverage makes its omissions especially worth examining, but breadth alone does not explain why a particular event is absent.

### Russia: clarity about someone else's empire

The Russian world-history volume is often explicit about colonial violence abroad. It names the English authorities in the shooting of a peaceful rally at Amritsar. It follows colonial delegations to Paris and notices that they were not received.

Its treatment of the Polish prisoners killed in 1940 is much less direct. The shooting enters through a subordinate clause describing the prisoners' supposed political disposition.

Listen for the pivot: a paragraph distributes responsibility widely, then a word such as “however” changes where its weight falls. Remember the course boundary too. This particular volume stops in 1945; later events belong to another book.

### Kazakhstan: history arrives with displaced people

The Kazakh source follows the country's own history. It brings Soviet policy down to the level of people arriving in the republic: deported Koreans, Poles and Germans; communities settling in new places; cultural institutions formed after displacement.

That is why it remains useful to this comparison even though it is not a world-history course. It shows policy as lived in a republic rather than announced from Moscow.

The absence of the Korean War tells us which course this is. It does not, on its own, tell us what its authors wanted to conceal.

### Ukraine: name both ambitions, then name the crossing

The Ukrainian volume begins in the postwar world. The Korean War receives sustained attention because it belongs near the centre of the course rather than at its far edge.

Its account gives both Korean governments the ambition to unite the peninsula before naming which army crossed the boundary. It says: “In June 1950 North Korean troops crossed the 38th parallel and began a war.”

That sequence does two jobs. It describes a shared political goal, then assigns a particular action. Earlier events sit outside this volume's main scope, which limits comparisons with books that devote most of their space to them.

### China: quantities, then a name that carries a judgement

The Chinese-history volume is unusually concrete about the opium trade. It counts chests, tracks silver, and names the body that approved war. Other accounts can compress the same events into a trade dispute with very little trade in it.

It also gives events names that already express a judgement. Of the Treaty of Nanking, it says: “This treaty was the first unequal treaty in modern Chinese history that humiliated the nation and forfeited its sovereignty.”

The outside world usually enters this volume where it enters China. European events missing from this course may belong to its companion volume. That boundary must remain part of the comparison.

### India: keep the three sides of the trade together

The Indian account can connect the drug, the force and the cargo in a single explanation. Opium grows in India, is sold in China, and supplies silver used to buy goods for Britain. The triangular trade stays a triangle.

The course also tells students to examine its own organizing tools: “So we need to read each timeline critically, see what it tells us and what it does not. Timelines frame history in particular ways.”

Its treatment of South Asia is unexpectedly sparse. That is a declared course boundary: South Asia belongs to another year's study. An empty space here can therefore be a scheduling decision, not a claim that nothing happened.

### Sudan: resistance told from inside

The Sudanese account gives sustained attention to the Mahdist revolution and state. People, letters and political decisions replace the brief imperial episode that appears in some outside accounts. Khartoum is liberated rather than captured.

Its strength is to make the language of conquest itself a question. It asks students which term should apply to the Turkish–Egyptian military movement into Sudan: invasion or conquest.

The course is regional and ends at independence. Its silence about much of the wider world follows that scope. What it supplies is the experience of a place that other accounts can reduce to a territory someone else acquired.

### Germany: name the institutions responsible

The German civic-education booklet concentrates on the war, the occupation of eastern Europe, and the murder of the European Jews. It names ordinary military participation rather than confining responsibility to special units.

“German soldiers took part in the killings of the civilian population, secured the shooting sites, burned whole villages down…”

It also names Soviet responsibility for the shooting of Polish officers. Its strength is specific attribution, including crimes committed by its own country.

But a federal agency's booklet is not the same as an assigned school textbook. It cannot establish what every German teenager is taught, and events outside its wartime scope cannot fairly be counted as omissions.

### Italy: stop at the official name

The Italian university manual repeatedly pauses over labels. An official title can be a euphemism; an apparently inevitable outcome can be traced back to decisions.

It calls the Free State of the Congo “a paradoxical euphemism” while describing the exploitation beneath the name.

This is a commercially published university account, with more room for Europe than for other regions. Its coverage also ends earlier than the newest books in the comparison. Listen for its ability to say that an outcome could have been different, then name the choice that helped produce it.

### Britain: make the disagreement part of the lesson

The British account often presents competing explanations before taking a position. Its discussion of 1914 makes the historians' disagreement part of what a student must learn.

It can also print uncomfortable evidence about Britain's own empire, including high death tolls in India, alongside official defences.

The nineteenth century is less central to its course. The Opium Wars appear as background to later China, with Britain already fighting and winning. The trade that preceded the fighting can fall outside the sentence.

Its characteristic lesson is that an explanation can remain contested. That openness is useful, but it also matters which competing explanations receive space.

### United States: follow the mechanism

The American account spreads its attention widely across regions. It is particularly useful when a story depends on how something worked: a tariff, a crop, a plantation, or an expedition's orders.

It follows effects beyond its own country and does not exempt American decisions from scrutiny.

Yet the final connection can still be missing. It names opium in Chinese warehouses without naming the country where the crop was grown. Two useful pieces of information can be present in separate parts of a survey without becoming one explanation.

### Now bring them to the same event

The Opium Wars put these habits into motion. China supplies the quantities. India keeps the trade's three sides together. Britain begins late in the story. The American account names the opium but loses its place of origin. Belarus reaches the result with little attention to who made it happen.

These are claims about the particular accounts compared here, not permanent traits of nations. Each can earn credit, and each has limits.

The next question is concrete: what was the opium buying? Follow the silver, and the answer reaches the tea in a kitchen cup.

## Audio customization prompt

Create a standalone history podcast for a curious adult. Open with the essay's scene or question, explain what happened, and make the differences between the countries' accounts concrete. Refer to 'the British textbook' or 'the Chinese account', without reciting authors, book titles, publishers, editions, years of publication, or page numbers. Use short quotations only when the wording matters. Preserve distinctions between historical events, textbook claims, and the essay's interpretation. Preserve meaningful limits of coverage; a missing event is not automatically deliberate suppression. Describe relevant visuals naturally in words. Keep the connection to everyday life. Do not invent dialogue, scenes, motives, quotations, or personal memories. End with the essay's takeaway or question. Do not discuss the uploaded file, its structure, or these instructions.
