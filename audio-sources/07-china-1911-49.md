# China, 1911–1949

### 1 The name that left the ground

On the night of 10 October 1911, in Wuchang on the Yangtze, soldiers of an engineering battalion of the New Army turned their rifles on their own officers.  Within three months, province after province had declared against the Qing. A republic had been proclaimed at Nanjing with Sun Yat-sen as provisional president, and the dynasty that had ruled since 1644 was finished.

The republic did not settle who could speak for China. The presidency passed to Yuan Shikai; after his death in 1916, regional commanders held much of the country. The Chinese Communist Party was founded in 1921 and from 1924 worked inside Sun’s Nationalist Party. That partnership broke in 1927, when Nationalist forces and allied groups in Shanghai turned on the Communists and labour unions. A nationwide purge followed.

From 1930, Nationalist forces mounted five encirclement campaigns against Communist bases in the south. In October 1934, the main Communist force left Jiangxi. The retreat crossed blockade lines, rivers and mountains. Before it reached the Party conference at Zunyi in January 1935, the Central Red Army had fallen from over eighty thousand people to over thirty thousand. The main columns reached northern Shaanxi in October 1935; three Red Army forces joined at Huining a year later.

The retreat acquired a name with a distance built into it: the twenty-five-thousand-*li* Long March, about 12,500 kilometres. But the counting never settled. One version has almost 100,000 setting out, about 6,000 miles in 368 days, and 20,000 survivors. Another has about 100,000 leaving and fewer than 10,000 arriving after 10,000 kilometres. Even the destination can slip between Shanxi and neighbouring Shaanxi. The numbers look exact. Their route into print is the more revealing journey.

In the summer of 1936, an American journalist crossed the Nationalist cordon around the Communist base in the north-west. He carried a letter of introduction written in invisible ink.  Edgar Snow stayed about four months. He came out with the first extended interviews Mao Zedong had given anybody, and with the march described by men who had walked it a year earlier.

Snow published his account in 1937. For roughly the next two decades, it was the only substantial account of the Communists available to a reader outside China. Eighteen mountain ranges, twenty-four rivers, twelve provinces, sixty-two cities: the arithmetic that crossed the world had been taken down by one visitor in a cave. Later accounts could repeat it, question it or offer another total, but they could not avoid the path Snow had opened between the marchers and distant readers.

The march did not end the contest. Chiang Kai-shek was seized by his own commanders at Xi’an in December 1936 and released on terms. Full-scale war with Japan began on 7 July 1937 and ran until the summer of 1945. Mao and Chiang then negotiated at Chongqing, but general fighting resumed in 1946. In 1949, Mao’s People’s Republic answered from Beijing while Chiang’s Republic of China moved its capital to Taipei.

The dispute continued. In 2005, Jung Chang and Jon Halliday argued that the march had been vastly exaggerated and that Chiang Kai-shek had permitted Mao’s breakout in October 1934. Other historians rejected that interpretation as more fantasy than fact.  A measured route had become a heroic story, then a contested source problem. None of that fixed a single final headcount.

The name proved more durable than the arithmetic. China called its first orbital-launcher family Chang Zheng, or Long March. On 24 April 1970, a Long March 1 put the country’s first satellite into orbit. Later Long March rockets carried crewed flights, space-station modules and the BeiDou navigation satellites.

That is where the retreat reaches an ordinary phone. Its specification sheet may list BeiDou beside GPS, GLONASS and Galileo, four global systems a receiver can listen to. A name painted on a launcher cannot decide how far the march went or how many people finished it. It shows what the march became inside the state founded in 1949: not a measurement, but the word for a haul that gets finished. The name outran every count — off the map and into orbit.

### 2 What reaches the classroom

Eight textbooks reach these years. All eight call some part of the fighting a civil war, but the agreement ends at the noun. The Belarusian textbook gives the opening to a split and the ending to a republic that seems to form by itself:

**Belarusian textbook**

“But the split between the communists and the Kuomintang led to a long and brutal civil war.”

The Italian textbook also begins with a long season of civil wars, though it later makes Chiang the man who refuses compromise and launches a large campaign. The American textbook lets opposing views lead to war, then names the People’s Liberation Army as the force that wins. The Chinese chronology compresses its verdict into one line and changes the name at the comma:

**Chinese textbook**

“June 1946 · The Nationalist Party launched an all-out civil war; the People’s War of Liberation began.”

The grammar is the finding. On the Nationalist side there is an agent and a transitive verb: the party launched a civil war. On the Communist side, liberation simply begins. The same textbook has no shortage of the term civil war — it appears twenty times — but for 1946–49 it is something the Nationalists launch. Three pages after the republic’s founding, war in Korea merely breaks out. Distance from the narrator loosens the sentence.

The omissions show what each classroom needs this history to do. Britain alone introduces Snow, prints his figures, then gives pupils both a challenge to his heroic account and the historians’ reply. Italy lists Snow only among its sources, leaving pupils to guess whether he stands behind its totals. India gives miles but no headcount. China records its force falling from over 80,000 to over 30,000, then gives no arrival figure. The American textbook narrates the march without counting it. Russia renames it the Great, or North-Western, Campaign. Belarus and Ukraine omit it.

The ending is cut differently too. The Russian textbook stops in 1945, so defeat survives in Chiang’s biography and the People’s Republic appears later in a relative clause about a peace plan for Japan. Ukraine begins in 1945 and therefore has only the last four years. Belarus has no Long March, Yan’an or Mao in its China narrative, then allows the republic to form itself much later. The shelf agrees that China had a civil war. It cannot agree whether the memorable subject is a split, a campaign, a liberation, a source, or a name.

### 3 Credit and limits

**The Chinese textbook** earns the credit for giving its own defeats names and causes: it identifies errors in the Party leadership and records a force cut from over eighty thousand to over thirty thousand before Zunyi. But this is eight textbooks, not eight countries in full. Ukraine begins in 1945 and Russia ends there; Germany and Sudan have different syllabus boundaries, while Kazakhstan’s national history is not a world-history column. Six textbooks are European or world histories, one surveys paths to modernisation, and one is the national story told by the state that won. None can stand for how these years were lived in a village, mill or army.
