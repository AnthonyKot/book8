# The War on Somebody Else’s Ground

On the night of 8 February 1904, Japanese destroyers run into the outer roadstead at Port Arthur and put torpedoes into the Russian Pacific squadron lying at anchor. Declarations of war follow the attack.  The port is Chinese. Its Chinese name is Lüshunkou, and Russia has held it on a twenty-five-year lease from the Qing since 1898. For the next eighteen months two empires fight for it across the fields of Manchuria, and the empire that owns the fields declares neutrality.

### 1 What an afternoon at sea left behind

The story begins nine years before the torpedoes. In April 1895, Japan defeated Qing China and received the Liaodong Peninsula in the Treaty of Shimonoseki. Days later Russia, Germany and France pressed Japan to hand it back.  In 1898 Russia took a twenty-five-year lease on the same peninsula, including Lüshunkou and Dalian, and built a railway branch south across Manchuria to reach them.

That reversal left Russia and Japan facing each other over Manchuria and Korea. Britain and Japan signed an alliance in 1902.  Negotiations between Tokyo and St Petersburg ran through 1903 and failed. Then came the night attack, a siege that lasted roughly eleven months, the surrender of Port Arthur on 2 January 1905, and the enormous land battle around Mukden in February and March.  The ground beneath the armies remained Chinese and Korean ground.

The war’s longest journey began in the Baltic. Russia sent a squadron seven months and most of the way around the world to reach the fighting. On the afternoon of 27 May 1905, it met the Japanese fleet in the Korea Strait. The Russian line was broken before dark; surrenders and pursuit continued into the next day.  What navies carried away from Tsushima was not the nationality of the ground. It was a demonstration of how the next battleship should fight.

At Portsmouth that September, Japan received southern Sakhalin and a free hand in Korea but no money. The treaty was mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt, who received the Nobel Peace Prize the following year. News of the terms brought rioting in Tokyo, and Korea was annexed in 1910.

For years, naval designers had argued that a battleship should carry a single calibre of gun, the largest, and nothing else worth counting.  Tsushima made the argument visible. The shooting happened at ranges where the medium guns carried by the dozen were spray, while the fleet that could choose the range chose it. Naval attachés sailed home with the result.

Four months later Britain laid down HMS *Dreadnought*: ten twelve-inch guns, steam turbines, and enough speed to fight at a distance of her own choosing.  The battle did not design the ship. Work on all-big-gun designs had begun before Tsushima. But Tsushima made the case impossible to ignore, and *Dreadnought* turned that case into an object. When she appeared, every battleship afloat was second-rate, including the ships that made up Britain’s overwhelming lead. A lead nobody could challenge had become a race anybody could enter.

The German battleships built in that race were scuttled by their own crews at Scapa Flow in Orkney on 21 June 1919. Most were later raised for scrap. Seven wrecks are still down there.

Since the 1950s, plates cut from those wrecks have been sold for a quality nobody intended them to possess. Steel smelted before atmospheric nuclear tests carries no fallout. An instrument built to measure very faint radiation cannot be housed in a case that is faintly radioactive itself, so steel made for a vanished naval race acquired a second life in radiation-sensitive instruments.

The connection does not end at the steel. Anyone alive since the mid-1950s carries carbon-14 above the pre-war level, one of the ways biologists can date human cells.  The chain runs from Chinese ground to a battle in the Korea Strait, from that battle to a British ship, from the ship to a German fleet, and from the fleet’s graves to instruments made to notice what the nuclear age put into the rest of us. The metal is useful now because it has never seen a bomb.

### 2 The country missing from the war’s name

Now put a fifteen-year-old in front of the shelf. Six of the eleven classrooms reach 1905, and five narrate the war. Only one gives the battlefield a nationality. The Indian textbook puts the line beside the abolition of China’s examination system:

**Indian textbook**

“In 1905, just after the Russo-Japanese war (a war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory) the centuries-old Chinese examination system that gave candidates entry into the elite ruling class was abolished.”

The other textbooks make China fade by degrees. The British account calls Manchuria a neighbouring territory and places the war in a chart leading to 1914. The Italian textbook supplies exact geography and the only campaign, including the undeclared attack, but never says which country Manchuria belongs to. The Belarusian textbook reduces ownership to the adjective *Chinese*, though it also prints the ports’ modern names and explains the war’s domestic purpose. The American textbook says Russia leased the land from China, then lets China leave the story.

The Chinese textbook does something stranger. It describes Russia forcibly leasing Lüda, defines the name as Lüshunkou and Dalian Bay, and covers the late-Qing reforms of 1905. It does not mention the war. The Indian and Chinese textbooks even place the same reform before the student: India dates the end of the examination system by the war; China lists the reform without it. Every in-scope textbook but the one standing on the battlefield tells the war.

The ship loses its origin in much the same way. The British textbook lists the Russo-Japanese War and the building of *Dreadnought* two lines apart, then explains elsewhere that the new ship let Germany begin again on equal terms. The American textbook describes the guns and turbines without Tsushima. The Russian textbook reaches back once to the defeat and, on the same page, gives the ship a different parent:

“Dreadnought — a type of English battleship (after the name of the first ship of this class, ‘Dreadnought’ — fearless), created in response to the naval programme of Germany. Thanks to its steam-turbine engine it was capable of developing high speed and of fighting beyond the reach of enemy torpedoes.”

Three classrooms print *Dreadnought*. Two name Tsushima. None joins them. One names the Chinese ground; the Chinese classroom omits the war. A student receives the places, battles and machines as separate units, with the wire between them removed.

### 3 Credit and limits

The Indian textbook earns the credit for twelve bracketed words that make the war part of Chinese history; the Italian textbook earns a smaller share for the only full campaign and the undeclared attack. The comparison is six textbooks whose syllabuses reach 1905, not six countries in the abstract. The Russian textbook begins in 1914, and the Chinese silence belongs to one survey, not an entire curriculum. That survey may omit the war because its late-Qing unit is organised around Chinese actors. Still, it narrates other foreign impositions and the forced lease of this very port. The silence is a signal, not a verdict.
