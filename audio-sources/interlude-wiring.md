# The wiring diagrams

Four accounts can agree on the sequence of events and still teach four explanations for a war.

Consider Munich, the German–Soviet non-aggression pact, and the invasions of Poland in September 1939. Put those events in chronological order and the exercise looks straightforward. Ask which caused which, and the agreement breaks down.

This comparison follows four particular accounts: Russian, British, German and Chinese. They cover different ground. The point is to hear where each places the weight of its explanation.

### Russia: Munich makes the pact necessary

In the Russian account, Munich carries the main explanatory burden. The pact then becomes a consequence of Western policy rather than an independent Soviet choice. The book calls it “a forced step on the part of the USSR.”

Germany's invasion opens the September war. The Soviet entry into Poland follows in a sentence where troops have received an order, but nobody gives it. The action is present; the decision-maker is missing.

The student is offered a chain in which Munich explains what follows. Versailles and the Depression do not carry the argument here. The important distinction is between an event being mentioned somewhere in a book and being used to explain this war.

### Britain: the explanation remains an argument

The British account gives both Munich and the pact a place in the explanation. It presents disagreement rather than closing it down.

Listen to how it attributes one defence of the Soviet decision: “Russian historians justify the pact on the grounds that it gave the USSR time to prepare its defences against a possible German attack.” That is a position the account reports, not a conclusion it simply adopts.

The Soviet entry into Poland is connected to the agreement: Poland was divided “as agreed in the pact of August 1939.” The sentence's main concern is the collapse of Polish resistance.

Elsewhere, the British account makes a connection none of the other three makes in this comparison: “It was the war that finally put an end to the depression.” Here the war explains the end of an economic crisis. Later, the Chinese account will use that crisis to explain a war's beginning.

### Germany: the central decision comes from Berlin

The German account centres German responsibility. The pact serves as an instrument for “the smashing of Poland and the division of the country between Germany and the Soviet Union.” The agreement is also described as “the joint division, exploitation and oppression of Europe.”

Munich appears as a missed opportunity to stop Hitler. Officers around Ludwig Beck had prepared a coup in the event of war over the Sudetenland. The agreement removed that occasion. As the account puts it, “A promising opportunity to overthrow Hitler was thereby squandered.”

This is a different role for the same event. Munich does not supply an explanation that transfers responsibility to the Western powers. It marks a chance for German opposition to interrupt a course driven from Berlin.

### China: the story starts earlier and elsewhere

The Chinese volume is a Chinese-history course. Munich, the pact and the two September invasions are absent from it. That limit matters: their absence cannot by itself establish deliberate avoidance.

Its explanation follows a longer history of foreign pressure on China. Of the Treaty of Nanking, it says, “this treaty was the first unequal treaty in modern Chinese history that humiliated the nation and forfeited its sovereignty.”

The Paris settlement enters through China's experience: “at the Paris Peace Conference, power triumphed over justice, and China suffered a serious diplomatic defeat.”

Then the Depression becomes a cause of Japanese aggression. The account says: “In the autumn of 1929 a worldwide economic crisis broke out, gravely affecting Japan. The Japanese ruling clique, in order to ease internal contradictions and escape its predicament, was impatient to launch a war of aggression against China.”

The story moves from that explanation to the railway explosion near Liutiaohu. The war to be explained begins before 1939, in Asia, within a longer account of imperial pressure.

### Same event, different explanatory job

The contrast is easiest to hear through the Depression. In the British account, war ends it. In the Chinese account, it helps explain the move towards war. In the Russian and German arguments examined here, it carries no comparable weight.

Munich also changes roles. It makes the Soviet pact necessary, remains part of an open argument, marks a missed opportunity to overthrow Hitler, or lies outside the volume's coverage.

Dates alone cannot show these differences. The crucial question is what job each event does in the explanation.

Think back to the history you learned. Which event made the next one seem inevitable? Which decision remained a choice? And whose decision was it?

## Audio customization prompt

Create a standalone history podcast for a curious adult. Open with the essay's scene or question, explain what happened, and make the differences between the countries' accounts concrete. Refer to 'the British textbook' or 'the Chinese account', without reciting authors, book titles, publishers, editions, years of publication, or page numbers. Use short quotations only when the wording matters. Preserve distinctions between historical events, textbook claims, and the essay's interpretation. Preserve meaningful limits of coverage; a missing event is not automatically deliberate suppression. Describe relevant visuals naturally in words. Keep the connection to everyday life. Do not invent dialogue, scenes, motives, quotations, or personal memories. End with the essay's takeaway or question. Do not discuss the uploaded file, its structure, or these instructions.
