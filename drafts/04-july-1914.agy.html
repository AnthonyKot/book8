Who started 1914

On the afternoon of 28 June 1914, on Franz Josef Street in Sarajevo, a nineteen-year-old student steps out from outside a delicatessen and fires two pistol shots into an open car. Within five weeks, five great powers and their colonial empires are at war. How five classrooms get from those two shots to a global war—and why three others skip the July Crisis altogether—is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who started it—and how does your textbook's grammar get from two shots in Sarajevo to a world war?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 28 June 1914: Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and his wife Sophie are assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist associated with the Black Hand.
  • 5–6 July 1914: Imperial Germany issues a pledge of unconditional diplomatic and military support (later termed the "blank check") to Austria-Hungary.
  • 23 July 1914: Austria-Hungary presents Serbia with a 48-hour ultimatum containing ten stringent demands; Serbia accepts nine but rejects terms infringing on its judicial sovereignty.
  • 28 July 1914: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia and begins artillery bombardment of Belgrade.
  • 29–30 July 1914: Imperial Russia orders partial, then general mobilization along its western frontiers to protect Serbia.
  • 1–4 August 1914: Germany declares war on Russia (1 Aug) and France (3 Aug), then invades neutral Belgium (4 Aug), triggering Britain's entry into the war against Germany. Austria-Hungary declares war on Russia on 6 August.
  • Historiography: The post-1919 consensus (Versailles Article 231) assigned sole guilt to Germany; interwar revisionism emphasized shared imperial rivalry and miscalculation; Fritz Fischer's 1961 work re-established Germany's primary responsibility for provoking war; modern research stresses competitive mobilization and crisis management failures.

2 The ladder

Five narrators who cover the outbreak of July 1914, ordered from thinnest agency (a nation pulled into war against its will) to thickest (premeditated design by named leaders). China, Sudan, and India name the war's existence in timelines or post-war treaties but skip the crisis and who opened fire—an avoidance signal in three of eight in-scope books. Ukraine (covers post-1945) and Germany (National Socialism primer) are out of range. Kazakhstan is a national history course and never a world-event column here.

3 The tell

Every narrator in this set acknowledges that Franz Ferdinand was shot in Sarajevo, that Austria sent an ultimatum to Belgrade, and that armies mobilized across Europe in late July 1914. The divergence is in how the sentence structure distributes intent—and whether the classroom encounters a deliberate decision, a shared system failure, or a passive force of nature.

OpenStax (US) performs a remarkable shift in grammatical voice. Germany extends a “blank check” to Austria-Hungary, emboldening Vienna to declare war on Serbia. But when Russia orders mobilization, Germany suddenly loses its active subjecthood: it “now found itself pulled into the fight whether it wanted to be or not.” A major military empire that declared war on two neighboring powers in three days is framed as a reluctant passenger dragged along by structural alliance obligations.

Belarus (BY) opens its section by evoking the Versailles Treaty of 1919—where Germany was initially assigned single-handed guilt—only to dismantle that verdict for the student. After detailing how Germany issued ultimatums and invaded Belgium, the text steps back to summarize modern consensus: “it became clear that the problem of responsibility was much more complicated… all its main participants were to one degree or another guilty.” Guilt is diluted across all imperial cabinets, converting a specific war-initiating move into a shared European outcome.

«У каждой из сторон была своя доля вины. Однако непосредственно развязывание войны связано с неспровоцированными военными действиями австро-германского блока…»

“Each of the sides had its share of guilt. However, directly the unleashing of the war is connected with unprovoked military actions of the Austro-German block…”

RU · Чубарьян 2023 · §3–4, p. 29

Russia’s unified textbook (RU) executes a double move within two paragraphs. First, it concedes systemic symmetry: every great power considered war inevitable, prepared for it, and carried “its share of guilt.” But having established that background equality, Chubaryan inserts a decisive legal distinction: the actual unleashing of hostilities was an “unprovoked military action” by the Austro-German bloc against Serbia, Belgium, and Luxembourg. Shared appetite does not erase the identity of the nation that strikes first.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) trace the mechanics of escalation step by step. Austria makes “the first move” with the ultimatum of 23 July; Russia takes “the second step” by mobilizing. But the pivot of the entire crisis belongs to Berlin: “It was thus the initiative of the German government… that made the situation definitively precipitate.” Italy isolates the German guarantee not as passive alignment, but as the active catalyst that broke diplomatic restraint.

Lowe (UK) stands alone by turning the entire chapter into a courtroom of historiography. Instead of delivering a state verdict, Lowe names the competing arguments: Austrian aggression, Russian mobilization, German encouragement, and British silence. He introduces named scholars—Fritz Fischer, Terence Zuber, Samuel Williamson—and concludes by reporting that “a majority of historians… accept Fritz Fischer’s theory” that German leaders deliberately provoked the conflict. The British classroom does not merely describe the outbreak; it teaches the student how historians debate blame.

The starkest finding of all, however, comes from the classrooms that say nothing about July 1914. China (CN), Sudan (SD), and India (IN) mention the First World War as a background milestone—for the Twenty-One Demands, the Sykes-Picot Agreement, or colonial troop levies. But none of the three allocates a single sentence to Sarajevo, the ultimatum, or the mobilization cascade. In three out of eight in-scope national syllabuses, the question of who fired the first shot in Europe is not answered differently; it is omitted entirely as irrelevant to national history.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the primary credit line for this chapter. It is the only textbook in the corpus that explicitly teaches high school students that historical truth is established through evidence-based debate among named scholars rather than state decree. By introducing Fritz Fischer's thesis alongside counter-arguments, Lowe equips the reader to evaluate how historical verdicts are built.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) deserves credit for conceptual clarity: it distinguishes between structural preconditions (alliances, imperial rivalries, armaments races) and contingent human choices ("errors and rash choices committed by the political and military leaders"). It resists both historical determinism and vague multi-lateral hand-waving.

Chubaryan (RU) is notable for maintaining a two-tiered analytical framework: acknowledging the shared imperialist ambitions and preparations of all European belligerents while firmly pinning the immediate legal act of unprovoked aggression on the Austro-German alliance.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder analyzes five narrators that provide detailed narrative accounts of the July Crisis of 1914. It does not represent every textbook published worldwide.

The absence of the July Crisis narrative in China, Sudan, and India represents an avoidance signal (3 of 8 in-scope books omit the crisis while covering the war's existence or aftermath). This reflects curriculum prioritization of domestic revolutionary history and anti-colonial struggle rather than European diplomatic history, rather than deliberate suppression of July 1914.

Ukraine (1945–2018 syllabus) and Germany (National Socialism civic primer) fall outside the chronological range for World War I outbreak coverage. Kazakhstan is a national history textbook and is excluded from world-history comparison ladders per project rules.

All non-English textbook extracts (Russian, Belorussian, Italian) have been cross-verified against raw extracted text files (`corpus-orig/`) and checked for accurate translation context.
