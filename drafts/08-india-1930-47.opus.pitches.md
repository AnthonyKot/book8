### Pitch 1: The oldest tax in the world *(my candidate — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** One classroom in eleven narrates what Gandhi actually walked to the sea to do, and it explains the law first: “Under the colonial system, Indians could not gather or sell salt; instead, they had to purchase it from the British and pay a heavy tax. On March 12, 1930, Gandhi began a protest known as the Salt March, in which he led his supporters on a two-hundred-mile, twenty-four-day march to the Arabian Sea to collect salt from the seawater” (US, p. 504). Three other classrooms teach the *method* — Russia’s “refusal of English goods,” Belarus’s “boycott of goods,” Italy’s “campaigns of civil disobedience and boycott of English institutions” — and not one of them names a single good. The word “salt” does not appear in the Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Italian, British or Indian books in connection with India at all.
- **Loop.** Salt is the one mineral a human body must eat and cannot make, which is exactly why states have taxed it since they have existed: the Han court made salt and iron a state monopoly in the second century BCE, France’s *gabelle* helped make a revolution, and the British Indian salt tax survived every reform because it collects from everyone who eats → the 1930 campaign therefore does not attack a policy, it attacks the point where the state touches every single body it rules → a man bends down and picks up mud, and the empire has to decide whether to arrest him for it → it does, sixty thousand times, and every arrest publishes the same argument again.
- **Lander.** The salt cellar on the table, and a second classroom that already teaches this exact instrument without knowing it connects: the Chinese book, describing Emperor Wu, «实行盐铁官营，由政府垄断盐、铁的生产和销售» — “implemented the state monopoly of salt and iron; the government monopolised the production and sale of salt and iron” (CN, p. 23, 第4课). Two textbooks in this corpus, two thousand years apart, one commodity. The reader in any of the eleven countries has a state’s oldest revenue instrument in their kitchen.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms narrates the Salt March (US). 3 of 11 teach the boycott as a method and 0 of 11 name the boycotted good. 1 of 11 teaches the salt monopoly as statecraft, in a chapter about the Han dynasty (CN). 0 of 11 connect the two.
- **Receipts-lite.** The Salt Act of 1882 and the Indian salt revenue; the Great Hedge of India — the customs barrier of thorn and brush, over 1,100 miles long at its height, built to stop salt smuggling — documented in Roy Moxham, *The Great Hedge of India* (2001); the Dandi memorial at Navsari, Gujarat, open to visitors. <!-- CHECK: Indian Salt Act 1882; Inland Customs Line hedge peaked at roughly 1,100–2,500 miles in the 1870s; salt duty abolished by independent India in 1946–47 -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** It answers the question the ladder asks — was independence given or taken — with an object rather than a verdict, and it does it from the one column that bothered to explain the law being broken. It also braids straight back to ch. 1: the same company that engineered an opium market in China ran a salt monopoly in the colony that grew the opium.

### Pitch 2: The good nobody names *(my candidate)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** A silence with three witnesses. Belarus: «Сатьяграха включала бойкот товаров, школ, судебных и государственных учреждений» — “satyagraha included a boycott of goods, schools, judicial and state institutions” (BY, p. 136). Russia: nonviolent resistance meant “the refusal of titles, posts, awards, English schools, English courts, elections, English goods” (RU, p. 123). Italy: “campaigns of civil disobedience and boycott of English institutions” (IT, p. 529). Three classrooms, one method, and the noun is “goods” in all three. Nought of eleven names cloth. Nought of eleven contains the words *khadi*, *charkha*, *homespun* or *Lancashire*. Two books get close enough to be tantalising and stop: Russia notes that “demands for independence clothed in traditional dress became comprehensible to many tens of millions of ordinary people,” and OpenStax that Gandhi “wore traditional modes of dress.”
- **Loop.** India spun and wove the cloth the world bought until British tariff and British machinery reversed the trade, so that by the nineteenth century India exported raw cotton and imported finished cloth from Lancashire → the nationalist answer is not a tariff, which a colony cannot levy, but a wheel: spin your own thread, wear what you spin, and stop buying → *khadi* turns a boycott into something a villager can do daily and a photographer can see → the spinning wheel goes onto the Congress flag in 1931, and in July 1947 the constituent assembly keeps the flag and replaces the wheel with the Ashoka chakra, the wheel of law from a third-century BCE pillar.
- **Lander.** The wheel in the middle of India’s flag — on every Olympic broadcast, every cricket match, every airline tail — is where the spinning wheel used to be. The second half of the lander is linguistic and reaches every corpus country: *khaki* (Urdu/Persian for dust-coloured), *pyjama*, *calico*, *chintz*, *dungaree*, *cashmere* are Indian cloth words that entered English, Russian, Italian and Ukrainian and are still worn by police forces and toddlers on four continents.
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 11 classrooms teach the boycott; 0 of 11 name the boycotted commodity; 0 of 11 mention khadi, the charkha or Lancashire; 0 of 11 explain the flag.
- **Receipts-lite.** Congress flag with charkha adopted 1931; Constituent Assembly resolution on the national flag, 22 July 1947; the Khadi and Village Industries Commission still trades under an Act of 1956; Gandhi’s wheel is in the Sabarmati Ashram museum, Ahmedabad. <!-- CHECK: 1931 Congress flag; 22 July 1947 flag resolution replacing charkha with Ashoka chakra; KVIC Act 1956 -->
- **Risk.** The Lancashire half of the loop must be stated with causal precision. The boycott did not kill the Lancashire cotton industry; Japanese and Indian mills and the Depression did most of it. What the boycott produced was the political fact — a form of protest a hundred million people could perform without leaving home.

### Pitch 3: The Indian chairman in Khartoum *(my candidate — non-obvious, braids ch. 2 → ch. 8 → ch. 14)*

- **Seed (from the comparison, not from the India pages).** The book that is formally *out of scope* for this chapter is the one that puts India inside somebody else’s independence. Sudan’s MoE textbook lists, among the seven forces that made Sudanese nationalism: “The impact of the Indian Congress, manifested in the Sudanese intellectuals’ admiration for the personality of (Mahatma Gandhi) and his resistance to British colonialism” (SD, printed p. 90). Two hundred pages later it reproduces Article 7 of the 1953 Anglo-Egyptian agreement in full, including a clause no other book in the corpus contains: a seven-member Mixed Electoral Commission — three Sudanese, one Egyptian, one British, one American, one Indian — and “the chairmanship of the commission shall belong to the Indian member” (SD, printed p. 264).
- **Loop.** Gandhi works out satyagraha in South Africa against a colonial statute, and NCERT’s own timeline files that under AFRICA → the method travels back to India, and out again along the same imperial channels that carried the administrators, to Sudanese intellectuals reading about the Indian Congress in the 1920s → India becomes independent in 1947 and immediately becomes the thing an empire needs when it is leaving: a party that is neither the colonised nor the coloniser → so when Britain and Egypt have to certify that Sudan’s self-determination vote is honest, the treaty text hands the chair to India, and the man who takes it is Sukumar Sen, who had just run the first Indian general election, the largest vote ever held anywhere → Sudan is independent on 1 January 1956.
- **Lander.** The neutral election observer — the outsider whose signature makes a result count — has been on the news in every one of these eleven countries, and 1953 in Khartoum is one of the places the job was invented. The second lander, if a harder object is wanted: the purple stain on a voter’s finger, an Indian export used in dozens of countries’ elections. <!-- CHECK: indelible ink developed at India’s National Physical Laboratory, manufactured by Mysore Paints since 1962, exported to many countries — this is a later development than 1953 and must not be presented as part of the loop -->
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms records Gandhi as an influence on another colony’s national movement (SD). 1 of 11 prints the clause (SD). 0 of 11 — including India’s own book — say that independent India took a hand in ending anybody else’s empire.
- **Receipts-lite.** Anglo-Egyptian Agreement on Self-Government and Self-Determination for the Sudan, 12 February 1953, Article 7; Sukumar Sen, first Chief Election Commissioner of India, chaired the Sudan Electoral Commission; Sudanese parliamentary elections November 1953; independence 1 January 1956. <!-- CHECK: Sen's chairmanship of the Sudan Electoral Commission, 1953; agreement signed 12 February 1953 -->
- **Why it is worth the risk.** It is the only candidate that makes ch. 2’s Sudanese column and ch. 8’s Indian blank explain each other, and it feeds ch. 14 directly. It is also the only one where the finding is a piece of treaty text sitting in a school textbook nobody would think to open for this topic.

### Pitch 4: The line drawn in five weeks *(the map lander)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Three classrooms describe the consequence and none names the cause. Ukraine: “The resettlement was accompanied by mass murders from both sides. In clashes about 100 thousand people died” (UA, p. 131). Italy: “over 100,000 dead and the transfer from one state to the other of 17 million persons” (IT, p. 530). Lowe: “especially in the Punjab, where about 250 000 people were murdered” (UK, p. 537), followed by A. N. Wilson’s complaint that Mountbatten “should have taken more care in deciding the frontiers.” Nowhere in eleven books does the name Radcliffe appear, or the words “boundary commission.”
- **Loop.** Partition needs a border, and the border needs to be drawn before 15 August → the job goes to a British barrister who had never been east of Suez, chosen precisely because he knew nothing and no one, with about five weeks and out-of-date census maps → the awards are signed on 12 August and withheld until 17 August, so that for two days after independence tens of millions of people do not know which country they are standing in → the columns start moving in both directions across a line nobody has seen, and the Punjab becomes the killing ground every one of these books counts and none of them explains.
- **Lander.** Open the map app. The India–Pakistan border through the Punjab is not a river or a ridge; it is a set of district boundaries stitched together in a Delhi bungalow in the summer of 1947, and it is one of the few frontiers on earth with a nightly televised ceremony at its gate. The unfinished part of the same job is Kashmir, which three of these books already tell you is still contested.
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 11 classrooms count the partition dead (UK, UA, IT); 4 of the 7 in-scope books give no figure at all; 3 of 11 carry Kashmir into the present (UA, US, IT); 0 of 11 name the man who drew the line or the five weeks he had.
- **Receipts-lite.** Radcliffe arrived in India 8 July 1947, chaired both Boundary Commissions, awards published 17 August; the Wagah–Attari border ceremony has run daily since 1959. <!-- CHECK: Radcliffe arrival 8 July 1947; awards signed 12 August, published 17 August; Wagah ceremony from 1959 -->
- **Weakness.** It is the story of a catastrophe rather than a connection, and it has no object in the reader’s hand — a line on a map is the thinnest kind of lander this book allows. Strongest as two sentences inside whichever thread wins.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (salt)** first. It is seeded by the ladder’s loudest asymmetry — one classroom out of eleven
explains what the most famous act of the period actually was — it has an object every reader owns, and
the Chinese book supplies an independent second witness to the same instrument two thousand years
earlier, which is exactly the “shared across corpus countries” test. **Pitch 3** second, and the most
original of the four: it is the only one that turns this chapter’s conspicuous blank into a finding
about what India *did* rather than what its textbook omits, and it braids two other chapters. **Pitch 2**
has the best lander of any candidate — the flag — but needs the tightest causal discipline and the most
new history per sentence. **Pitch 4** should be harvested for two lines and otherwise banked.
