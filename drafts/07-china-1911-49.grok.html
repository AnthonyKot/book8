China 1911–1949

On 1 October 1949, on the gate tower above Tiananmen Square in Beijing, Mao Zedong declares that a Central People’s Government has been established. Behind that sentence sits a republic proclaimed in 1912, a May Fourth generation that walked the same square in 1919, a Long March, a war with Japan, and a final fight between Nationalists and Communists. What eight national classrooms call that final fight — and who they put in the subject of the sentence that wins it — is the subject of this chapter.

The question · naming

What do you call the fight that ends on Tiananmen in 1949 — a civil war, a Chinese revolution, or a people’s war of liberation?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 10 October 1911: the Wuchang Uprising begins the Xinhai Revolution. On 1 January 1912 a provisional Republic of China is proclaimed in Nanjing with Sun Yat-sen as provisional president; the Qing court abdicates in February 1912.
  • After Yuan Shikai’s presidency and death, China fragments into competing regional armies — the Warlord Era of the late 1910s and 1920s.
  • 4 May 1919: student protests in Beijing against the post-war settlement’s treatment of Shandong (the May Fourth Movement). The Chinese Communist Party (CCP) is founded in 1921; Sun’s Nationalist Party (Kuomintang / Guomindang, KMT/GMD) reorganises with Comintern advice.
  • 1926–28: the Northern Expedition under Chiang Kai-shek reunifies much of the country under the Nanjing Nationalist government. In 1927 the KMT–CCP alliance breaks; mass killings of Communists and leftists follow in Shanghai and elsewhere.
  • 1934–36: after Nationalist encirclement campaigns, Communist forces abandon southern base areas and march to a new base in the northwest (the Long March); Yan’an becomes the CCP centre. Japan’s full-scale invasion of China begins in 1937; a second KMT–CCP united front forms against Japan.
  • After Japan’s 1945 surrender, KMT–CCP talks fail. Full-scale civil war resumes in 1946. By late 1949 Communist forces control the mainland; Chiang’s government relocates to Taiwan. On 1 October 1949 the People’s Republic of China is proclaimed in Beijing.

2 The ladder

Eight narrators who cover some or all of 1911–1949, ordered from thinnest agency (a People’s Republic that forms without an actor) to thickest (named parties, named coup, named liberation war). Germany’s NS booklet and Sudan’s national syllabus never reach this row; Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event peer column here. Those absences are syllabus boundaries, not a verdict on China.

3 The tell

Nobody in this set denies that by late 1949 the Communists held the mainland and the Nationalists did not. The disagreement is about the name of the fight and about who is allowed to hold a verb while it ends.

Belarus writes the purest agentless close in the corpus: the People’s Republic of China was formed — no party, no army, no square. A page earlier the same book does give the middle of the story a name, “a long and brutal civil war,” and keeps Chiang as “official leader” until 1949. Formation is weather; the civil war is atmosphere. India’s NCERT keeps the passive establishment sentence (“was established in 1949”) but then does a job almost nobody else attempts: it defines the PRC’s early programme as “New Democracy,” an alliance of classes, and sets it against the Soviet Union’s “dictatorship of the proletariat.” The Indian classroom is watching two socialist models, not only a Chinese civil war.

Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945, so 1911, May Fourth, and the Long March never appear. What it has is the close: “completion of the civil war,” a truce that is only a truce, and two patrons — the USSR for the CPC, the USA for the Kuomintang. Mao still gets the proclamation on Tiananmen. Russia’s grade-10 book reaches further back and names a different wound. The Long March is not a victory epic: Chiang’s offensives force a retreat, and “the Soviet movement in China suffered a defeat,” even as a Yan’an bridgehead remains. Later, Chiang flees to Taiwan with two million supporters after defeat in the 1946–1949 civil war. The word throughout is civil war. The Red Army’s move is a North-Western March that costs the “Soviet movement” its claim to success.

抗战胜利后，国民党为坚持独裁统治，发动全面内战。中国共产党领导人民解放战争

“After victory in the War of Resistance, the Nationalist Party, in order to maintain its dictatorial rule, launched an all-out civil war. The Chinese Communist Party led the People’s War of Liberation.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 · Unit 8 opener · printed ~p. 149

China’s state-compiled outline is the home-turf column, and the grammar is the finding. The Nationalists launch an “all-out civil war” in order to keep “dictatorial rule.” The Communists do not merely win that war; they lead a differently named war, the People’s War of Liberation (人民解放战争), which is also the lesson title. The same book stages Chiang’s 12 April 1927 Shanghai action as a “counter-revolutionary coup” that “wantonly” kills Communists and revolutionary masses — a date-stamped verdict no other classroom in this set matches for intensity. When Mao speaks from the gate tower, the quoted line is institutional — the Central People’s Government “has been established today” — not the later commemorative paraphrase about a people that “stood up,” which does not appear in this lesson’s founding extract.

Distance shows in what the freer columns will count. Lowe alone puts a number on the 1927 “purification movement”: “some estimates put the total murdered as high as 250 000.” The same chapter is the only one that walks the Long March with headcounts in plain English — almost 100 000 set out, about 20 000 survivors, roughly 6 000 miles in 368 days. Italy is freer still about Chiang’s postwar choice: knowing he had US support, he “refused every compromise and launched” a grand military campaign; the Communists are “little helped” by the USSR. OpenStax keeps the American mirror in the frame — the United States treats Nationalist Taiwan as the “real” China — and is the only book here that puts “People’s Liberation Army” into the victory sentence as the force that “decisively defeated” the GMD. That is the PLA’s English name entering a Western survey; it is not yet China’s full war-title as verdict.

So the ladder is also a dictionary. Civil war (BY, UA, RU, UK, US, IT timeline language). Chinese revolution / communist revolution (IT’s longer arc). New Democracy (IN). People’s War of Liberation (CN alone as lesson name and lead verb). Same mainland, same October day, different nouns for the road that got there.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns the credit line for this event’s agency honesty at a distance. It alone stages the postwar breakdown as Chiang’s refusal of compromise under American cover, names the thinness of Soviet help to the Communists, and still calls the long arc a season of civil wars ending in a communist revolution. Italy is not a party to the 1949 settlement; the verbs stay sharp.

Lowe (UK) deserves the numbers credit. The 250 000 upper estimate on the 1927 purges and the Long March’s ~100 000 / ~20 000 / 6 000-mile arithmetic are the only place in this set where a survey classroom forces the student to sit with scale. Market distance buys a body count the home-turf book does not print in the same register.

NCERT (IN) earns a third nod for comparative theory: “New Democracy” set against a Soviet “dictatorship of the proletariat” is a distinction the European and American columns mostly skip. It is short, and it is load-bearing.

纲要 (CN) is the depth lead, not the fairness lead — lessons on Xinhai, May Fourth, the April 12 coup, the Long March, the War of Resistance, the People’s War of Liberation, and the founding, with names and dates stacked denser than any peer. Depth is not the same as distance. The chapter needs CN for space and self-pole grammar; it needs IT, UK, and IN for angles CN will not take against its own victory story.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight books whose syllabus touches China’s republican decades. It is not “the world.” Germany’s booklet is a National Socialism primer; Sudan’s secondary history stays regional. Those zeros are other syllabuses, not avoidance of 1911–1949. Ukraine covers only the 1945–49 close — no Xinhai, no Long March — because the volume opens in 1945. That is a bound, not a silence about earlier China.

Kazakhstan’s national-history volume is kept out of the ladder by design (CONTEXT.md): it is not a world-history peer column. Any China material it may hold belongs in signature-topic or receipts discussion, not as a ninth rung here.

Machine-translated discovery files pointed to the pages. Chinese, Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Italian lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English here for the reader. Before any of them becomes a hard claim in a later rewrite, the printed PDF page should be opened again. Page numbers follow printed edition markers where the extract shows them; a few CN unit openers sit across lesson boundaries and should be rechecked against the PDF folio.

Kept because errors are data: Belarus dates Japan’s occupation of Manchuria to 1932, while the Mukden Incident and occupation are conventionally 1931; Italy’s Long March paragraph places the departure among communists “encircled in Hunan,” while most survey accounts centre the Jiangxi base; Russia’s “Soviet movement… suffered a defeat” is a periodisation and verdict other books refuse. None of those lines was silently corrected.

Finally, none of these classrooms is a full military history of the Northern Expedition, the Long March routes, or the 1948–49 decisive campaigns. If you want order-of-battle and county-level land reform, every column will leave you unsatisfied — including the home-turf one.
