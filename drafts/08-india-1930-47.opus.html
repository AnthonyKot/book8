India 1930–1947

On the morning of 6 April 1930, at Dandi on the Gujarat coast, a sixty-year-old lawyer walks into the shallows and lifts a lump of natural salt out of the mud. He set out twenty-four days earlier with seventy-eight others; the road behind him is now full. Picking up that salt is a criminal offence, because in British India salt may be produced and sold by one party only. Seventeen years later, at midnight on 15 August 1947, British rule in India ends and two states exist where one colony had been. What seven classrooms do with the distance between those two mornings is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who ends the British Empire in India — and what verb does that sentence need?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • March 1919: the Rowlatt Act extends wartime detention powers into peacetime. Protest spreads. On 13 April 1919, in the walled garden at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer fire on a penned crowd for about ten minutes.
  • The death toll was disputed from the start. The British Hunter Committee (1920) recorded 379 killed; an Indian National Congress inquiry put the figure at about a thousand. Both numbers are still in circulation.
  • 12 March – 6 April 1930: the Salt March, roughly 240 miles from Sabarmati to Dandi, opens a civil-disobedience campaign in which tens of thousands are arrested. Reform Acts follow the pattern of 1909, 1919 and 1935: wider franchise, more Indian office-holders, no transfer of sovereignty.
  • 1937: under the 1935 Act, Congress wins majorities in eight of eleven provinces. August 1942: the Quit India resolution; Gandhi, Nehru and most of the Congress leadership are jailed for the remainder of the war. 1943: famine in Bengal.
  • 16 August 1946: “Direct Action Day” and the Calcutta killings. March 1947: Mountbatten arrives as viceroy. The Indian Independence Act receives royal assent on 18 July 1947.
  • 15 August 1947: independence for India and Pakistan. The Radcliffe boundary award, dividing Punjab and Bengal, is published on 17 August — two days after the border it describes came into force.
  • Scholarly estimates of the partition dead range from roughly 200,000 to around two million; refugee movement is usually put between ten and twenty million. No figure is settled.
  • 30 January 1948: Gandhi is shot dead in Delhi by Nathuram Godse. Kashmir is fought over in 1947–48 and remains divided.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators cover this, and all seven of the in-scope books do — there is no avoidance signal anywhere on this topic. They are ordered by how much agency the sentence gives Indians for the ending of British rule: from a timeline cell with no actor in it to a paragraph that stages an argument about who the actor was. China’s volume is a national history, Germany’s a National Socialism booklet, Sudan’s a regional syllabus; they are in the receipts. Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event column here.

The gift, and the historians who deny it

3 The tell

Every book on this ladder has the same date and the same outcome: August 1947, one colony, two states. The disagreement is about what kind of verb the ending needs. Five of the seven put Britain in the subject position — granted (US), took the decision to grant (RU), decided to give (UK), announced the granting (UA). Two remove the transaction altogether. Belarus writes independence as something India and Pakistan “attained,” with no second party in the sentence. India’s own book writes it as something they “become.”

Russia goes furthest along the donor axis and then adds a legacy clause. «Англия, покинув Индию, оставила ей демократию» — England, having left India, left it democracy — is the only sentence in this corpus that credits an empire with the political system its former colony kept. It arrives three lines after the partition, in the same paragraph that has divided the country “along religious lines,” and it is the last word on India in the book. Chubaryan’s India section is otherwise the most attentive to method of any Russian passage on a colonial subject: it dates the Rowlatt Act, names the two phases of civil resistance, gives Congress ten million members and 150,000 volunteers, and records that during the second campaign the authorities “did not dare to arrange a bloody slaughter again” because “the soldiers this time refused to shoot.” Then it hands the outcome back to London.

Which brings us to Amritsar, where the ladder rearranges itself. Chubaryan is the most agentive narrator of the killing in the whole set: «Расстрел английскими властями мирного митинга 13 апреля 1919 г. в Амритсаре» — the shooting by the English authorities of a peaceful rally on 13 April 1919 in Amritsar — followed by «эта кровавая бойня», this bloody slaughter. Sabbatucci–Vidotto give the episode a section heading of its own, “Il massacro di Amritsar,” and a consequence: after it “the abyss between colonisers and colonised became unbridgeable.” OpenStax names the unit that fired — “the British Indian Army opened fire on the protestors, who had little chance of escape from the closed-in space” — calls the event a massacre in the next sentence, and sets it as a multiple-choice question at the end of the chapter. Belarus keeps the act and drops every coordinate: even in cases when English troops opened fire on demonstrators or rally participants, Gandhi insisted on observing the principle of nonviolence. No place, no date, no number; the sentence’s destination is Gandhi’s discipline, and the shooting is the subordinate clause it travels through.

And Lowe. Amritsar is not in his text and not in his index. Neither is Rowlatt, nor the Salt March, nor Quit India, nor any campaign of civil disobedience in India at all — the only two uses of that phrase in the book belong to the Palestinian intifada and to an election dispute elsewhere. His India opens with the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909 and reaches 1945 through three Acts of Parliament that “all gave the Indians more say in the government of their country.” It is a chronology of concessions with nothing in it to concede to. M. K. Gandhi enters the book on page 535, as a Congress leader who “believed in non-violence” and was still hoping for a united India in 1947; his index entry is two pages long.

The counting is stranger still. Three books put a figure on the walled garden, and no two agree: Italy “almost 400,” OpenStax “hundreds… and more than a thousand wounded,” Russia “over 1 thousand people were killed and about 2 thousand wounded.” None of the three is invented. 379 was the British committee’s count and about a thousand was the Congress inquiry’s, and the gap between them has been argued over for a century. Each classroom has picked a side of that argument, and none of the three tells the student that a side is being picked. Errors are not the finding here; the silent choice is. There is a larger version of the same silence three years before partition, and only two books on this ladder break it.

“So we need to read each timeline critically, see what it tells us and what it does not. Timelines frame history in particular ways.”

IN · NCERT, Themes in World History, Class XI · “On Reading World History,” p. viii — 124 pages before the timeline in question

Partition inverts the ladder completely. The books that made Britain the giver mostly print no death toll at all: Russia none, OpenStax none, Belarus none, NCERT none. Ukraine gives “about 100 thousand” dead in the resettlement clashes. Sabbatucci–Vidotto close the story with a sentence built to hurt — the epic of a national liberation movement that had asserted itself by peaceful means thus concluded with over 100,000 dead and with the transfer from one state to the other of 17 million people. The highest numbers in the corpus are British: 5,000 killed in Calcutta, and “about 250 000 people were murdered” in the Punjab. The imperial narrator counts the most dead — and then prints its own prime minister’s answer for them. Attlee argued “with some justification,” Lowe writes, that Britain could not be blamed for the violence; this was due “to the failure of the Indians to agree among themselves.” Lowe also prints A. N. Wilson on Mountbatten’s “superficial haste, his sheer arrogance and his inattention to vital detail,” and V. P. Menon on the goodwill Britain earned. The passage is a balance sheet of historians. What it is not is a passage in which anything happens in India between 1919 and 1946.

Which leaves the Indian column, and it needs its own preface. NCERT’s chief advisor tells the Class XI student, on page viii, that this year you are not reading about the history of South Asia — Indian history is next year’s book. That is a syllabus boundary, printed in the front matter, and it is why the South Asia column of Timeline IV carries the whole independence movement in two cells: Non-Cooperation in 1921, and “Quit India Movement (1942); India and Pakistan become independent (1947).” In the same decade-row where the Salt March would sit, South Asia’s entry is the first Indian talkie, Alam Ara (1931). Gandhi’s other appearance in this timeline is not in the South Asia column at all; it is under AFRICA, on the previous page — “Mahatma Gandhi advocates satyagraha to resist racist laws (1906).” Jawaharlal Nehru’s name occurs in the book only as the name of a university in the contributors’ list. On this event the self-pole narrator is not a pole. It is a declared vacancy, announced in advance, by a book that has already told the student to read timelines for what they do not say.

4 The thread

Two books on this ladder print the year 1943 with a famine attached to it. OpenStax gives it a paragraph, in the war chapter, between the jailing of the Congress leadership and the arrival of Subhas Chandra Bose: “A devastating famine in Bengal reached a peak in 1943. It was caused by the dispatch of Indian food supplies to support the war effort in Europe, natural disasters toward the end of 1942, an influx of refugees fleeing the Japanese offensives in Burma, and general governmental mismanagement. The famine added fuel to the Indian desire for independence.” (p. 544.) Lowe gives it three lines, twenty-odd pages past his India section and in a different argument — §24.7, “Verdict on decolonization,” where he is weighing whether the empire was worth having. He quotes Piers Brendon that “the history of India is punctuated by famines which caused tens of millions of deaths,” and then: “During a severe famine in Bengal in 1942–3, Churchill refused to divert shipping to take food supplies to Calcutta. The result – over 3 million people died from starvation.” (p. 560.) The four causes and the one name are not the same account. Neither book puts the event in the same chapter as the movement it belongs to.

The other nine classrooms return nothing. Searched for Bengal and for famine in 1943, for голод в Бенгалии, for carestia in that year, for بنغال: Russia none, Ukraine none, Belarus none, Italy none, China none, Germany none, Kazakhstan none, Sudan none, India none. India’s own rationalised edition does not carry its own two to three million dead. Its Timeline IV does count famine dead — “Famine in the Deccan, southern India (1876-78), over 5 million die,” in the SOUTH ASIA column, p. 131 — and counts industrial dead once more, at Bhopal in 1984. The 1940–50 cell of the same column, on the next page, reads in full: “Quit India Movement (1942); India and Pakistan become independent (1947).”

Here is the machine, and it is from general history, not from any book above. Japan takes Rangoon in March 1942 and then Burma — the world’s largest rice exporter, and the source of a modest but decisive share of what eastern India ate. With the Japanese now at Bengal’s frontier, the colonial administration applies a denial policy to the coastal districts: country boats are confiscated or destroyed by the tens of thousands, and rice stocks above a household allowance are removed, so that an invader would find neither transport nor food. Those boats were also how Bengali fishermen fished and how rice moved between districts. At the same time the war has its own queue — Calcutta’s industries, the army, the theatres of the war outside India are provisioned first, grain prices are left to the market, and shipping is allocated elsewhere. Prices go up several times over, and stay up. Refugees arrive from Burma; a cyclone hits the delta in October 1942. What kills is none of these singly: it is that a labourer, a fisherman, a barber holding money in a rising market can no longer buy rice that physically exists a district away. Between two and three million people die, most of them in 1943, most of them not of hunger alone but of what follows hunger. This was not a harvest failure alone, and it was not a planned massacre. It was a priority hierarchy operating as designed.

What was built in response is still running. Rationing came to Calcutta and the Indian cities from 1943 onward and did not leave: ration cards, fair-price shops, statutory price ceilings, government purchase and storage of grain. India kept the machinery at independence and enlarged it, and the Public Distribution System that issues entitlements to several hundred million people today is the direct descendant of that wartime apparatus, buffer stocks and all. So is the vocabulary. A nine-year-old who watched the famine from a school in Santiniketan spent his working life on why people surrounded by food starve; Amartya Sen’s answer — that famines are failures of entitlement, of what a person can command, rather than of what exists — took the Nobel in economics in 1998 and is now the standard way a food crisis is read. “Food security” is not a slogan in that lineage; it is a state accounting term — months of consumption held in reserve, procurement prices, an export ban. A reader has met it in a headline this year, attached to a war or a harvest or a price. It exists so that what happened in Bengal in 1943 shows up on a ledger before it shows up in a death rate.

Two of the eleven classrooms tell any of this. The United States names the famine, its year, its peak and four of its causes, inside the story of the independence movement. Britain names a prime minister, a decision about ships and a number larger than any partition figure it prints — and files it under a verdict on empire rather than under India. The other nine print nothing: Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Italy, China, Germany, Kazakhstan, Sudan — and India.

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the credit line, and earns it against its own interest. It is the only book in the corpus that stages the central question as a live dispute rather than answering it: the official British account of a carefully prepared handover, then Sumit Sarkar and Anita Inder Singh arguing that the Acts of 1919 and 1935 were designed “not to prepare the way for independence but to postpone it,” then the economic case that India had become a drain, then Howard Brasted for the defence. It prints the highest partition death figures on the ladder, quotes Attlee’s exculpation and immediately quotes a historian who blames Mountbatten. A market textbook in the country that ran the Raj puts “Independence was not a gift from the British” on the page in quotation marks. That is the most agentive sentence about Indians in this entire corpus, and it is printed in London.

OpenStax (US) is the method lead. It is the only classroom of the eleven in which the reader learns how the movement worked — the salt monopoly, the 240-mile walk, the twenty-four days, the sixty thousand arrests, the Quit India resolution and the immediate jailing of the leadership, the Bengal famine and its causes, Bose and the Indian National Army, the 1946 naval mutiny. It also volunteers a fact no other book needs: that the United States pressured Britain to let India go. Distance again buys candour, and OpenStax spends it.

NCERT (IN) gets the honesty credit, which is a different prize from either. Its India pages are close to empty, and it says so on page viii before the reader reaches them, in an essay on selection that cites E. H. Carr, warns that “history writing cannot do away with this element of selectivity,” and instructs the student to interrogate timelines for their omissions. A blank that announces itself, explains its own boundary, and hands the student the tool to detect it is not the same object as a blank. It is the only book here that treats its own frame as evidence.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

Coverage on this topic is 7 of 7 in-scope books. There is no avoidance signal anywhere in this chapter, and no zero in it should be read as one. China’s volume is a national history that has no reason to narrate Indian independence, Germany’s is a National Socialism booklet, Sudan’s secondary syllabus runs 1821–1953 in its own region. Kazakhstan’s book is a national history and is never a peer column here.

This chapter’s own planning note was wrong, and the error is worth keeping. It predicted an Amritsar three-way of British silence, American candour and an Indian self-pole. Two-thirds survived checking: Lowe’s zero is real, and OpenStax does name the army, the enclosure and the massacre. The candour is there but the word is “massacre,” not the stronger one the note remembered. The third leg failed outright — the Indian book is not a self-pole on this event, because it has declared India out of scope, and the strongest Amritsar sentence in the corpus turned out to be Russian. Errors are data, including ours.

Page numbers were derived, not copied. Lowe’s extraction has no printed folios, so his pages come from the book’s own index (“India 534–7,” “Gandhi, M.K. 535–6”), which does not match the extraction’s pagination. For the Russian, Belarusian and Ukrainian books the folio sits at the foot of the page and for the Indian and American books at the head; page attributions here were checked against each book’s own contents page before use, and they differ by one or two from the research dossier. Any page cited here should be confirmed against the printed edition before it is treated as a hard reference.

The Sudanese lines in the receipts are reconstructed. That PDF’s Arabic extraction is ligature-mangled — letters decompose and reorder — so the strings cannot be searched or copied, and both quotations below were rebuilt into standard orthography and must be read against the printed page. Printed pages in that book are eight less than the extraction’s.

Finally, none of these seven is a history of India, and the gap shows most where it matters most. The single largest human fact in the period — how many people died in the partition — has no agreed number in any of them, and four of the seven give none at all. Nobody on this ladder names Cyril Radcliffe or his boundary commission; the lines drawn in five weeks by a man who had never been east of Suez are, in these classrooms, lines that appeared.
