Russo-Japanese War, 1905

In the winter of 1904, at a Chinese harbor the maps still call Lüshun and the Western books still call Port Arthur, Japanese warships open fire on the Russian Pacific fleet. No Chinese fleet is in the fight. The peace, when it comes, is signed in New Hampshire. What five classrooms do with that war — and what one classroom that owns the ground does not do — is the subject of this chapter.

The question · omission

Whose soil was this war fought on — and which classroom never names the war?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • Russia and Japan contested influence over Korea and Manchuria after Japan’s victory over China in 1894–95 and Russia’s lease of the Liaodong Peninsula (Port Arthur / Lüshun) from China.
  • 8–10 February 1904: Japan opens hostilities against the Russian fleet at Port Arthur; a formal declaration of war follows the first strikes.
  • The main land campaigns run through southern Manchuria (Chinese territory). Port Arthur falls in early 1905; Mukden, March 1905; the Russian Baltic Fleet is destroyed near Tsushima, May 1905.
  • 5 September 1905: Treaty of Portsmouth (New Hampshire). Japan gains a free hand in Korea, the southern half of Sakhalin, and Russian rights in southern Manchuria including the Port Arthur / Dalny lease.
  • Inside Russia, military defeat and domestic crisis feed the 1905 revolution (Bloody Sunday, January 1905; October Manifesto).
  • Korea becomes a Japanese protectorate in 1905 and is annexed in 1910. The fighting was never a Sino-Japanese war of 1904–05; China was the ground and the lease-holder, not a principal belligerent.

2 The ladder

Five narrators who actually cover the war, ordered from thinnest agency (victory as a date, soil as a parenthesis) to thickest (named refusal, named attack, war begins in that sentence). China’s volume is in scope for late Qing history and never names the war — that zero is the chapter’s other finding, not a sixth row. Russia’s 1914–45 book, Ukraine 1945–, Germany’s NS booklet, and Sudan’s national syllabus are out of period; Kazakhstan is never a world-event column. Out-of-scope books sit in the receipts.

3 The tell

Nobody on this ladder denies that Japan beat Russia in 1904–05. The disagreement is about two sentences: the one that starts the war, and the one that says whose land the shells fell on.

India’s NCERT page is short and does a job no other column does. The war is a parenthesis on the way to the abolition of the Chinese examination system — and inside the parenthesis sits the ground: “fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory.” No Port Arthur, no Tsushima, no who fired first. Japan “defeated… Russia, a European power, in 1905.” Location is thick; combat grammar is thin. That combination is why this row opens the ladder and why the chapter’s question is omission, not body-count.

Lowe never needs Chinese soil. The war is Japan’s ticket to being “a power to be reckoned with” and “the dominant power in the Far East.” The sentence Europe is asked to remember is the status sentence: first time an Asian country defeated a European great power. The attack is absent. Port Arthur appears later, as a privilege Japan already holds when Manchuria returns in the 1930s. The United Kingdom’s interest is the new balance, not the harbor.

Belarus puts a motive where the others put a fleet. “Ruling circles” want a “small but victorious war” to strengthen the tsarist position; the government “went to war with Japan” with “lightness”; the war “turned into a catastrophe.” The agent of the decision is Russian. The agent of the battlefield result is Japan, which “inflicted defeat” and took Korea, Southern Sakhalin, and the lease of Chinese ports — named, with today’s Chinese names in brackets. The ports are spoils. The soil is not a subject. The same page wires the catastrophe to Bloody Sunday and the 1905 revolution, which is how this chapter feeds 1917.

«Per la prima volta nell’età moderna un paese asiatico batteva in un’autentica guerra una grande potenza europea…»

“For the first time in the modern age an Asian country was beating in an authentic war a great European power…”

IT · Il mondo contemporaneo · p. 236 — the supremacy sentence UK softens to “European great powers” without “white race”

Italy stages the campaign the survey books compress: Japanese initiative, no declaration of war, Port Arthur under siege for almost a year, Mukden, the Baltic fleet’s long voyage, Tsushima, Portsmouth under United States mediation. Then the sentence UK half-shares and Italy finishes: the defeat destroys not only European military myth but a presumed superiority of the “white race.” That racial vocabulary is unique in this set. The United States is blunter about the first move and cooler about the myth. Russia “refused to compromise”; Japan “attacked the Russian fleet at Port Arthur… beginning the Russo-Japanese War.” Refusal, attack, beginning — three beats, two agents, one starter. No “white race.” No Chinese examination system. The lease of Port Arthur from China is named a few lines earlier as Russia’s ice-free harbor project; the ground enters as real estate, then drops out of the war sentence.

Then there is the classroom that owns the ground. China’s 纲要 上 covers the Jiawu War of 1894–95, spheres of influence, and the Lushun (Port Arthur) leases. Search the volume for the war’s name and for the treaty town: 日俄战争, 日俄, 俄日, 朴茨茅斯, 朴次茅斯 — zero hits. 对马 appears only as a character string inside 对马克思. 旅顺 appears for earlier crises, not for 1904–05. Five of the six books whose syllabus can reach this war cover it. China’s silence, against that baseline, is an avoidance signal — not because every book must cover every war, but because the war was fought on Chinese territory over Chinese ports and the Chinese textbook never gives it a name. India, half a world away, puts the soil in parentheses. Beijing does not put the war on the page.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

NCERT (IN) earns the credit line for the finding this chapter is built on. One parenthesis — “fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory” — does more comparative work than pages of battle narrative without a ground. No other covering book says the omitted party’s name that way. Distance helps: India’s classroom is far from Port Arthur and still closer to the map of empire than Europe’s status sentence.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) is the depth lead: initiative, undeclared attack, siege, Mukden, Tsushima, Portsmouth, and the only full statement that European military myth and “white race” supremacy fell together. UK carries the Asian-beats-Europe line without the racial clause; Italy does not flinch at either half.

OpenStax (US) deserves the starter nod. “When Russia refused to compromise, Japan attacked… beginning the Russo-Japanese War” is the densest agency sentence in the set — and it is written by the country that hosted the peace, not by either belligerent’s schoolbook. Belarus earns a separate line for motive: the “small but victorious war” that was supposed to save the throne and helped break it, which is the braid into 1905 and later 1917.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is five books that cover 1904–05 as an event. It is not “the world,” and it is not six — China’s zero is in-scope avoidance, not a sixth telling. Russia’s Чубарьян volume begins in 1914; the war appears only as background to the Entente (“after the defeat in the Russo-Japanese War,” ~p. 26). That is syllabus boundary, not national silence about a defeat. Ukraine 1945–, Germany’s NS booklet, and Sudan’s regional syllabus are likewise out of period. Kazakhstan is national history and is never placed as a peer column on world events.

Machine-translated discovery files pointed to the pages. The Belarusian and Italian lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English for the reader. Before any hard claim in a later rewrite, open the printed PDF again — especially OpenStax’s “leased… in 1897,” which most handbooks date to 1898. Errors and compressions are data; they are not silently “fixed” in the quote.

None of these classrooms is a campaign history. Casualty totals for Mukden or Tsushima do not appear in this set. If you want ship lists and trench lines, every column will leave you unsatisfied — including Italy’s, which is the fullest.

Causal precision for the braid: the war did not “cause” 1917. In the books that connect them (BY, IT, UK), defeat and domestic crisis feed the 1905 revolution and the October Manifesto; 1917 is a later chapter. The empty Chinese name for the war did not cause the war either — it is a classroom fact about memory, not a battlefield cause.
