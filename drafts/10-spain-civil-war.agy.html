Spain, 1936–1939

On July 17, 1936, army officers rise in Spanish Morocco, while radio stations across Spain broadcast martial music and conflicting bulletins. Within weeks, German Ju-52 transport planes are airlifting General Franco’s legionnaires across the Strait of Gibraltar. What six national classrooms do with the three-year war that followed is the subject of this chapter.

The question · vocabulary

What do you call an intervention when only one side stops sending guns — and whose volunteers are called an army?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • February 1936: The Popular Front electoral coalition wins the Spanish general election; Manuel Azaña becomes president.
  • July 17–18, 1936: Military officers led by Generals Mola, Sanjurjo, and Franco launch a coup against the Second Spanish Republic.
  • August 1936: European powers agree to a Non-Intervention Agreement initiated by France and Great Britain. Thirty nations join, including Germany, Italy, and the USSR.
  • Foreign military support: Fascist Italy dispatches ~50,000–70,000 troops (Corpo Truppe Volontarie) and aircraft; Germany sends the Condor Legion (~5,000–12,000 personnel and combat air units); the USSR supplies weapons, armor, and Comintern-organized International Brigades (~35,000–40,000 foreign volunteers).
  • 26 April 1937: The German Condor Legion and Italian air force bomb the Basque market town of Guernica (Gernika).
  • March 1939: Republican forces surrender Madrid. General Francisco Franco establishes an authoritarian military dictatorship that lasts until his death in 1975.
  • Historical consensus estimates roughly 500,000 total casualties from battle, aerial bombardment, and political terror, followed by hundreds of thousands imprisoned or exiled.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who actually cover the war, ordered from thinnest agency (a rebellion as backdrop for alliance building) to thickest self-incrimination (a state's own live-fire test ground). Ukraine’s volume starts in 1945, China’s is national history, Sudan and India omit the war entirely; they are in the receipts, not on the ladder. Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event column here.

General rehearsal and live-fire testing

3 The tell

Every textbook on this ladder agrees that a military uprising in July 1936 turned Spain into a three-year battlefield, and that General Franco established a dictatorship that outlived the Second World War. The disagreement is about what the foreign armies were doing inside the sentence.

Belarus presents the thinnest agency on the international stage. In Koshelev’s account, the Spanish war is primarily the catalyst for European alliance formation. Franco’s army raises a rebellion against a government considered “excessively left-wing and anti-Catholic.” Germany and Italy help the nationalists, the Soviet Union supports the republicans, and the democratic states refuse aid. Out of that refusal, Hitler and Mussolini form the “Berlin—Rome Axis.” The internal war is compressed so that the diplomatic outcome—the Axis alliance—can take the stage.

Russia’s unified textbook builds a distinct symmetry. France, Britain, and the United States proclaim a “policy of non-intervention,” while Hitler and Mussolini back the rebels with air strikes, arms, and a “125-thousand-strong Italian expeditionary corps.” But Chubaryan does not leave the Republican side as a simple victim: the Republican rear is “engulfed by a kind of second civil war” between communists and anarchists, and the war ends as “a struggle between two Spanish authoritarian regimes.” That symmetry—two authoritarian regimes locking horns while external powers exploit the field—allows the textbook to record Franco’s post-war terror (0.5 to 2 million political prisoners) without elevating the Republic into a democratic ideal.

Lowe’s UK narrative turns to numbers and neutral obligations. Britain and France give an undertaking to remain neutral and are the only powers that keep it. Hitler sends tanks and Luftwaffe units to practice bombing undefended targets; Mussolini provides 70,000 troops. Crucially, Lowe is the only narrator to insert historian Hugh Thomas’s mutual atrocity tallies: 75,000 murdered by Nationalists, 55,000 by Republicans. Guernica appears with a precise toll—1,600 civilians killed by a hundred Condor Legion bombers—paired with 150,000 post-war Nationalist executions.

«Mussolini inviò in Spagna un contingente di 50.000 "volontari" (ma si trattava in realtà di reparti regolari)…»

“Mussolini sent to Spain a contingent of 50,000 ‘volunteers’ (but they were in reality regular units)…”

IT · Sabbatucci–Vidotto, Il mondo contemporaneo (2004) · p. 413

Italy’s textbook engages in direct grammatical self-scrutiny. Sabbatucci & Vidotto put Mussolini’s “volunteers” inside scare quotes, immediately correcting the official fascist vocabulary: ma si trattava in realtà di reparti regolari (“but they were in reality regular units”). Non-intervention is framed as a diplomatic illusion that concealed British sympathy for Franco and French fear of open conflict. Italy leaves the war with half a million dead, 300,000 political exiles, and an explicit warning: Spain prefigured the methods of the coming world war—urban carpet bombing, reprisal killings, and roundups.

OpenStax in the United States highlights the ideological architecture of Francoist terror. While European powers pursue nonintervention and the U.S. enforces neutrality laws, Spanish fascists claim Catholic Spain is threatened by a “Jewish-Bolshevik-Masonic conspiracy against property and social order.” That specific ideological phrasing is load-bearing: OpenStax shows how a conspiracy theory was deployed to justify the “liquidation of hundreds of thousands of Republicans.”

Germany’s federal booklet operates at the highest level of self-incrimination. The NS regime does not merely assist Franco; Hitler uses the uprising to create an “international battlefield against ‘Bolshevism.’” The booklet excerpts Hermann Göring’s testimony at the Nuremberg trials: Franco sent a cry for help, and Göring dispatched transport fleets, fighters, bombers, and anti-aircraft guns to test under live fire whether the equipment had been appropriately developed. Spain becomes a Generalprobe—a military general rehearsal—and Guernica becomes the symbol of modern air warfare directed against civilians.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

bpb (DE) earns the credit line for this chapter. By citing Hermann Göring’s own Nuremberg testimony, the German booklet explicitly documents how its own state used Spain as a live-fire testing ground for weapons and air tactics later turned against European cities. It does not soften the Condor Legion's bombing of Guernica into an unintended collateral event.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns a close second for linguistic candor: putting Mussolini’s 50,000 “volunteers” in scare quotes and identifying them as regular army units is an exemplary self-correction of national historiographical spin.

OpenStax (US) deserves recognition for naming the ideological mechanism—“Jewish-Bolshevik-Masonic conspiracy”—used by Franco’s forces to justify mass liquidation of political opponents.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder reflects the six books in our corpus that cover the Spanish Civil War. It does not represent every textbook in the world. Ukraine’s secondary syllabus begins in 1945 (Franco appears only in post-1975 democratization lessons); China’s standard textbook covers domestic Chinese history; Kazakhstan’s volume is a national-history syllabus, reserved for signature republic topics rather than world-history event comparisons.

Two in-scope textbooks omit the Spanish Civil War entirely: India’s NCERT volume and Sudan’s Ministry of Education textbook. Because six of eight in-scope narrators cover the event, these two zeros represent genuine curriculum avoidance signals under our baseline rule.

Casualty counts vary significantly across sources—from Lowe’s 500,000 war dead and 150,000 post-war executions to Chubaryan’s range of 0.5 to 2 million political prisoners. These discrepancies reflect different counting metrics (battlefield vs prison mortality vs long-term repression) rather than simple factual contradiction.
