The Great Depression

On 24 October 1929 — "Black Thursday" — thirteen million shares change hands on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange as ticker machines run hours behind. Within three years, world trade shrinks by more than half, factories shut across three continents, and over thirty million people are out of work. What eight school systems do with that collapse is the subject of this chapter.

The question · causes

What caused the Great Depression — a structural breakdown of market capitalism, or a series of policy errors by states?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 24 October ("Black Thursday") and 29 October 1929 ("Black Tuesday"): Stock market collapse on Wall Street marks the beginning of the Great Depression.
  • 1929–1932: Global economic contraction. Industrial production falls by approximately 50% in the United States and Germany; world trade drops by over 60% in value.
  • Unemployment surges worldwide: ~13–14 million in the United States, ~6 million in Germany, ~3 million in Great Britain by 1932–1933.
  • June 1930: The US passes the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, raising duties on over 20,000 imported goods; foreign nations retaliate with tariff barriers.
  • Banking crises: Over 9,000 banks suspend operations in the United States between 1930 and 1933. Britain abandons the Gold Standard in September 1931.
  • Political consequences: Franklin D. Roosevelt elected US president in November 1932, launching the "New Deal" in 1933. Economic distress accelerates Adolf Hitler's rise to power in Germany (1933) and fuels militarist expansion in Japan (invasion of Manchuria, September 1931).

3 The tell

Every textbook covering this decade agrees that in October 1929 stock prices in New York crashed, and that years of global hardship followed. The disagreement is over who or what holds the active verb when the system breaks down.

Across the top of the ladder, the crash onset is strictly agentless. In India's NCERT, Wall Street "crashes" on a timeline, and Great Depression "follows." In China's state textbook, an economic crisis "broke out" in the autumn of 1929, as if it were a sudden thunderstorm. In Russia's unified course, an "unexpected catastrophic fall in the value of shares occurred." In Belarus, "a crash occurred on the New York Stock Exchange — a landslide fall." Even in the American and British market leaders, OpenStax notes that "no economic depression is caused by one element alone," while Norman Lowe insists the Wall Street crash "did not cause the depression; it was just a symptom." The market does not get pushed over by a villain; it collapses under its own weight.

Where the books divide sharply is in diagnosing the structural flaw. The Russian textbook locates the disaster in capitalist production mechanics: a "serious disturbance (disbalance) in the economy, and above all in the chain production – distribution – consumption." Belarus agrees, noting that technology expanded production faster than wages could buy goods, and poses the explicit exercise question to Belarusian students: "Why did it not affect the economy of the Soviet state?" Italy's university textbook places moral blame on American foreign policy, charging that the United States, "rather than assuming the responsibilities connected to the role of hegemonic power," retreated behind high protectionist walls and spread ruin to its debtors.

«Западные демократии, впервые став на путь государственного регулирования экономической и социальной жизни, вышли из кризиса…»

“Western democracies, having for the first time stepped onto the path of state regulation of economic and social life, emerged from the crisis…”

BY · Кошелев и др. (2021) · p. 125 — emergency state regulation without naming Franklin D. Roosevelt

Recovery agency brings out another grammatical divide: the missing president. In the US, UK, Italian, and Russian narratives, recovery is personified by Franklin D. Roosevelt, who sweeps into office promising a "new deal for the forgotten man," while Herbert Hoover is faulted for remaining "inactive" in the conviction that prosperity was "somewhere around the corner." But Belarus removes FDR and the proper noun "New Deal" entirely from its core crisis section. Instead, recovery is attributed to an abstract collective subject: "Western democracies, having for the first time stepped onto the path of state regulation." In the Belarusian classroom, it is not an charismatic American leader who saves the system, but the objective necessity of state intervention overriding laissez-faire market doctrine.

Finally, non-Western syllabuses demonstrate what the Great Depression is for in different national histories. In China, the 1929 crash is assigned exactly one sentence on page 154: it is not a lesson in macroeconomics, but the external catalyst that plunged Japan into domestic crisis, driving its military leaders to launch the invasion of China at Shenyang on 18 September 1931. In Sudan, the crisis is experienced through the lens of colonial wage exploitation. As global demand for Sudanese cotton collapsed, the British administration slashed graduate salaries from eight pounds to five and a half — while explicitly exempting British and foreign employees. That racialized pay cut provoked the 1931 Gordon College student strike, turning a Wall Street crash into the incubator of Sudan's anticolonial student movement.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

OpenStax (US) earns the credit line for structural candor. It admits that 80 percent of American families had no savings when the crisis struck, details how the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act stifled international trade, and explains the specific regulatory mechanisms — such as the FDIC and Social Security — created to prevent future collapse. Distance from the era allows it to present a thorough multi-causal breakdown.

MoE Sudan (SD) earns a secondary credit line for capturing the colonial ripple effect. While Western textbooks treat the Depression as an affair between North American stockbrokers and European central banks, Sudan's textbook demonstrates how global commodity price crashes were passed down to colonized subjects through unequal wage cuts, directly linking world economic crisis to local resistance.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) deserves recognition for international systemic framing. It clearly states that America's retreat into isolationist tariffs and failure to act as a hegemonic stabilizer turned a domestic panic into a global catastrophe.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder compares eight textbooks whose scope covers the 1929–1933 period. It does not represent every classroom on Earth. Ukraine's volume covers 1945 to 2018 (referencing the Great Depression only on page 46 as a benchmark for 2010 financial reforms); Germany's volume is a specialized National Socialism primer; Kazakhstan's volume is a national history course. Those absences reflect curriculum boundaries, not national avoidance.

Machine-translated discovery files guided our search, but all quotations from Russian, Belorussian, Italian, Chinese, and English sources have been verified against native extractions from original PDF texts. Belarus's omission of the term "New Deal" in its crisis narrative is a notable structural feature of its curriculum design.

The Sudanese quotations carry specific caveats. Because PDF extractions of Arabic text from this volume suffer from ligature distortion, all Arabic lines have been reconstructed to standard orthography and marked with `` for verification against the printed book. Page numbers for Sudan correspond to printed page numbers.

Finally, none of these secondary school textbooks provides a full technical macroeconomic debate. Monetarist arguments regarding money supply contraction, Austrian capital theory, and modern econometric revisions are condensed into broad narrative themes accessible to teenagers.
