1917

On the night of 25 October 1917 — 7 November on the calendar you use — armed men in Petrograd take the telegraph offices, the railway station, the bridges and the power station, and then walk into the Winter Palace and arrest the ministers sitting round a table inside. The streetcars are running while they do it. The theatres are open, and so are the restaurants. Deaths at the palace that night are counted on one hand. Almost nobody carries that picture. The picture almost everybody carries — a vast crowd pouring over iron gates in the dark, under fire — is a real picture, and it was made later, on purpose, twice.

Eight textbooks reach that night. Five of them narrate it. Between the quiet event and the loud image there is a gap, and every one of those five fills the gap with a noun.

The question · naming

Revolution, insurrection, or coup — who does your textbook's noun put in control of that night?

1 The boring facts

One calendar rule first, because it decides how everything below is dated. Russia was still on the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind the Gregorian one used almost everywhere else; that is why the October Revolution happens in November on the calendar in your phone. Every date here is printed Julian first, Gregorian second.

  • 23 February / 8 March 1917: bread shortages and striking workers in Petrograd — the day is International Women's Day — grow into mass garrison mutinies. The Tsar orders force, then loses the garrison.
  • 2 / 15 March 1917: Nicholas II abdicates, ending three centuries of Romanov rule.
  • Dual power (dvoevlastie) is established between the Provisional Government — led first by Prince Lvov, then by Alexander Kerensky — and the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies.
  • 3 / 16 April 1917: Lenin returns from exile across Germany and publishes the "April Theses" — all power to the Soviets, out of the war.
  • 3–7 / 16–20 July 1917: the "July Days" demonstrations fail; the Provisional Government suppresses the Bolsheviks and Lenin goes into hiding in Finland.
  • 27–30 August / 9–12 September 1917: General Kornilov's march on Petrograd collapses after Kerensky releases and arms Bolshevik factory workers as Red Guards.
  • 24–25 October / 6–7 November 1917: Red Guards and pro-Bolshevik troops take the key installations of the capital and then the Winter Palace, arresting the Provisional Government's ministers. The cruiser Aurora fires a blank round as a signal.
  • 12 / 25 November 1917: elections to the Constituent Assembly give the Socialist Revolutionaries the largest share and the Bolsheviks about a quarter. The Assembly meets in January 1918 and is dispersed after one day.
  • 3 March 1918 (both calendars now the same): Soviet Russia signs the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, ceding large western territories to leave the war.

2 The ladder

Eight narrators, in two groups that are answering two different questions. Five of them tell you what happened in Petrograd; three of them never enter Petrograd at all and tell you what 1917 did somewhere else. Splitting them is not a ranking. It is the finding: the second group is not giving a low-agency account of the night, it is giving no account of the night, because the night is not what 1917 is for in those classrooms. Ukraine's volume begins in 1945 and Germany's is a National Socialism booklet; both are in the receipts. Kazakhstan's book is a national history and is never a world-event column here, though what it does with 1917 is worth the receipt it gets.

3 The tell

Put the two loudest sentences side by side. Minsk: “The October Revolution of 1917 became the most important event in world history.” London: “It was almost a bloodless coup.” They are describing the same hours, and neither is exaggerating its own evidence — the Belarusian book says on the page before that the Red Guards met no resistance, and Lowe says the plans “went off without a hitch,” which is a compliment to the planners and a demotion of everyone else. The gap between “most important event in world history” and “almost a bloodless coup” is not a gap about facts. It is the gap this chapter is about: an event too small to look like its own consequences, and five classrooms choosing a noun to bridge it.

Each noun hands the steering wheel to somebody different. Revolution gives it to a population: something a society does. Insurrection gives it to an organised minority acting against a standing authority — an operation with a commander, which is why the Italian book's very next move is to name one. Coup gives it to a small group at the top and, by implication, takes it away from everyone else in the country; a coup is something done to a state, not by a people. Three nouns, one night, and the difference between them is the whole argument about what the Soviet state was allowed to claim afterwards.

Russia's volume declines the choice by not making October a separate event. February and October sit under one numbered heading — “The Great Russian Revolution of 1917” — so there is no second thing to name. The syntax then does something more precise than hiding: the actors are on the page, but never in the driver's seat of the sentence. The Provisional Government is the grammatical subject of its own overthrow; the left-radical socialists arrive in a by-phrase; the transfer of power to the Soviets is not taken, it “was proclaimed,” with nobody proclaiming it; and in the sentence that makes Lenin head of government, the office comes first and the man arrives at the end, after his job title. Read it twice and you can name everyone involved. Read it once, at the speed a student reads, and what you remember is a government to which things happened.

Belarus does the opposite and does more work. Its § 14 runs the whole year as a teaching sequence: the bread supply fails, dual power is defined and diagrammed, the Bolsheviks take the installations without resistance, the Constituent Assembly results are printed as numbers — Socialist Revolutionaries 40.4 per cent, Bolsheviks 24 per cent, liberals 7.5 per cent — and the section closes on a world-historical verdict and Bernard Shaw's admiration for the Soviet system after his 1931 visit. Notice what that sequence has to do. It prints an election in which the Bolsheviks came second, and it concludes on the same page that the October Revolution “reflected the interests of broad strata of the population.” The evidence and the verdict are four lines apart, and a student is expected to hold both.

Italy and the two Anglophone books change register from history to operations. Sabbatucci–Vidotto's Trotsky is “the organizer and the military mind”; Lowe's Trotsky “made most of the plans, which went off without a hitch”; OpenStax's Lenin “led a coup and seized power from the other political factions in Petrograd” — from the other factions, not from the country, which is a smaller and more accurate claim than it looks. The American book alone attaches a foreign hand to the story before the night: “Germany helped him return.” No book on this ladder denies any of the others' facts. They are choosing which verb the facts hang from.

And then there are the three books that never come to Petrograd. In Khartoum the revolution is an impact “calling for freedom and calling for struggle and liberation,” one item in a list of things that pushed the Sudanese national movement into a new phase; it has no city, no date beyond the year, no Lenin. In Beijing it is a timestamp on the arrival of an idea: Marxism spreads in China “after the October Revolution,” Beijing and Shanghai become the centres, and three years later Comintern representatives sit down with Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao. In Delhi it is a cell in a timeline and, forty-one pages away, a “Russian success” that “exercised a powerful influence around the world.” These are not avoidance: all three books cover 1917, and cover it as the thing it did to them. What none of the three needs is the night itself. The image of the revolution travelled to places the event never reached — which is the subject of the next section, and it is also, for three of these eight classrooms, the entire subject.

4 The picture

One book on this ladder notices, mid-narration, that the thing it is describing is going to become a picture — and says so in the future tense, inside the same sentence in which it says the thing was almost harmless.

«L'assalto al Palazzo d'Inverno — destinato ad assurgere a episodio-simbolo della rivoluzione, come era stata la presa della Bastiglia nel 1789 — fu praticamente incruento: pochissime furono le vittime nei rari e confusi scontri che ebbero luogo nei corridoi e nei saloni dell'antica reggia.»

“The assault on the Winter Palace — destined to rise to the status of episode-symbol of the revolution, as the taking of the Bastille had been in 1789 — was practically bloodless: very few were the victims in the rare and confused clashes that took place in the corridors and the halls of the old royal palace.”

IT · Il mondo contemporaneo (Laterza 2004) · p. 306

Start with what the five books actually put in the palace. Lowe: telegraph offices, the railway station, and then the Winter Palace surrounded. Belarus: railway stations, bridges, telegraph, power station. Sabbatucci–Vidotto: the palace surrounded and isolated and taken that same evening, against “heterogeneous and demoralised units” charged with defending it. This is a checklist, executed by a few thousand people, in a city of over two million. The defenders were cadets and a women's battalion; the ministers were arrested in a dining room; the Aurora's famous shot was a blank. Outside, the streetcars ran. That is the whole difficulty. Nothing in that description looks like the founding of a state that would organise a third of the planet's politics for seventy years, and everyone involved knew it within a year.

So the picture was built, in public, at anniversaries. On 7 November 1920, the third anniversary, the director Nikolai Evreinov staged The Storming of the Winter Palace on Palace Square itself — around eight thousand performers, many of them Red Army soldiers, an audience estimated at a hundred thousand, searchlights, artillery sound, the Aurora in the river. Then, for the tenth anniversary, the state commissioned Sergei Eisenstein to film it. October was shot in 1927 in and around the real palace, with the real square, and released in 1928; its storming sequence — the crowd swarming the wrought-iron gates, pouring through the courtyard, flooding the staterooms — is the sequence in your head. Frames from it have been reprinted and rebroadcast ever since as though a camera had been present in 1917. No camera was.

Be exact about what that produced, because the punchy version is wrong. The re-enactment and the film did not make the revolution, and they did not make it succeed; the Bolsheviks had held power for three years before the first crowd was assembled on Palace Square and for ten before the film. What the anniversaries produced was the evidence — the visual record a small night did not generate for itself, manufactured afterwards at the scale the outcome seemed to require, and then absorbed into the general stock of things everybody has seen. The event was over. The picture was the part still being decided.

Where the classrooms meet Eisenstein at all, they meet him in the chapter on culture: a name beside Pudovkin in the Soviet school of cinema, a still from Battleship Potemkin, “the first great films” in a paragraph on the avant-garde, a title under Stalin and the arts forty-five pages after the same book's account of the night. Only one classroom names October, and not one connects it to the picture its students already have. Nobody is hiding anything. The night is in chapter 16 and the camera is in chapter 17, and no classroom is built to put them on the same page.

Which is where you come in, holding a phone. The thing that has not changed since 1917 is that a building being entered generates a few seconds of footage and no meaning at all, and the meaning arrives separately, later, attached. Every year somewhere in the world there is film of a government building with people going into it, and the caption is chosen within hours from the same three words these classrooms are still arguing over: revolution, insurrection, coup. The picture is never neutral and it is almost never sufficient; the noun is doing the work, and the noun is chosen by whoever ends up in a position to choose it. That is not a lesson about the past. It is the mechanism, running, with better cameras.

The picture then travelled further than the night ever did. In Khartoum the revolution is an impulse toward liberation; in Beijing it is the year Marxism began to spread; in Delhi it is a Russian success that influenced the world. Within fifteen months the government made on that quiet night was the one power not invited to Paris — which is chapter 6 — and in Beijing it becomes the origin story of a party that will spend the next three decades fighting for China, which is chapter 7.

So go back to the streetcar. It is the most reliable detail in this chapter and the one no textbook prints: on the night the Provisional Government fell, the trams of Petrograd kept to their routes. The men taking the city had the power station on their checklist and the current stayed on. Eleven years later somebody built a better version on the real square, with eight thousand extras, and that is the version you have. The night underneath it was the size of an ordinary evening — theatres open, restaurants open, the trams running to timetable while the ministers were arrested indoors. Whatever you decide to call it, that is the traffic it happened in.

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Koshelev (BY) takes it, from the column with the strongest verdict in the corpus, and takes it for what is printed after the verdict. Two lines below “The October Revolution of 1917 became the most important event in world history,” on the same page, the book sets its class this exercise: «Почему это событие описывают и оценивают разные люди совершенно по-разному?» — “Why do different people describe and evaluate this event completely differently?” A boxed note on the same page then tells students that 7 November was the state holiday of the USSR, that the Russian Federation has marked 4 November instead since 2005, that Russian media and the public are of two minds about the change, and asks: «Как вы считаете, является ли день 7 ноября достойным памяти?» — “In your view, is the day of 7 November worthy of memory?” A pupil can see both of those on one page: a verdict, and the book's own admission that the verdict is contested and that the calendar has already moved. Whatever else this column is doing, it hands a fifteen-year-old the question this book is made of, and does not answer it for them.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight pinned editions, not eight nations, and the eight are not doing the same job. Five narrate 1917 internally; three carry the year in a sentence or a timeline cell, and putting Sudan's two lines beside Belarus's six-page section as if they were rival accounts of the same night would be a category error. That is why they are in separate movements above.

Two volumes are out of chronological scope: Ukraine's 11th-grade textbook covers 1945–2018, and Germany's bpb booklet covers National Socialism and the war. Those zeros are syllabus boundaries; their search stems are in the receipts.

Kazakhstan's 11th-grade История Казахстана is a national history and is never a peer column in a world-event ladder in this book. It is the most interesting non-column in this chapter and its material is in the receipts.

The picture section is the part of this chapter that is not built from the corpus. The 1920 re-enactment and the 1928 film are general history, marked for checking, and the claim made about them is deliberately narrow: they produced the surviving imagery, not the outcome. How much of any individual reader's mental image comes from Eisenstein rather than from illustrations, later films or nothing in particular is not measurable, and this chapter does not pretend to measure it.

Kept verbatim, because errors are data: NCERT dates the founding of the Comintern to March 1918. The Third International was founded in Moscow in March 1919. And the Sudanese passage is reconstructed from an Arabic extraction whose ligatures do not survive machine reading; its printed page must be read against the PDF before this chapter ships.

Page conventions differ by book and are cited as found. Russian and Belarusian printed pages are one less than the extraction's; Chinese printed pages are four less; NCERT's are twelve less; the OpenStax extraction's printed numbers are one less than its file numbers. The Lowe extraction carries no printed page numbers, so PDF pages are given with the section number. Italian numbers are the extraction's own.

Однако большевики, провозгласив свободу и равенство всех наций и народов, в то же время призывали оставаться под знаменами Октября.» — “The colonial peoples of Russia expected national liberation from the October Revolution. However the Bolsheviks, having proclaimed the freedom and equality of all nations and peoples, at the same time called on them to remain under the banners of October.” And on p. 100 it quotes Mustafa Shokay at the Turkestan congress of 27 November 1917: «Россия и Туркестан, пережившие Октябрьский кровавый переворот, вольны сами решать свою судьбу» — “Russia and Turkestan, having lived through the October bloody coup, are free to decide their own fate.” Alash and Turkestan autonomy and the city-by-city establishment of Soviet power from October 1917 to March 1918 occupy pp. 92–96.
