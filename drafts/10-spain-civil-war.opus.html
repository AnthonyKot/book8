Spain, 1936–1939

On Monday 26 April 1937 it is market day in Guernica, a Basque town of a few thousand people about thirty kilometres east of Bilbao. Aircraft appear over the centre in the late afternoon and keep coming, in waves, for something over three hours. Six of the eleven classrooms in this book teach this war. Three of the six print the town’s name. Two print the name of the unit that flew. One prints the words of the man who sent it.

The question · vocabulary

When foreign aircraft bomb a town in somebody else’s civil war, how much of that act does your classroom give a name to?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • February 1936: a Popular Front coalition wins the Spanish general election. On 17–18 July 1936 a section of the army rises against the government, beginning in Spanish Morocco and spreading to the mainland.
  • From late July 1936, German Junkers Ju 52 and Italian Savoia-Marchetti transports carry the Army of Africa across the Strait of Gibraltar to southern Spain.
  • The Non-Intervention Agreement is proposed by France in August 1936; a Non-Intervention Committee begins meeting in London on 9 September 1936. Germany, Italy and the USSR adhere to it and all three supply the war.
  • The Condor Legion, a German air unit under Spanish nominal command, operates in Spain from November 1936 to 1939; around 19,000 German personnel pass through it in rotation. Italy’s Corpo Truppe Volontarie peaks at roughly 50,000 men.
  • Roughly 35,000–40,000 foreign volunteers from more than fifty countries serve in the International Brigades, recruited largely through the Comintern; they are withdrawn in the autumn of 1938.
  • 26 April 1937: Guernica is bombed. Death tolls have been contested since the week it happened — the Basque government said 1,654; later research, including Hugh Thomas’s, gives figures in the low hundreds.
  • Madrid surrenders on 28 March 1939; Franco announces the end of the war on 1 April 1939. He governs Spain until his death on 20 November 1975.
  • Total dead are usually given as around half a million, including deaths from repression after the fighting stopped; the composition of that figure is still argued about. Nobody disputes that both sides killed prisoners.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who cover the war, ordered from the least of the act named to the most: from a classroom where nothing is dropped on anything, to a classroom that prints the unit, the date, the Basque spelling of the town, and the sworn testimony of the man who signed the order. Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945 and China’s is a national history; they are in the receipts, with the two in-scope books that carry nothing. Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event column here.

3 The tell

Nothing on this ladder is in dispute. Germany and Italy armed and reinforced Franco; the Soviet Union armed the Republic; Britain and France stayed out and said so; the rising began in July 1936 and the Republic lost in March 1939. All six books say all of that. What separates them is how many proper nouns they are willing to spend on one afternoon in April 1937.

Belarus spends none. Koshelev’s war is two verbs in one sentence — Italy and Germany helped the nationalists, the USSR supported the republicans — and the symmetry is the point: two aids, matched, neither of them carrying a cargo. No aircraft, no bombs, no town, no dead. The rebellion itself is raised by “part of the Spanish army,” and the rebels are allowed to explain themselves in the same sentence, as men who considered the government “excessively left-wing and anti-Catholic.” What the Spanish war produces in this book is not a Spanish outcome at all: Hitler and Mussolini, «видя фактический отказ демократических государств помогать испанскому правительству» — “seeing the factual refusal of democratic states to help the Spanish government” — form the Berlin–Rome Axis. The war is a hinge in somebody else’s diplomacy. The only picture it gets is the victory parade.

Russia puts the aircraft in the air and never lands them. Chubaryan writes that Germany and Italy supported the rebels «ударами с воздуха» — with strikes from the air — and then names an Italian corps, a battle at Guadalajara, a battle on the Ebro. It is a real military narrative. What it has no room for is a target. Search the whole volume for Герника and there are two hits: a photo credit, and a caption on an unnumbered colour plate at the back — Pablo Picasso. Guernica. 1937. Queen Sofía Museum. Madrid. The bombing is not in the book; the painting of the bombing is, filed a hundred and twenty pages later among Cézanne, Mondrian and Dalí. In this classroom Guernica is a museum address.

Italy writes four pages, and they are the best four pages in the corpus on why Spaniards fought Spaniards — the landowners holding “over 40% of the cultivated lands,” the anarcho-syndicalist union, the failed coup of ’32, the rising of ’34. Sabbatucci and Vidotto also give the sharpest available sentence on what the foreign air forces were doing: Germany “sent above all aircraft and pilots and made use of the war to test the efficiency of its air force.” They then write, at the end, that Spain is where “the bombing of inhabited centres, reprisals, round-ups” were adopted “for the first time.” The method is named, the purpose is named, the precedent is named. The place is not. Guernica, Gernika and Legion Condor do not occur anywhere in the manual — and Italy is one of the two countries whose air forces were flying.

OpenStax names the town and doubts the sentence it is in. The construction is worth reading slowly: “Though it is debatable how militarily effective the German and Italian bombing of civilian targets like Madrid and Guernica was, it did cause people to shudder at the increased horror of warfare.” The bombing is a noun, not a verb; its subject is a compound adjective; the main clause is about a shudder. There is no unit, no date, no toll. Two sentences later the reader gets a photograph — not of the town in 1937, but of a mural in the modern town reproducing Picasso’s painting, with a caption noting that the mural’s title asks Madrid to give the painting back.

Lowe gives all four coordinates in one sentence — a hundred bombers, the German Condor Legion, the Basque market town, 1600 people — and adds the clause that explains what an air force was doing over a market: Hitler “allowed his air force to practice bombing undefended civilian targets.” That verb is the whole rehearsal thesis, in a school textbook, in passing. Lowe is also the book where a familiar term goes missing. “Non-intervention” appears three times in Mastering Modern World History — every one of them about American policy toward Latin America. For Spain he writes that France and Britain “refused to intervene,” and that all the powers “had given an undertaking to remain neutral, but only Britain and France kept the agreement.” The Non-Intervention Committee met in London under a British chairman, and the phrase it was named after never reaches the Spanish page.

«Guernica hatte keine Flak, überhaupt gab es keine Geschütze in der Stadt, nicht einmal ein Maschinengewehr wäre zu finden gewesen.»

“Guernica had no anti-aircraft guns; there were no guns in the town at all, not even a machine gun was to be found.”

DE · Padre Alberto de Onaindía, eyewitness account printed in full column in bpb Nr. 316 (2012), p. 8

The German booklet is the only one that treats the raid as a thing somebody decided to do. Hitler decides during the Olympics; the delivery is made “despite international agreements to preserve neutrality”; the Condor Legion is dispatched “secretly”; the war “was to become a testing field.” Then the page splits in two, under a pair of headings that do this chapter’s work in seven words — „Proben“ für die einen … Ernstfall für die anderen, “‘Trials’ for the one side … the real thing for the other.” The left column is Göring at Nuremberg explaining that he rotated crews through Spain so the personnel would also gain experience. The right column is a Basque priest counting waves of Junkers and, afterwards, the diameter of the craters. Perpetrator and witness, printed side by side, sourced, at school-textbook length.

Line the six up and the order does not track guilt, or distance, or ideology. The two states whose aircraft flew sit at opposite ends of the ladder: Germany prints the town eleven times in an eighty-four-page booklet, and Italy prints it zero times in a manual of some seven hundred and fifty. The two books furthest from Spain in every direction — Minsk and Moscow — are the two that keep the aeroplanes permanently airborne. If the ladder is re-sorted by agency rather than by naming, one column moves: Italy jumps two rungs, because “made use of the war to test the efficiency of its air force” is a thicker sentence about intention than anything OpenStax or Lowe writes. Italy will tell you exactly what the intervention was for. It will not tell you where it landed.

The same national reflex runs through the word volunteer, and it is a reliable instrument: the scare quotes go on the other side’s people. Except in Italy, where they go on Italy’s own. Sabbatucci and Vidotto write that Mussolini sent 50,000 «volontari» (ma si trattava in realtà di reparti regolari) — “‘volunteers’ (but in reality they were regular units)” — a textbook dismantling its own state’s euphemism in a parenthesis. Russia’s are «интернациональные бригады добровольцев из разных стран (в том числе из Франции, Великобритании, США и др.)», “international brigades of volunteers from various countries (including from France, Great Britain, the USA, etc.)”; the list of countries does not include the USSR, which appears one sentence earlier not as volunteers but as a government that “supported” the Popular Front. Lowe’s 40,000 volunteers from over fifty nations arrive with a handler attached: they “were organized and deployed by the Russians, working from a base in Paris.” OpenStax has no volunteers at all — the roughly 2,800 Americans who went to Spain are not in the American textbook. And Italy prints the one detail that costs it something: at Guadalajara in March 1937 «gli italiani della Brigata Garibaldi inflissero una dura sconfitta ai loro connazionali inquadrati nei reparti fascisti» — “the Italians of the Garibaldi Brigade inflicted a harsh defeat on their compatriots enrolled in the fascist units.” Russia narrates the same battle with the Italian corps as the object and “the republican army” as the subject. Same field, same month; one book counts Italians on both sides, the other counts one.

One last thing the naming does, outside the Spanish chapter entirely. The two books that name Guernica best both reach for it again later, in the argument about the Allied bombing of German cities — and they reach for it from opposite sides of the same ledger. Lowe quotes a peace campaigner conceding the raids “probably were war crimes, but that the Nazis themselves were the first to begin bombing innocent civilians in Guernica (during the Spanish Civil War), Warsaw and Rotterdam.” The German booklet, describing the destruction of German cities from 1942, writes that this happened «ebenso wie deutsche Luftangriffe zuvor Städte wie Guernica, Coventry, Warschau, Belgrad, Leningrad, Minsk und Rotterdam zerstört hatten» — “just as German air raids had earlier destroyed cities like Guernica, Coventry, Warsaw, Belgrade, Leningrad, Minsk and Rotterdam.” One classroom uses the town to answer a charge; the other uses it to accept one. Both put it first in the list. A place-name that three of these six books can manage without turns out, in the two that keep it, to be load-bearing a whole war later.

4 The thread

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

bpb (DE) earns the credit line, and earns it the hard way. This is a booklet published by a German federal agency, about German history, and its account of Spain is built out of two documents that a national narrator has every incentive to leave out: the commander-in-chief of its own air force explaining to a war-crimes tribunal that he rotated crews through a foreign civil war so that “the personnel would also gain a certain experience,” and a foreign priest describing what that felt like from underneath. Naming the unit is one thing; giving the victim equal column width, and letting the perpetrator convict himself in his own words, is another. Note also which sentence it does not write. It does not say the Spanish war caused anything. It says the war was used — „militärische Generalprobe“, a military dress rehearsal — which is a claim about intent and equipment, not a claim that Guernica made 1939 happen.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) is the depth lead and the self-incrimination lead at once, which is unusual, and it is why the missing town stings rather than merely registering. Four pages on the Spanish causes of a Spanish war, the aristocracy’s 40 per cent of the cultivated land, the anarchists and the communists shooting at each other in Barcelona, the POUM “liquidated also with the intervention of Soviet agents” — and, on Italy itself, no euphemism left standing: “volunteers” corrected to regular units, Guadalajara reported as Italians beating Italians, and the fascist intervention called massive. A book that hard on its own state has bought the right to be read closely, including where it goes quiet.

Чубарьян (RU) takes a narrower credit, for a paragraph that a 2023 Russian state-approved volume did not have to print. It tells the reader that «Идея Народного фронта, поддержанная VII конгрессом Коминтерна, на практике оказалась борьбой компартии за оттеснение политических соперников» — “the idea of the Popular Front, supported by the VII Congress of the Comintern, in practice turned out to be the Communist Party’s struggle to push aside its political rivals” — and that «Тыл республиканской Испании был охвачен своего рода второй гражданской войной, где коммунисты боролись против анархистов, троцкистов, а порой и против социалистов», “the rear of republican Spain was engulfed by a kind of second civil war, where the communists fought against anarchists, Trotskyists, and at times against socialists,” and that this weakened the Republic’s capacity to resist. It is the only book on the ladder that makes the Soviet-aligned side of the war a subject with a cost attached, in a chapter whose other sentences are firmly on that side.

Lowe (UK) deserves the practical nod. It is the only column that gives a reader all four coordinates of the raid in one sentence, the only one to write down that a foreign air force was there to practise, and the only one that troubles to say the intervention probably decided the outcome — “the extent of foreign help for the Nationalists was probably decisive.”

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is six pinned editions, not six countries and not “the world.” Ukraine’s volume covers 1945–2018 and China’s is a national history of China; those zeros are syllabus boundaries. Two in-scope books carry nothing: Sudan’s ministry secondary history and India’s NCERT Themes in World History. By this book’s own rule of thumb — a zero counts as an avoidance signal only when most of the eleven cover a topic — six of eleven is at the edge of the threshold and does not clear it, so no avoidance is claimed here in either direction. Both zeros also have ordinary explanations: the Sudanese volume is a national history whose interwar chapters are about the Gezira scheme and the Graduates’ Congress, and the rationalised NCERT volume runs from antiquity to the modernisation of Japan and China without an interwar-Europe unit at all. Kazakhstan is not a column here; its book is national history.

Kept verbatim because errors are data, and because this chapter is about names. Lowe prints “Generals Mola and Sanjurjos” for the two conspirators — the second is José Sanjurjo, without the s — and calls the Falange leader “José Antonio de Rivera (Primo’s son),” splitting a compound surname so that the family name reads as de Rivera rather than Primo de Rivera. Neither wobble changes an argument. Both are worth a second look on the printed page.

A second keep, on numbers, and this one does change something. Lowe’s Guernica kills “1600 people”; the German booklet says „mehrere Hundert“, several hundred. 1,654 was the figure the Basque government issued in 1937 and it has been argued over ever since; the lower estimates come from later archival work, including by the historian Lowe cites elsewhere in the same section. Two textbooks, one raid, an order of magnitude apart, and neither tells the student the number is disputed. Compare the Italian troop counts on the same three pages of the same three books: Russia’s 125,000, Britain’s 70,000, Italy’s own 50,000. The last of those is the smallest, and it is the one printed in Rome.

A third keep, milder. Belarus mentions the war’s foreign volunteers only in its culture chapter, where “the civil war in Spain caused the strengthening of the political influence of the intelligentsia throughout the world” and “in the late 1930s J. Steinbeck, E. Hemingway, L. Aragon became front-line correspondents and took part in military actions.” Hemingway and Aragon were in Spain. Steinbeck was writing The Grapes of Wrath, and became a war correspondent in 1943. Read against the printed page before it is called anything.

Finally, none of these six books is a history of Spain. Not one of them tells the war from inside the Republic’s own institutions or the Church’s, and the deaths behind the lines — the priests and nuns killed in republican territory, the tens of thousands shot in nationalist territory during and after the war — are a Hugh Thomas tally in one column, a clause in two, and absent from three. If you want to know what happened in a particular Spanish village between 1936 and 1943, every column here will leave you unsatisfied.
