The Holocaust

On 20 January 1942, in a requisitioned villa on the Wannsee outside Berlin, fifteen men from ministries, the party and the security police sit down for about ninety minutes. Thirty copies of the minutes are typed. One of them is found in 1947, in the files of the German Foreign Office. Six of the classrooms in this corpus describe what those men were coordinating. Two of the books that could have described it say nothing at all.

The question · agency

Who murdered the Jews of Europe — and how many people does your textbook put into that sentence?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • April 1933: the first German laws excluding Jews from the civil service. 1935: the Nuremberg Laws.
  • 9–10 November 1938: coordinated attacks across Germany and annexed Austria. Synagogues burned, Jewish-owned shops wrecked, roughly 30,000 Jewish men arrested and sent to concentration camps.
  • September 1939: Germany invades Poland, which holds the largest Jewish population in Europe. Ghettos follow through 1940–41.
  • 22 June 1941: the invasion of the Soviet Union. Four Einsatzgruppen of the SS and police, together with police battalions and local auxiliaries, shoot Jewish communities behind the front. On 29–30 September 1941, at the Babi Yar ravine outside Kyiv, the killers’ own report records 33,771 Jews shot in two days.
  • December 1941: first systematic killing with gas at Chełmno. 20 January 1942: the Wannsee Conference. During 1942 Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka begin operating; Auschwitz-Birkenau receives transports from across Europe.
  • 27 January 1945: Soviet troops reach Auschwitz. April 1945: British and American troops reach Bergen-Belsen and Buchenwald.
  • Scholarly estimates of the Jewish dead cluster around six million; the range in the literature runs roughly 5.6 to 6 million. Roma and Sinti, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, Poles, and others were killed under related policies.
  • Babi Yar was used for further killings until 1943, and estimates for the whole site over the occupation run well above the two-day figure.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who describe the murder, ordered from the thinnest agency (an agent named once, in a prepositional phrase) to the thickest (a ministry list, a soldier’s letter, and a refusal to let one man carry it). Two books that cover this period and this continent contain nothing on the topic; they are in the receipts, with the search stems, and in the limits. Kazakhstan’s volume is national history and is never a peer column here. China’s and Sudan’s syllabuses do not reach the subject.

One caution before the table. The German column is not a school textbook. It is Informationen zur politischen Bildung No. 316, a booklet issued by the Federal Agency for Civic Education and written by a historian, and the whole booklet is about this. Comparing its page count to a chapter in a world survey measures the genre, not the classroom. What can be compared is the grammar.

3 The tell

Nothing on this ladder is in dispute. All six books say the murder happened, all six give a figure between five and six million or a little above, and none of them hedges. The chapter is not about denial. It is about how many people each classroom needs in the sentence that does the killing — and the range runs from one abstract noun to a continent.

The simplest way to see it is to count one name. In Lowe’s section 6.8, seven printed pages long, the word “Hitler” appears forty times; four other perpetrators are named — Himmler, Heydrich, Goering, Goebbels — and three of them appear inside a single sentence describing what deniers claim. In OpenStax’s Holocaust section, three printed pages, “Hitler” appears not once; the named men are Goebbels, Himmler, Eichmann, and Otto Ohlendorf, the Einsatzgruppen commander, quoted from his own testimony at Nuremberg. In the German booklet’s Holocaust chapter, “Hitler” appears sixteen times, alongside at least fifteen other named organisers: Himmler, Heydrich, Eichmann, Göring, Globocnik, Jeckeln, Greiser, Hans Frank, Stuckart, Freisler, Neumann, Luther, Müller, Bouhler, Brandt.

Neither extreme is a failure. Lowe names Hitler forty times because he has decided to teach the argument itself — intentionalists against functionalists, a school textbook staging a live historiographical dispute and then telling the student where the weight of evidence sits. That is a real service, and the cost is that the apparatus below Hitler stays out of focus. OpenStax has made the opposite trade: it describes the machinery — the squads, the ghettos, the railway lines built to reach the camps, the conference, “the collaboration of tens of thousands of people from across Europe” — and the man at the top never has to be argued about, because he is not in the paragraph.

Then there is the ravine. Three books put a number on Babi Yar, and the numbers do not match. Lowe: “in two days at the end of September 1941, some 34 000 Jews were murdered in a ravine at Babi Yar, on the outskirts of Kiev in Ukraine.” The German booklet: “at the end of September, in only two days, in the ravine of Babi Yar near Kyiv, more than 33,000 people were shot by SS and police,” and, in the caption under the photograph, in the present tense and without the passive: “In the ravine of Babi Yar near Kyiv, SS and police shoot more than 33,000 people on 29/30 September 1941.” Chubaryan: “After the taking of Kyiv, in the area of Babi Yar about 70 thousand Jews were shot.”

Two of those sentences carry a date range and one does not, and that is where most of the gap lives. Thirty-three to thirty-four thousand is the two-day figure, and it is close to identical in the British and German books because both are downstream of the same document — the killers’ own operational report, which recorded 33,771. The Russian sentence gives no dates at all: it says “after the taking of Kyiv,” and the ravine kept being used until 1943. Estimates for the whole site across the occupation run far above the two-day count. So the three books are not necessarily contradicting each other. Two are counting a weekend and one appears to be counting a site, and none of the three tells the student which. The word attached to the larger number is “Jews,” and the larger number, in most reconstructions, includes people who were not.

Stay at the same pit for one more sentence, because the nouns swap places in a way that is worth reading slowly. The Russian book, at Babi Yar, says евреев — Jews. The British book says Jews. The German book, at Babi Yar, says Menschen — people. It is not that the German book avoids the word: two sentences earlier on the same page it counts “over 26,000 Jews” murdered at Kamenets-Podolsk, and the paragraph is entirely about the shooting of Jewish communities. But at the ravine itself, twice, in the text and in the caption, the noun is “people.” That is the one place in this corpus where the perpetrator’s classroom is the vaguer of the two about who was in the ground.

Now the word for November 1938. Four of the six books use the name that came out of the broken shop windows: Kristallnacht in Lowe, Kristallnacht glossed as “the ‘Night of Broken Glass’” in OpenStax, notte dei cristalli in Sabbatucci–Vidotto, «Хрустальная ночь» in Chubaryan. The Russian book prints a second name for it as well, and prints the arithmetic that produced it.

«В ходе погрома было разрушено 267 синагог и свыше 800 магазинов. Ущерб только от разбитых витрин составил 5 млн марок. С этим связано другое название этого события — „Ночь разбитых витрин“. Более 20 тыс. евреев были брошены в концентрационные лагеря.»

“In the course of the pogrom 267 synagogues and over 800 shops were destroyed. The damage from the broken shop windows alone came to 5 million marks. Connected with this is another name for the event — ‘Night of Broken Shop Windows.’ More than 20 thousand Jews were thrown into concentration camps.”

RU · Чубарьян, Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл. (2023) · p. 93

Four sentences: synagogues, shops, an insurance figure in marks, twenty thousand people. The Russian book is the only one that shows the reader why the event is named after glass — the glass had a price and the price was written down. The German booklet does not use the word at all. Its period starts with the war, so it refers back to November 1938 only twice, and both times it calls it what it was: der Pogrom 1938, the pogrom of 1938.

The same collision happens again over the camps, and this time the two books are arguing about a metaphor without knowing it. Chubaryan writes: «Фабриками смерти стали концентрационные лагеря (Аушвиц (Освенцим), Треблинка, Майданек и др.)» — “The concentration camps became factories of death (Auschwitz (Oświęcim), Treblinka, Majdanek and others).” The German booklet devotes a paragraph to that exact image.

„Die wohl am wenigsten zutreffende Metapher für die Vernichtungslager ist die der ‚Todesfabrik‘. So industriell das Verfahren des Tötens in den Gaskammern erscheinen mag, so wenig griff hier ein Rädchen ins andere. […] Die Vorstellung einer ‚Todesfabrik‘, des reibungslosen Ineinandergreifens vieler Teile einer großen Maschine, verschleiert das tatsächliche, brutale Geschehen und entlastet die Phantasie, sich das Unvorstellbare vor Augen zu führen.“

“The probably least fitting metaphor for the annihilation camps is that of the ‘death factory.’ However industrial the procedure of killing in the gas chambers may appear, one cog here meshed very little with the next. […] The notion of a ‘death factory,’ of the frictionless interlocking of many parts of one large machine, veils the actual, brutal events and relieves the imagination of having to hold the unimaginable in front of it.”

DE · Informationen zur politischen Bildung Nr. 316 (2012) · p. 47

One classroom reaches for the machine because it makes the scale sayable in a single line. The other refuses the machine because a machine has no hands in it. Both sentences are about the same camps and the same year, and the disagreement between them is the whole question of this chapter compressed into one noun.

Finally, who the six million are counted with. Chubaryan gives the Holocaust its own sentence and then puts the camps’ dead into one list: “about 11 million people were destroyed: Jews, Russians, Ukrainians, Poles and all those whom the Nazis considered inferior.” A hundred pages later the word loosens further — “The fascists carried out a policy of the deliberate destruction of whole peoples (holocaust, genocide)” — where “holocaust” has become a general term for the destruction of peoples and no people is named. The book’s own glossary is the tightest thing in it: “Holocaust — the targeted policy of Nazi Germany, its allies and accomplices in the persecution and destruction of the Jews.” The Ukrainian volume names six million once, in the first line of a section about the founding of Israel, and the murder’s function in the chapter is to explain why a state was needed. OpenStax counts “more than six million Jewish people and at least three million members of other minority groups” and then adds a sentence almost nobody else risks: “Historians disagree about how many died in the camps, and the true number will likely never be known.” And Sabbatucci–Vidotto is the only book in the corpus that stops to argue about the name, telling the student that “holocaust” means a sacrifice and is therefore improper, that Jews prefer shoah, and that of all the mass exterminations it has listed on the same page — Armenians, Stalin’s deportations, Cambodia — “none had the systematic and planned character of the ‘final solution.’”

4 The thread

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

bpb Nr. 316 (DE) takes the credit line, and the reason is narrower than “it is German and therefore contrite.” It is the only text here that treats the size of the perpetrator group as a historical question and answers it against its own interest: not Hitler’s decision alone; the violence radicalised in the course of the war; ministries and bureaucracy coordinating; a Wehrmacht course teaching a rhyme about partisans and Jews; the French police assisting the first deportation train out of Compiègne; and one plain sentence — German soldiers took part. It is also the only text that inspects its own vocabulary in public, both when it decodes the Wannsee minutes (“the parallelisation of the line of approach — that is, they came to an understanding about murder”) and when it refuses the death-factory image. Distance did not buy that candour. Proximity plus a mandate did.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns the second nod for doing in a survey what surveys almost never do: naming the word problem. It tells the student that the word in the chapter title is the wrong word, gives the word Jews use instead, sets the comparison against other mass killings out loud, and then declines to flatten them into each other. A university manual can afford that argument; it is still the only one here that spends the space.

Lowe (UK) gets the third for teaching sixteen-year-olds a historiographical dispute rather than a conclusion — and for the passage on deniers, which names the three claims (exaggerated numbers, an unwitting Hitler, subordinates acting alone) and says they have been discredited. Naming an argument in order to answer it is more useful to a student than never mentioning it. OpenStax (US) earns a line for the opposite virtue: it is the only column that admits its own numbers are contested.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

The German column is not a school textbook and must not be read as one. It is a federal civic-education booklet on this single subject, written by a university historian; comparing its depth with a chapter inside a world survey measures the format. Everything the ladder claims about it is a claim about its grammar and its named agents, not about how much room it gives the topic.

Two zeros are recorded in the receipts, and neither should be read as a verdict on a country. The Belarusian world-history volume contains no Holocaust, no camps, no ghetto, no Babi Yar, and — outside one false positive inside the word вневременность — no Jews. It defines «Геноци́д» in its glossary of historical concepts and never attaches the word to an event, and it records the Nazi party programme as advancing “anti-Semitic and patriotic slogans” without saying what the first adjective led to. In its occupation pages the population marked for reduction is Slavic. But this corpus pins one volume, «Всемирная история» for grade 11; whether the Belarusian national-history volume names the Minsk ghetto is a question these files cannot answer, and it should be answered before the zero is called avoidance.

The same caution, more strongly, for India. NCERT’s Themes in World History, Class XI, contains no Holocaust, no Auschwitz, no Wannsee, no six million; “Hitler” appears once, in a timeline, and “Jews” once, in the Balfour Declaration. That is a fact about this volume. It is not a fact about Indian schooling: the subject is taught in a different NCERT book, in the Class IX chapter “Nazism and the Rise of Hitler,” which is outside this corpus. A zero in a pinned edition is evidence about that edition and the syllabus that shaped it, and nothing more until the rest of the shelf is read.

Kazakhstan’s volume is national history and never appears as a peer column here. It is worth one line anyway, because of the noun: its occupation pages say “the fascists proceeded to the planned extermination of the Soviet people,” and the reichskommissariat map it prints — Ostland, Ukraine, Muscovy, Volga-Ural, Turkestan — is drawn in more detail than in any world-history book in the corpus. The victim there is a citizenship, not a people.

Sudan’s book is out of scope — its syllabus stops at 1948 and its subject is the region — but it does contain the sentence in which Nazism and Jews meet, and it is a migration statistic: Jewish immigration to Palestine continued “especially in the era of Nazism in Germany, which inflicted torment on the Jews there,” after which the numbers jump from 9,533 in 1932 to 61,854 in 1935. No camps, no six million, no Holocaust. That is a syllabus boundary and a subject boundary at once, and it is the only place in that book where the two words are in the same clause.

Kept verbatim because errors are data: the numbers in the Babi Yar row are not reconciled here and should not be reconciled by us. Two books date their figure to two days and one does not date its figure at all. Until someone reads Chubaryan’s sourcing, the honest statement is that the sentences count different things, not that one of them is wrong.

The name counts in the tell come from text extractions, with section boundaries drawn by us. They are the kind of claim that a printed page can overturn, and they should be re-run before this chapter ships. And none of these six books is a history of the Holocaust as it was lived. If you want Warsaw, Vilna, Salonica or Amsterdam from inside, every column here will leave you with a number and a map.
