### Pitch 1: The city under two names *(seed from the ladder — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** NCERT alone puts the ground in the sentence: “a war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory” (IN, p. 167). Belarus alone finishes the spoils with today’s Chinese names: Japan received “the right to lease the Chinese ports of Port Arthur and Dalny (now — Lüshun and Dalian)” (BY, p. 71). China’s own textbook never names the war that produced those leases (CN zero; stems in receipts). Five of six in-scope books cover the war; the classroom that sits on the harbor is the one that does not.
- **Loop.** Russia needs an ice-free Pacific base and leases Chinese Liaodong (Port Arthur / Lüshun) → Japan and Russia fight a full modern war across Manchuria and the Yellow Sea while Qing China is not a principal belligerent → Portsmouth transfers the Russian lease rights to Japan, not to China → the harbor stays Chinese territory under foreign lease, then under Japanese control, and re-enters Chinese administration only after 1945 — still the same bay on the map.
- **Lander.** Open a map app and type **Dalian** or **Lüshun**. The city under the guns is still there; only the textbook name for the war that was fought over it is missing in one of the six rooms that could have told it.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms names Chinese soil as the war’s location (IN); 1 of 11 pairs Port Arthur/Dalny with Lüshun/Dalian (BY); 0 of 11 run the lease → war → lease-transfer loop; China’s zero is the finding against a 5/6 coverage baseline.
- **Receipts-lite.** Treaty of Portsmouth, 5 September 1905; Convention for the Lease of the Liaotung Peninsula, 1898; Lüshun Museum / Port Arthur fortress ruins still visited as a site. <!-- CHECK: Russian lease convention 27 March 1898; Soviet handover of Lüshun base to PRC completed 1955 -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** It is the comparison’s own hinge — soil named by IN, renamed by BY, erased as a war-title by CN — and the lander is a city every corpus country can look up. Causal precision: the Chinese silence did not cause the war; the war was fought on Chinese ground, and the silence is a classroom fact about whose injury gets a chapter number.

### Pitch 2: “A small but victorious war” *(linguistic — seed from BY)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Belarus is the only column that gives the throne a motive: “the ruling circles of Russia believed that a small but victorious war would help strengthen the position of the tsarist regime… the Russian government went to war with Japan.” Next sentence: the war “turned into a catastrophe” and feeds Bloody Sunday and 1905 (BY, p. 58). Italy and the UK connect defeat to 1905 unrest; only BY names the gamble beforehand.
- **Loop.** Autocracy under domestic pressure seeks a short foreign win to restore prestige → underestimates Japan, loses fleet and army in sequence → prices, casualties, and humiliation detonate the 1905 revolution and force the October Manifesto → the dress rehearsal of 1905 becomes the vocabulary through which 1917 is later taught (feeds ch. 6) without the war “causing” 1917.
- **Lander.** The phrase itself travels: “small victorious war” / “short victorious war” is still the label analysts reach for when a government is accused of picking a fight to solve a problem at home. The reader has heard the structure even if they have never heard Port Arthur.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms states the motive-before-the-war (BY); 3 of 11 wire defeat to 1905 (BY, IT, UK); 0 of 11 keep the four-word gamble and the catastrophe in one causal breath for a general reader outside the Belarusian page.
- **Receipts-lite.** Bloody Sunday, 9/22 January 1905; October Manifesto, 17/30 October 1905; the phrase’s later life in commentary on Russian wars is checkable in any serious 1905 survey (e.g. Ascher, *The Revolution of 1905*). <!-- CHECK: standard English rendering “small victorious war” from маленькая победоносная война -->
- **Why it fits.** Linguistic hinge, shared political ear, clean braid to ch. 6. Risk: stays inside Russian domestic story unless the lander is kept international (the phrase as a type, not a Russian exclusive).

### Pitch 3: Peace in New Hampshire *(my candidate — material)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Italy: Russia “had only to accept the mediation offered by the United States and to sign, in September, the treaty of Portsmouth” (IT, p. 236). United States: “The Treaty of Portsmouth, signed in September 1905, acknowledged Japan’s right to Korea and awarded Japan control of southern Manchuria” (US, p. 363). The war is Asian; the ceasefire paperwork is American. No covering book says why the table was in New Hampshire or what the town was.
- **Loop.** Neither side can dictate a march on the other’s capital; Roosevelt offers mediation to raise U.S. standing as a Pacific power → delegates meet at the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard (New Hampshire / Maine border) because it is secure, wired, and far from both publics → treaty signed 5 September 1905; Roosevelt wins the Nobel Peace Prize in 1906 → the United States writes itself into the Asian balance without firing in the war, a rehearsal for later Pacific arbitration and for OpenStax’s comfort naming who attacked first.
- **Lander.** Portsmouth, New Hampshire, is still a shipyard town and a tourist waterfront. The peace that redrew Korea and Manchuria was signed in a place many readers would file under “lobster,” not “Mukden.”
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 name Portsmouth as the treaty (IT, US; BY names the treaty without the place); 0 of 11 say the treaty was signed in New England or that the mediator took a peace prize for it.
- **Receipts-lite.** Treaty text and signatures, 5 September 1905; Nobel Peace Prize to Theodore Roosevelt, 1906; Portsmouth Naval Shipyard Museum / Treaty trail markers in Kittery–Portsmouth. <!-- CHECK: negotiations at the shipyard’s Building 86 / General Stores Building; prize awarded 1906 -->
- **Why it fits.** Material, shared, slightly non-obvious; braids to U.S. agency on the ladder without making the thread a victory lap. Weakness: softer link to the CN zero than Pitch 1.

### Pitch 4: The ice-free harbor *(my candidate — material, why Chinese soil)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** OpenStax alone states the real-estate reason before the attack: “Seeking a harbor that was ice-free year-round, Russia leased land from China on the Liaodong Peninsula in 1897 and built a new port, Port Arthur” (US, p. 363). <!-- CHECK: lease year often 1898 in handbooks; OpenStax says 1897 — keep as printed, flag in Limits --> NCERT supplies the consequence the lease made possible: the later war was “fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory” (IN, p. 167). Vladivostok freezes; the guns follow the open water.
- **Loop.** Russian Pacific strategy needs a winter port → Qing weakness after 1895 lets Russia lease Chinese Liaodong → the base sits on someone else’s coast and becomes the prize Japan must neutralize → the first blow of 1904 is not on Russian home soil but on a leased Chinese harbor → the war’s geography is a footnote about ice until the lease is named.
- **Lander.** Any bulk port the reader has seen on a winter news map — the reason ships cluster where water stays open — is the same arithmetic. Port Arthur was not a random battlefield; it was a climate decision written onto Chinese coastline.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 states the ice-free motive (US); 1 of 11 states Chinese soil (IN); 0 of 11 join “ice” to “Chinese soil” as one mechanism.
- **Receipts-lite.** Lease convention for Liaotung / Port Arthur, 1898; ice conditions at Vladivostok vs Lüshun in any naval handbook of the period; the harbor is still a port city under the name Lüshun/Dalian. <!-- CHECK: Vladivostok historically ice-bound part of winter without icebreakers; Port Arthur chosen as ice-free -->
- **Why it fits.** Answers *why that ground* without sneering; pairs US and IN seeds; natural setup for Pitch 1’s city lander. Can merge with Pitch 1 if the user wants one tighter thread (ice → lease → war on Chinese soil → Dalian still there).

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (city under two names)** first: it is earned entirely by the comparison (IN soil + BY dual names + CN zero), the lander is a living city, and it keeps causal precision (silence ≠ cause). **Pitch 4** folds into 1 if you want the “why Port Arthur” mechanism. **Pitch 2** is the strongest braid to ch. 6 and the best linguistic lander; use it if the guinea-pig reader lights up at the phrase more than at the map. **Pitch 3** is the clean non-obvious material candidate (peace in New Hampshire) and the best U.S.-side wonder beat; weaker on the omission question that drives this draft.
