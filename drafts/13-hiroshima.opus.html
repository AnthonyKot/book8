Pacific & Hiroshima

At 8:15 in the morning on 6 August 1945, a single bomb detonates about six hundred metres above the centre of Hiroshima. Three days later a second one detonates over Nagasaki. In between, on 8 August, the Soviet Union declares war on Japan and moves into Manchuria. Eight classrooms have those nine days on the page. What each of them needs the bomb to do in the paragraph is the subject of this chapter.

The question · space

What is the bomb for — and how many lines does your classroom give it before the paragraph moves on to something else?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • Japan had been at war in China since 1937 (in Manchuria since 1931). On 7–8 December 1941 it attacked Pearl Harbor and, in the same hours across the date line, British Malaya, Hong Kong and the American-held Philippines.
  • 1942–45: after Midway (June 1942) the American advance runs island by island. Iwo Jima, February–March 1945; Okinawa, April–June 1945, with very heavy casualties on both sides and among Okinawan civilians.
  • Japanese cities were already burning before August. The incendiary raid on Tokyo of 9–10 March 1945 killed on the order of a hundred thousand people in one night, using conventional bombs.
  • The Manhattan Project's first test device was detonated at Alamogordo, New Mexico, on 16 July 1945 (the Trinity test).
  • The Potsdam Declaration of 26 July 1945 demanded Japan's unconditional surrender and was rejected in the days that followed.
  • 6 August: a uranium bomb ("Little Boy") on Hiroshima, dropped from the B-29 Enola Gay. 8 August: Soviet declaration of war. 9 August: Soviet forces enter Manchuria, and a plutonium bomb ("Fat Man") falls on Nagasaki. 15 August: Emperor Hirohito's broadcast. 2 September: the surrender is signed in Tokyo Bay aboard the USS Missouri.
  • Death tolls are estimates and remain contested, because they depend on where you draw the line in time. Figures commonly cited today run to roughly 90,000–140,000 at Hiroshima and 60,000–80,000 at Nagasaki by the end of 1945, with further deaths for decades afterwards.
  • Whether the bombs were militarily necessary, and what weight to give the Soviet entry, has been argued by historians since the 1960s and is not settled.

2 The ladder

Eight narrators whose volumes reach August 1945, ordered from thinnest agency (the bombing has no sentence at all) to thickest (a named president, a named plane, his own diary). Germany's booklet stops at the National Socialist war in Europe and Sudan's syllabus ends in 1948; neither reaches the Pacific. Kazakhstan's volume covers the bombings at length, but it is a national history, not a world-history narrator, so it is tracked in the receipts rather than run as a peer column here.

3 The tell

Nobody in this set disputes that American aircraft destroyed Hiroshima on 6 August and Nagasaki on 9 August, or that Japan surrendered within weeks. Six of the eight name the United States as the actor without hedging. The disagreement is not about who dropped it. It is about what the sentence is doing there — and how much page the classroom is willing to spend before it changes the subject.

Ukraine spends none. The eleventh-grade volume begins in 1945 and opens its Japan section with the deck of the Missouri; the war ends without the bomb in it. The cities appear once in the whole book, in the interrogative, at the top of the section: “Which Japanese cities were subjected to atomic bombing?” The student is expected to supply two proper nouns the textbook never prints. Even the question is passive — the cities зазнали, underwent, suffered — so that nobody is asked who did it. This is a syllabus boundary as much as a choice, and the limits section below says so. But the boundary and the question point in the same direction: the bombing is prior knowledge, not this book's business.

India's NCERT carries the event twice, in two different grammars, thirty-two pages apart. On the world timeline the sentence has a subject: “USA drops atom bombs on Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.” In the Japan chapter, where the argument lives, the subject vanishes: “nuclear bombs were dropped … to shorten the war.” The purpose clause survives the loss of its author. A student reads that the bombs had a reason, without ever reading who held it — and then reads, in the next sentence, that “others think” the destruction was unnecessary. The debate is staged; the defendant has been excused.

China names the actor and then keeps walking. Four sentences put four forces on the board in five days: the American bombs, the Soviet declaration, the Soviet army in Manchuria, and the general counter-offensive in the Liberated Areas. No casualty figure appears. The paragraph is not going to Hiroshima; it is going to a date six weeks later.

10 月 25 日，陈仪在台北代表中国政府庄严宣布台湾光复。从此，台湾作为中国的一个省，回到祖国怀抱。

“On 25 October, Chen Yi in Taipei, representing the Chinese government, solemnly announced the recovery of Taiwan. From then on Taiwan, as a province of China, returned to the embrace of the motherland.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 (2019) · the same paragraph as the atomic bombs, four sentences later

Belarus writes the shortest causal chain in the corpus, and it is a chain: an allied ultimatum, refusal implied, “после чего” — after which — the bombs. Cause, then effect, in one sentence, with no number of dead anywhere in the paragraph. The sentence that follows hands the page to somebody else: “A great contribution to the common victory was made by the Soviet Union,” which then gets three weeks, a million-strong army, and four named territories. The bomb is the transition; the Kwantung Army is the story.

Russia's volume does something the Belarusian one does not: it counts. About 90,000 at Hiroshima, tens of thousands later from radiation sickness, about 270,000 across both cities in total. It is the only Russian-language book in this corpus that lets a student see the human size of the thing. That size is what the rest of the century would borrow as a unit of measurement. And then it builds the surrender out of a compound subject: “The atomic bombing of Japan by the USA and the entry of the USSR into the war compelled the Japanese authorities to go to surrender.” One verb, two nouns joined by и. Grammatically neither is sufficient alone. That is the whole argument, and it is made without an adjective.

Italy, Britain and the United States are the three classrooms where the bomb has to explain itself. Sabbatucci–Vidotto give Truman a decision with two purposes — “innanzitutto … ma anche,” first of all, but also — and the second purpose has an audience rather than a target: the rival Soviet allies. Lowe puts the word “controversial” in the topic sentence, then encloses the American case inside a possessive: “President Truman's justification was …” A justification is something a person offers, not something a book asserts. Two sentences later, “Many historians believe that the bombings were not necessary.” Eighteen pages on, in the chapter on the origins of the Cold War, the same event returns with a different motive attached — that Truman's main aim was “to show Stalin what might happen to Russia if he dared go too far.” The same bombs are doing two different jobs in two different chapters of one book.

OpenStax, the classroom of the state that dropped them, spends the most space and prints the most damaging document. It reproduces Truman's diary of 25 July 1945 in full, including “The target will be a purely military one” and the promise to spare “women and children,” and then, on the next page, states flatly that the bomb was used against Hiroshima and Nagasaki, “both cities populated by civilians including women and children.” The book does not reconcile the two. It lists five possible motives — American lives, the cost of the Manhattan Project, a demonstration to the Soviet Union, revenge for Pearl Harbor, and a long history of anti-Asian sentiment — and then sets the Oppenheimer panel's recommendation against Leo Szilard's petition inside one document exercise, asking the student which side made the better argument. It is the only column that hands the reader the primary sources needed to disagree with the textbook.

4 The thread

The one book in this corpus that does not narrate Hiroshima as an event uses it as a unit. Kazakhstan's eleventh-grade volume arrives at the city from a steppe two thousand kilometres away, inside its pages on the Semipalatinsk test site, and prints the appeal adopted at the rally in the village of Karaul in February 1989 that began the Nevada–Semipalatinsk movement:

«За 40 лет здесь взорвались тысячи Хиросим.»

“Over 40 years, thousands of Hiroshimas have exploded here.”

KZ · История Казахстана, 11 кл. (2015) · extract p. 220 — the Karaul appeal, reproduced in the textbook. Special voice, not a ladder column: national history, tracked in the receipts. On the page before, the same section prices the site's 456 explosions at “more than 2.5 thousand bombs dropped on Hiroshima” (extract p. 219).

Nothing in that sentence is about Japan. A city has become a quantity, and a plural one, and no one on the page stops to explain it. The word travelled further as a unit than the event travelled as a lesson — and the reason is arithmetic. The device over Hiroshima released the energy of roughly fifteen thousand tons of TNT. Seven years later the first thermonuclear test in the Pacific released something like seven hundred times that; nine years after that, the largest device ever detonated released about fifty megatons, the equivalent of more than three thousand Little Boys. Kilotons and megatons are honest units and they mean nothing to anybody. So the ruler that survived was the city: arsenals, test-site totals and reactor accidents have been priced in Hiroshimas ever since, by governments announcing yields and by the campaigners opposing them, in Russian, Kazakh, Italian and English alike.

Which is why the reader has met this unit already, and not in a history book. It appears every time arsenals are compared in a newsfeed — a new warhead, a stockpile total, a treaty lapsing — as “equivalent to N Hiroshimas,” carried without translation and without a footnote. It is the only unit of measurement in ordinary use named after a place where people died.

And the unit has no agreed value. The ladder above prints the size of one Hiroshima five different ways: perhaps 84,000 (UK), about 90,000 (RU), 100,000 (IT), more than 70,000 instantly and past 100,000 in the aftermath (US), approximately 120,000 civilians (IN). Those gaps are counting windows and sources rather than arithmetic, as the limits below say. But the effect is that the world does its most consequential multiplication with a number no two of these classrooms state the same way. The multiplier is announced to three significant figures; the thing being multiplied varies by nearly half.

Two of eleven classrooms use the city as a unit rather than as an event. Kazakhstan does it twice, in the test-site pages quoted above. OpenStax does it once, far from its own bomb chapter: the 1952 hydrogen device was “one thousand times more powerful than the bomb dropped on Hiroshima” (§14.2, p. 581). Both put it outside the chapter where the bomb falls, which is where the unit actually lives. The other nine name the city only on the morning it burned, or — in Ukraine's, Germany's and Sudan's volumes — not at all.

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

OpenStax (US) earns the credit lead, and the reason matters more than the fact. This is the narrating state's own classroom describing its own act, which by this book's thesis is where candor should be thinnest. Instead it prints Truman's private promise to hit “a purely military one,” prints on the following page that both targets were civilian cities, names revenge and anti-Asian sentiment among the plausible motives, and stages the scientists' own split as a document exercise. Distance from the actor usually buys candor. Here a market-published, freely licensed book bought it without the distance.

Lowe (UK) deserves the second nod for the cleanest available sentence on a contested question: the bombings were “one of the most controversial actions of the entire war.” He gives the American case, gives the peace-feelers counter-case, gives the race-to-beat-the-Russians reading, and does not tell the student which to hold. A school textbook is allowed to leave a verdict open, and this one does.

Чубарьян (RU) gets credit for the numbers. Of the books written in Russian in this corpus, it is the only one that tells a student how many people died at Hiroshima, and the only one anywhere in the ladder to give a combined figure for both cities. Belarus's page has no dead in it at all; China's has none; Ukraine's has no page. Whatever else the compound-subject sentence is doing, the counting is a service to the reader, and it should be said.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight national textbooks whose volumes reach August 1945 — the state-approved or dominant market editions in our corpus, not every book published in these countries. Germany's federal civic-education booklet covers the National Socialist war and the Holocaust in Europe and never reaches the Pacific; Sudan's secondary syllabus ends in 1948 and stays regional. Those two zeros are genre, not avoidance.

Ukraine's zero needs care. By our corpus rule an omission is an avoidance signal when most in-scope narrators cover the topic, and seven of eight do. But the Ukrainian volume is the 1945–2018 grade, and August 1945 sits on the far side of its starting line; the bombings belong to the tenth-grade course. The finding that survives that correction is narrower and stranger than “avoidance”: the book asks the student to name the cities it never names, and opens its account of Japan with the surrender rather than with what produced it.

Kazakhstan's eleventh-grade volume is a national history, not a world-history narrator, so under our corpus rules it is not a peer column. It is worth saying what it does anyway, because no other book does it: it treats Hiroshima and Nagasaki inside the story of the Semipalatinsk test site, and reaches for the bombs as a unit of measurement rather than as an event. It also prints a casualty set — 249,000 people, of whom 28,000 died and 114,000 “simply disappeared” — that sits outside every other narrator's range and outside the mainstream literature. Its pages come from a scan whose optical character recognition breaks words into spaced letters, so those figures must be read off the printed page before anyone repeats them.

Two textbook errors kept verbatim, because errors are data. Belarus dates the allied surrender ultimatum to 5 August 1945; the Potsdam Declaration was issued on 26 July 1945, eleven days before Hiroshima. China's page calls the Missouri a cruiser (巡洋舰); it was a battleship. Neither changes the narrative either book is telling, which is part of why they are worth noticing.

The casualty figures in the ladder cannot be reconciled with one another, and that is not a scandal. Roughly 84,000 (UK), 90,000 then 270,000 combined (RU), 100,000 and 60,000 (IT), more than 70,000 instantly rising past 100,000 (US), about 120,000 civilians (IN) — these mostly reflect different counting windows and different sources, not different arithmetic. Anyone quoting one of them should quote the window with it.

Machine-translated files were used to find the pages; every line above was then pulled from the original-language extraction and translated by hand for this chapter. One Italian phrase, «agli alleati rivali sovietici», arrives from the extraction with the two words joined by a line-break artifact; the printed page should be checked before this chapter ships. None of these classrooms is a military history of the Pacific war. If you want carrier actions and island casualties, every column here will disappoint you.
