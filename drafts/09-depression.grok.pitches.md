### Pitch 1: The sticker on the account *(material — seed from US credit — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder / tell).** OpenStax alone prints the afterlife: “The creation of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) in 1933 meant that depositors could feel their money on deposit was safe,” and a sidebar that the FDIC “still assist[s] people in the United States today” (originally $5,000 coverage, now $250,000) (US, printed pp. 494–495). No other covering book treats deposit insurance as a living object. Lowe and IT name the New Deal as 1930s policy; they do not hand the reader a present-tense institution.
- **Loop.** Banks fail and uninsured deposits vanish → Roosevelt’s banking reform includes federal deposit insurance → the guarantee becomes ordinary banking furniture → later crises are argued in the language of “are deposits safe?” rather than “who holds the gold in the vault.” The Depression did not invent thrift; it produced a legal promise that still sits on account paperwork.
- **Lander.** Any bank statement, ATM card, or “Member FDIC” / deposit-guarantee notice a reader has ignored for years. Even outside the United States, national deposit-insurance schemes are the same species of object — a crisis answer that became background. <!-- CHECK: FDIC created by Banking Act of 1933; coverage history; comparable schemes e.g. EU deposit guarantee directives -->
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 names FDIC and its still-running status (US); 0 of 11 join crash → bank runs → deposit insurance → ordinary modern account as one causal loop; BY’s “state regulation” path never needs the name.
- **Receipts-lite.** Banking Act of 1933 / FDIC charter; OpenStax sidebar figures; a current bank’s insurance disclosure. <!-- CHECK: original $5,000 limit 1933; present FDIC limit $250,000 per depositor per ownership category -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** Seeded in the lede (“deposit insurance”); earned by the US column’s exclusive afterlife; shared lander (a money promise people still hold). Causal precision: FDIC did not “end” the Depression — it produced a durable rule about what a bank failure does to a household’s cash.

### Pitch 2: Cotton still on the ticker *(material — seed from SD signature)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Sudan alone makes the crisis a price: cotton’s price is “affected by the capitalist market”; Sudan “was indeed affected” when demand fell; the government cut labor and salaries; Gordon College students strike when Sudanese graduates’ pay falls from eight pounds to five and a half (SD, ~pp. 88, 105). Gezira cotton is ~40% of government income and ~two-thirds of exports in the same lesson. No other book needs a bale of cotton to explain 1929.
- **Loop.** A colonial cash-crop economy ties state revenue to one export → world demand collapses in the early 1930s → prices and salaries fall at the periphery without any local stock exchange → political awakening (strike, workers’ movement) rides the same price series the metropole textbooks file under Wall Street → the commodity, not the ticker, is how the crisis enters ordinary life in an agrarian export economy.
- **Lander.** Cotton is still a world commodity: clothing, medical cotton, futures prices on screens far from Khartoum. A shirt or a bandage is a distant cousin of Gezira’s 1931 problem — not because the shirt “caused” the Depression, but because export monocultures still transmit distant demand shocks. <!-- CHECK: Gezira Scheme history; cotton as share of Sudanese exports in interwar years -->
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 builds 1929 as cotton + salaries (SD); 0 of 11 in the full US-centered chapters (RU/BY/IT/UK/US) put an African export crop in the patient slot; CN’s only patient is Japan.
- **Receipts-lite.** Gezira Scheme (British-period cotton irrigation); Gordon College strike 1931; any modern cotton futures quote. <!-- CHECK: Gezira opened to tenants 1925; nationalization 1950s per SD’s own 1951 note -->
- **Why it fits.** Strongest “who the crash happens to” hinge; material lander shared via clothing, not via U.S. presidential names. Risk: can pull the chapter into a Sudan-only story if the comparison’s New York grammar is not kept in view. Causal precision: the cotton price fall did not cause the Wall Street crash — it is what the crash (and the demand collapse around it) did to a colony priced in that market.

### Pitch 3: “Depression” as a word still in the mouth *(linguistic — seed from label split)*

- **Seed (from the ladder / tell).** Labels do not agree. RU/BY: “world economic crisis” + “Great Depression.” IT: “great crisis” / “great crash,” with “grande depressione” for aftermath. UK: “Wall Street Crash” + “Great Depression” / “world economic crisis.” US: “Great Depression.” CN: “worldwide economic crisis” only. SD: “global economic crisis” only — never Depression, never Wall Street (dossier stems). The noun is part of the finding.
- **Loop.** English-language journalism and U.S. politics brand the slump a “Depression” → schoolbooks that teach a U.S.-centered story inherit the brand → books that need Japan’s road to Manchuria or Sudan’s cotton can run the same years under “economic crisis” without the American proper noun → later speakers reach for “another depression” or “not since the Depression” as a portable scare-measure, often without the 1929 mechanism.
- **Lander.** The word **depression** in ordinary economic argument — news headlines, family stories about grandparents, jokes about markets — is the school label that escaped the classroom. Readers who have never opened a 1930s chapter still own the word.
- **Who tells it.** 5 of 11 use a Depression label in the event treatment (IN, BY, RU, UK, US); IT prefers “crisi”/“crollo” with depressione secondary; CN/SD: 0 for “Depression” / “كساد” as the event name in these passages.
- **Receipts-lite.** Contemporary 1930s U.S. usage of “Great Depression”; contrast with German *Weltwirtschaftskrise* and Russian *мировой экономический кризис* as default school labels. <!-- CHECK: early print uses of “Great Depression” for 1929–33 -->
- **Why it fits.** Pure comparison hinge; linguistic lander travels across corpus countries. Weaker cup-of-tea sensory hit than a bank sticker or a cotton shirt unless the reader cares about argument words. Bank as tell reinforcement even if another pitch carries the thread.

### Pitch 4: War closed the books *(my candidate — non-obvious, shared causal claim)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Two thick Western columns print the same limit on the New Deal story. Lowe: “It was the war that finally put an end to the depression” (UK, ~p. 498). OpenStax: “only the economic stimulation of the onset of World War II brought the world closer to a return of prosperity” (US, p. 491). Italy agrees in substance: real productive relaunch only late in the decade, “with rearmament and the war” (IT, ~p. 372 area). RU dates crisis atmosphere into the war years without that exact punch line. The recovery everyone’s textbooks praise is incomplete in the textbooks’ own next sentence.
- **Loop.** New Deal / state regulation / public works reduce pain and rewrite the social contract → unemployment and output do not fully return to pre-crisis prosperity on peacetime policy alone → rearmament and wartime production absorb labor and capacity → the same decade’s political radicalization (RU’s Nazi “wave,” BY’s fertile soil for extremists, US/UK links to authoritarianism) sits next to an economic recovery that full employment under arms finally finishes → later arguments that “war is good for the economy” are borrowing a 1930s receipt, not inventing a cynicism.
- **Lander.** Every modern claim that military spending “creates jobs” or that a war boom cured a slump is speaking this grammar. The reader has heard the shape in news and argument; the chapter can show where schoolbooks already concede the mechanism. <!-- CHECK: US unemployment series 1933–1941 vs wartime; standard economic-history caveat that correlation is not a moral brief for war -->
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 11 print war/rearmament as the final recovery engine (UK, US, IT); 0 of 11 in the thin columns (IN, CN, SD) need recovery at all; BY stops at “state regulation” without war-as-cure; RU keeps crisis-to-dictatorship more than crisis-to-full-employment.
- **Receipts-lite.** Lowe p. 498 and OpenStax p. 491 as the paired lines; wartime production and unemployment figures in any standard U.S. economic history. <!-- CHECK: U.S. unemployment ~25% early 1930s, still high late 1930s, sharp fall after 1940–41 mobilization -->
- **Why it fits.** Non-obvious as a *thread* (it is half-visible in the tell already); shared across corpus countries via the later war chapters this book is walking into (Spain, Munich, world war). Causal precision is mandatory and is the pitch’s strength: war production did not “justify” the war — in these classrooms it is what finished a recovery peacetime policy had not finished. Feeds ch. 10–11 without stealing their agency fights. Risk: can sound like a sneer at the New Deal if the punchline is not kept to “what closed the output gap,” not “the Deal was fake.”
