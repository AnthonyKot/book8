Korea

Before dawn on 25 June 1950, artillery opens along a line of latitude that runs across the Korean peninsula. The line is five years old. It was drawn in Washington in August 1945 by two American officers working from a small-scale map, and it was never meant to be a border. Within two days Seoul has fallen. Seventy-odd years later the line is still there, and the war that crossed it has never been ended by a treaty. This is the chapter the whole book has been walking toward: one event, seven classrooms, and a single question none of them can dodge.

The question · agency

Who started it — and when a textbook has already answered that in the war’s name, what is left for the paragraph to do?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • Korea was ruled by Japan from 1910 until Japan’s surrender in August 1945.
  • August 1945: the peninsula is divided at the 38th parallel for the purpose of accepting the Japanese surrender — Soviet forces north, American forces south. The division was administrative, not a settled frontier.
  • 1948: two states are proclaimed — the Republic of Korea in the south (August, Syngman Rhee, Seoul) and the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea in the north (September, Kim Il Sung, Pyongyang). Each claims the whole peninsula. Soviet and American troops withdraw in 1948–49.
  • 25 June 1950: North Korean forces cross the parallel in strength. Seoul falls within days.
  • 27 June 1950: the UN Security Council passes Resolution 83 calling on members to resist. The Soviet delegate is absent — Moscow had been boycotting the Council over China’s seat, held by the Nationalists in Taipei.
  • September 1950: US-led landing at Incheon. UN forces cross the parallel northwards in October. Late October–November: Chinese People’s Volunteers enter the war and drive the front back south. April 1951: General Douglas MacArthur is relieved of command.
  • 27 July 1953: an armistice is signed at Panmunjom by the UN Command, the Korean People’s Army and the Chinese People’s Volunteers. South Korea did not sign. A Demilitarized Zone replaces the old line. No peace treaty has followed.
  • Mainstream estimates of the dead run to roughly two and a half to three million, a majority of them Korean civilians; the books below print figures from three to four million, or none at all.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators who actually teach the war, ordered from thinnest agency (a war with no author, in a list of other wars) to thickest (a named army, a named date, a named man who decided). Books outside the period or the syllabus — Russia’s 1914–45 volume, Germany’s National Socialism booklet, Sudan’s 1821–1948 syllabus, Kazakhstan’s national-history course — are absent on purpose. That absence is a curriculum boundary, not a verdict.

3 The tell

Every book here agrees on the calendar. Korea was divided in 1945, two states existed by 1948, fighting began in June 1950, the shooting stopped in July 1953 near where it started. The disagreement is entirely in one sentence — the one where the war acquires a subject.

Belarus removes the sentence. The war appears once, mid-paragraph, as an item in a list of things that flared up in “the countries of Asia, Africa and Latin America,” alongside Vietnam and the Middle East. There is no June, no parallel, no Kim, no MacArthur, no dead. Even the name is uncapitalised — корейская война, a common noun, not a proper event. The verb that does the work in that paragraph belongs to the superpowers, and it is a verb of restraint: they “fought each other indirectly rather than directly.” The chronology at the back pairs the war with Vietnam on a single line: «Войны в Корее» — wars in Korea, plural, 1950–1953 and 1964–1973 sharing one entry. Two wars, one country, no starter.

India’s NCERT keeps the intransitive verb — the war “broke out,” the way weather does — and then changes the subject. Its Korea pages sit inside a chapter on paths to modernisation, and what the war is for in that chapter is stated plainly: it “caused not only massive losses of life and property, but also a delay in free-market economic development and democratisation.” Inflation, destroyed colonial-era industry, dependence on American aid, and Syngman Rhee extending his own term “twice through illegal constitutional amendments.” Who fired first is not a question this lesson needs, because the lesson is about what a war costs a developing economy. That is a real analytical frame, and it is also a way of not answering.

China answers, twice, in opposite directions, in the space of two clauses. The first four characters of the outbreak are 朝鲜内战 — the Korean civil war. That single word makes 25 June an internal Korean matter with no foreign author, and it is the most agentless start-clause in the corpus after Belarus’s. Then the grammar turns. For the rest of the paragraph the United States is the subject of every verb: it 操纵 (manipulated) the Security Council, 越过 (crossed) the parallel, 侵略朝鲜 (invaded Korea), 侵入 (intruded into) the Taiwan Strait, and at the end 不得不 — had no choice but to — sign. The lesson’s summary box on the previous page has already named the whole thing 美国发动的侵朝战争: “the war of aggression against Korea launched by the United States.” The start is authorless; everything after it has one author.

And the war’s Chinese name is a verdict with no date in it. 抗美援朝 — resist America, aid Korea — is two verbs and two objects. A student who learns only the name has already learned who was resisting and who was aggressing, before any paragraph opens. The book knows there is a second name, and keeps both, one line apart, in the chronology at the back: 「朝鲜战争结束，中国人民取得抗美援朝战争的胜利」 — “the Korean War ended; the Chinese people won the victory of the War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea.” The neutral name for the event, the national name for the outcome, in a single sentence.

（抗美援朝战争的胜利）雄辩地证明：西方侵略者几百年来只要在东方一个海岸上架起几尊大炮就可霸占一个国家的时代是一去不复返了。

“(The victory of the War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea) proves eloquently that the era is gone for ever in which, for several hundred years, Western aggressors could seize a country merely by mounting a few cannon on a coast in the East.”

CN · 彭德怀, quoted in the 史料阅读 source box · 中外历史纲要 上, p. 175

That source box is the hinge of this chapter, and it points backwards fourteen chapters. Peng Dehuai measures 1953 against a specific earlier event: cannon on an eastern coast, a country seized. That is the Opium War (ch. 1) — Chinese schoolchildren meet the same image at both ends of the same book. Korea is not filed in this textbook under “Cold War.” It is filed under the closing of an account opened in 1839.

Ukraine writes the most careful sentence in the set, and writes it in two halves. First: “both regimes sought to unite the Korean peninsula under their own power” — motive distributed evenly. Then: “North Korean troops crossed the 38th parallel and began a war.” Two verbs, and the second one, розпочали, does not describe a movement; it assigns authorship. Crossing is geography, beginning is responsibility, and the book supplies both in that order. Ukraine also prints the sentence no other classroom prints: at page 172, on regional conflicts, «Хоча його вдалося зупинити, сторони досі перебувають у стані війни» — “although it was possible to stop it, the parties are still in a state of war.” Present tense. In a 2019 schoolbook.

Italy and the two Anglophone books say “invaded,” and then differ in what they attach to it. Sabbatucci–Vidotto attach a supplier — the North Korean forces were armate dai sovietici, armed by the Soviets — but first supply a run-up: frontier incidents, rising tension, both states claiming the whole country. Lowe attaches an adverb instead: “Without warning.” Then, remarkably for a school textbook, he takes it back for two pages and runs the origin as an open question — Kim’s own idea; Dean Acheson’s speech that left Korea off the American defence perimeter; the new Chinese government; Stalin testing Truman; and, given its own line and no sneer, “the communists claimed that South Korea had started the war.” Five hypotheses, then a ranking: “probably the most widely accepted view nowadays.” OpenStax is the most specific and the most surprising. It names the unit (the Korean People’s Army), the date (25 June), the mood (“confident of welcome”) — and then removes its own most convenient villain: “Stalin had in fact warned Kim against it… the Soviet Union sent nothing.” The American book declines the American answer.

One habit is shared by everybody, in mirror image: the scare quotes. China quarantines “联合国军” — the so-called “United Nations Force.” Lowe quarantines the Chinese troops “described as ‘volunteers’.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto do the same with “volontari”. Ukraine sends its Southern coalition in «під прапором ООН», “under the flag of the UN,” a phrase that does duty as a quotation mark. Nobody in this chapter lets the other side keep its own word for its own soldiers — and no book stops to explain what put a UN flag over one of the two armies in the first place.

The dead are counted the same way — by whose they are. Ukraine gives one number and it is Chinese: Kim Il Sung’s government “was saved at the cost of lives of 1 million Chinese soldiers.” Lowe gives “about four million Korean soldiers and civilians… and five million people were homeless.” OpenStax gives “approximately three million people… most of them Korean civilians,” and calls the war “futile.” India gives “massive losses of life and property.” China, whose volunteers did much of the dying, gives no figure at all — its casualty paragraph is a paragraph about what the victory produced: national prestige, international standing, unity at home. Belarus gives nothing, because Belarus has no room; the war is one clause long.

4 The thread

Four books answer that question without noticing they have, and a fifth supplies the far end. OpenStax prints the mechanism in full: the Security Council’s actions “could have been prevented by a veto of one its five permanent members,” but “since the Nationalists’ loss in the Chinese civil war, the United States had insisted that China’s seat on the council belonged to Taiwan, not to the People’s Republic of China, and the Soviet Union had boycotted the council’s meetings in protest. It was thus unable to stop the resolution from passing” (pp. 583–584). Sabbatucci–Vidotto put the same fact inside a subordinate clause and keep walking: the Americans acted «sotto la bandiera dell’Onu, in quanto il Consiglio di sicurezza, in assenza del delegato sovietico, aveva condannato la Corea del Nord e autorizzato l’invio di truppe» — “under the flag of the UN, since the Security Council, in the absence of the Soviet delegate, had condemned North Korea and authorised the sending of troops.” China’s textbook, quoted in the ladder above, gives the same event a transitive verb — the United States 操纵, manipulated, the Council — and no empty chair. Ukraine holds the other end of the wire, a hundred pages away in its chapter on China: «Захід не визнавав нової держави — її місце в ООН до 1971 р. займали представники Гоміндану», “the West did not recognise the new state — its place in the UN until 1971 was occupied by representatives of the Kuomintang.” And Lowe, listing the war’s effects, delivers the verdict: China “was now clearly a world power. The fact that she was still not allowed a seat in the UN seemed even more unreasonable.”

Each classroom prints one gear. None of them assembles the machine — the gears sit in five books, in four languages, in different chapters and different countries, and the sentence that joins them is in none of them. Here it is, and it is the thing this book exists to add.

The People’s Republic is founded in October 1949; China’s seat on the Security Council stays in Taipei. In January 1950 Moscow walks out of the Council in protest and stays out. On 27 June the one veto that could have stopped Resolution 83 is not cast, because the chair it belongs to is empty. That absence is what puts a legal UN flag over the force that lands at Incheon, crosses the parallel in October and reaches the Yalu — the “United Nations Force” every row of the ladder argues about, with scare quotes or without them, exists because somebody was not in the room. China enters the war to meet that force; in February 1951 the UN General Assembly names the People’s Republic an aggressor; and the seat the boycott was staged to win stays in Taipei for another twenty-one years, until 1971. The empty chair did not produce the war — the tanks of 25 June did that. It produced the flag the war was fought under, and the boycott staged for China's seat ended up flying that flag against China.

Moscow read the result quickly. The Soviet delegate was back in the Council in August 1950 — in time to take the rotating presidency for that month, far too late to unmake June — and no permanent member has staged a boycott like it since. What June 1950 settled in practice is that an absence is not a veto. The Charter asks for the concurring votes of the permanent members; the Council has read a member who abstains, or who is not there, as declining to block rather than blocking.

That reading is on the newsfeed every week. Whenever a Security Council resolution “fails” in a bulletin, it fails because a permanent member voted no while sitting in its chair — never because one of them left the building, and the reason none of them ever leaves is a peninsula in 1950. Five of the eleven classrooms in this corpus print at least one gear of that machine: the United States the boycott, Italy the absent delegate, China the resolution, Ukraine the seat held until 1971, Britain the unreasonableness of it. None of the eleven assembles it.

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) takes the credit lead, and it is the strongest single installment in this book. Having written “Without warning,” he then spends two pages refusing to leave it there: a bulleted list of five competing explanations for the attack, the Acheson speech that may have invited it, and — printed without irony marks — the communist claim that the South started it. A market textbook aimed at exam candidates chose to show them that the origin of a war is contested evidence rather than a fact to memorise. That is the method of this book, done inside a textbook.

OpenStax (US) earns the second nod for subtracting the convenient answer. It would cost this textbook nothing to leave the 1950 invasion as a Kremlin operation; instead it records that Washington suspected exactly that and was wrong — Stalin “warned Kim against it,” told him to ask Beijing rather than Moscow, and “sent nothing.” It also spells out the mechanism by which the Security Council could act at all: the Soviet boycott over China’s seat. Candor about one’s own side’s misreading is the rarest thing in this corpus.

Гісем–Мартинюк (UA) deserves the sequencing credit. Giving both governments the same ambition before naming who moved is a harder sentence to write than either half alone, and the book still names who moved. It is also the only classroom in the corpus that tells a student the war is not over.

纲要 (CN) is the depth lead. It is the only book that puts a primary source on the page — Peng Dehuai’s own report — names the commander, counts the campaigns, and explains why Korea mattered to the state teaching it, including the Seventh Fleet in the Taiwan Strait. Its opening word, 内战, and its title, 抗美援朝, are the chapter’s clearest example of a verdict arriving before the evidence. Both things are true of the same lesson.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

Seven books teach this war; four in the corpus do not, and none of those four is avoiding it. Russia’s volume here is the grade-10 unified course covering 1914–1945; Germany’s is a federal civic booklet on National Socialism; Sudan’s secondary syllabus ends in 1948; Kazakhstan’s is a national-history course, not a world-history one. Under this book’s own rule, a zero counts as an avoidance signal only when most in-scope narrators cover the topic and a couple skip it. These are syllabus boundaries.

The Russian gap is the sharpest limitation in this chapter, and the poster exhibit does not close it. A wall-chart answer key and a narrative textbook are different objects, and it would be unfair to read a table cell as a national account of the war. The proper column is the grade-11 companion volume covering 1945 onward, which this corpus does not yet hold; until it does, the honest statement is that we do not know how the current Russian classroom narrates June 1950, only how its revision poster labels it.

Kazakhstan is not a ladder column here, but it is worth one line as a signature topic, because its Korea is a different Korea. The 1950–53 war is absent; what the book covers at length is the deportation of the Far Eastern Koreans — «Одной из первых жертв массовой депортации народов в СССР стали дальневосточные корейцы», “one of the first victims of the mass deportation of peoples in the USSR were the Far Eastern Koreans,” moved in two stages from autumn 1937 to spring 1938 and settled across eight Kazakh regions . The other Korea in that textbook is Samsung, taking over the management of Kazakhmys in the 1990s. A war fought by Koreans is absent; Koreans as deportees and Korea as an investor are present. That is what a national-history genre does to a world event, and it is a finding about genre, not about candour. (OCR note: the Kazakh extraction carries intra-word spacing artifacts; the line above is normalised and must be eye-checked against the printed page.)

Editions are pinned and they are not contemporaries: Italy 2004 is a university manual, the UK book is 2013, Ukraine 2019 — its “still in a state of war” line predates nothing later than its own printing, and its 2018 Trump–Kim paragraph reads differently now than it did then. None of these books is a military history; if you want the Pusan perimeter or the air war over the Yalu, every column here will disappoint you.
