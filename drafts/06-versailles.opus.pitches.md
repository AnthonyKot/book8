### Pitch 1: The thirteenth part *(my candidate — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** OpenStax measures the treaty and never opens it: the Treaty of
  Versailles was “a ‘massive document that ran to 436 pages containing 433 articles organized
  into fifteen parts’” (US, p. 476 — the quotation marks are the book’s own). Every column
  then teaches the same three or four parts: territory, army, guilt, money. Thirty pages after
  its own settlement section, Sabbatucci–Vidotto notices something and explains it without the
  treaty: the workers of 1919 won “the reduction of the working day to eight hours daily at
  equal wage: an objective that for thirty years had figured in first place in the programmes
  of the socialist movement and that was reached almost simultaneously, immediately after the
  end of the war, in all the principal European States” (IT, § 15.3, p. 327). Almost
  simultaneously. The manual credits the strikes and the fear of “doing as in Russia,” and
  never mentions that the treaty it described in § 13.12 has a whole part about the length of
  a working day.
- **Loop.** The peacemakers are negotiating in the year of the Berlin risings, the Hungarian
  Soviet Republic and the biennio rosso, and they write the fear into the treaty itself: Part
  XIII opens by stating that labour conditions produce “unrest so great that the peace and
  harmony of the world are imperilled,” and that peace “can be established only if it is based
  upon social justice” → those forty-one articles create the International Labour Organization,
  the one body at Paris where governments sit with employers and unions at the same table →
  its first conference, Washington, October 1919, adopts Convention No. 1: eight hours a day,
  forty-eight a week → the League dies, the treaty is dismantled clause by clause in the
  1930s, and Part XIII survives all of it, moves into the UN in 1946, and is still in Geneva
  writing the standards. The one section of the Treaty of Versailles still in force is the one
  no classroom on this ladder teaches — and it is in the treaty because of the country that
  was not allowed in the room.
- **Lander.** The working day. Eight hours, and the weekend on the far side of it, is the
  oldest surviving clause of the settlement this chapter is about — a treaty term in every
  corpus country’s labour code, checkable in the reader’s own contract. Whatever else 1919
  imposed on whom, this is the part of it the reader is inside on a Tuesday.
- **Causal precision (must survive into the prose).** Part XIII did not produce the eight-hour
  day; the strikes of 1918–19 did, and Sabbatucci–Vidotto is right about that. Part XIII
  produced the *international standard* — the thing that turned a national concession won
  under pressure into a treaty obligation exportable beyond Europe, and the institution that
  is still issuing them.
- **Who tells it.** 0 of 11 classrooms connect the eight-hour day to the treaty. 1 of 11
  counts the treaty’s parts (US). 1 of 11 describes the ILO at all (UK) — “its purpose was to
  improve conditions of labour all over the world by persuading governments to: fix a maximum
  working day and week,” and, later, “the International Labour Organization is still operating
  today” — both in the League chapter and the UN chapter, sixty and six hundred pages from
  § 2.8, and neither says the treaty created it. 3 of 11 teach the eight-hour day as a purely
  domestic labour-movement story (IT for Europe, UK for Russia and the USA, US for American
  reformers). <!-- CHECK: recount ILO/eight-hour stems across all 11 before this ships; the greps behind these numbers are Международн- организаци- труда, 国际劳工, ufficio/organizzazione internazionale del lavoro, восьмичасов, otto ore, eight-hour -->
- **Receipts-lite.** Treaty of Versailles, Part XIII, Articles 387–427 (the preamble is one
  paragraph and reads like a warning); first International Labour Conference, Washington DC,
  autumn 1919; ILO Convention No. 1 (Hours of Work, Industry), 1919; Albert Thomas, first
  Director, named by Lowe; ILO becomes a UN specialised agency in 1946 and takes the Nobel
  Peace Prize in 1969. All of it is on the ILO’s own site with the founding text.
  <!-- CHECK: Part XIII = arts. 387–427; Washington conference 29 Oct–29 Nov 1919; Convention No. 1 adopted 28 Nov 1919; ILO–UN agreement 1946; Nobel 1969; preamble wording "unrest so great that the peace and harmony of the world are imperilled" -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** The chapter’s question is *whose exclusion does your textbook
  call the injustice*, and this thread answers it from inside the paper: the excluded power is
  in the treaty text, as a motive. It is the only candidate whose lander is not a place, a
  photograph or a grievance but a condition the reader is standing in, and it keeps the
  register at “look what connects” — nobody hid Part XIII; it is simply the part that stopped
  being exciting once it worked.

### Pitch 2: The arch that was re-cut *(my candidate — non-obvious; the seed is inside a single corpus book)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** The Chinese volume prints the sentence this chapter quotes —
  巴黎和会上，强权战胜了公理, “at the Paris Peace Conference, power
  triumphed over justice” (CN, printed p. 137). Seventeen printed pages earlier, in the Boxer
  lesson, the same volume runs a 历史纵横 sidebar on the Ketteler stele: the arch the Qing
  court was made to erect in apology for a German minister’s death, and then — “in 1918
  Germany was defeated in the First World War and China became one of the victor nations; on
  13 November the excited people of Beijing renamed the humiliating Ketteler stele the ‘Stele
  of Justice Triumphant’ and moved it from Dongdan to Central Park,” before the 1952 Asian and
  Pacific Peace Conference renamed it again, “Stele of Defending Peace” (CN, printed p. 120).
  公理战胜 — justice triumphs. Six months later the same book writes
  强权战胜了公理 — power triumphs over justice. Same four characters,
  reversed. One book, two lessons, no line drawn between them.
- **Loop.** The 1901 Protocol charges China an indemnity payable to eight powers and a stone
  apology to one of them → China declares war on Germany in 1917 for a seat at the peace table
  → Germany loses, and in November 1918 Beijing re-cuts the apology into a victory arch and
  walks it across town → at Paris the treaty does give China the only clauses it ever wins
  there: Germany renounces the Boxer indemnity and hands back its concessions at Tianjin and
  Hankou → and the same treaty gives Shandong to Japan, so China refuses to sign the document
  carrying its own cancellation and takes it separately in 1921 → the arch stays up, is
  re-inscribed once more in 1952, and the sentence in the schoolbook is its promise turned
  the other way round.
- **Lander.** The stone is still standing, in Zhongshan Park beside the Forbidden City, on
  every map app, and it has been re-carved by three different verdicts about the same
  century. It is the only object in this chapter whose text was edited by the Treaty of
  Versailles.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 prints the arch (CN) and 1 of 11 prints the inverted sentence — the
  same one, and it never joins them. 0 of 11 mention that the treaty cancelled Germany’s Boxer
  indemnity or returned the Tianjin and Hankou concessions; in the Chinese volume
  赔款, indemnity, occurs six times and is money China pays, every
  time.
- **Receipts-lite.** Boxer Protocol, 7 September 1901; Ketteler memorial erected 1903, taken
  down November 1918, re-erected in Central Park (now Zhongshan Park) as 公理战胜; Treaty of
  Versailles, Section VIII (China), Articles 128–134; Sino-German agreement of 1921; the arch
  renamed 保卫和平坊 in 1952. <!-- CHECK: Art. 128 renunciation of Boxer Protocol claims incl. indemnity; arts. 129–130 Tianjin/Hankou concessions; the CN extraction reads 公理战胜牌 in one place and 公理战胜碑 in another — read against the printed page; Ketteler stele completed Jan 1903 -->
- **Honest risk.** The lander is one country’s monument, and AGENT.md’s shared-hook test is
  real. Two ways to pay for it: run the lander as the *reversal* (a promise carved in stone
  and its inversion printed as the lesson to memorise — a shape any reader recognises), and
  keep it to five sentences. If that still reads as the China chapter arriving early, bank it
  for ch. 7, where the sidebar is already waiting on the shelf.

### Pitch 3: The year the petitioners came *(develops the ladder’s smallest scene)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Four lines on page 51 of the Russian volume, with no names in
  them: “in the hope of taking part in the resolution of colonial problems, representatives of
  a number of colonial and dependent countries of the East also arrived in France… they were
  not granted the attention of the conference participants, and left Paris disappointed”
  (RU, p. 51). The rest of the ladder holds the pieces the Russian paragraph does not name:
  Sudan’s Arab peoples who “clung” to self-determination (SD, printed p. 189), China’s 4 May
  (CN, IN, US), and the Fourteen Points printed as a text without a distribution list.
- **Loop.** The Fourteen Points are translated, printed and broadcast worldwide during 1918 by
  the belligerents’ own propaganda machinery, and self-determination is read everywhere as a
  promise addressed to the reader → through the winter of 1918–19 petitioners converge on
  Paris: Faisal for the Arabs, Kim Kyu-sik for Korea, a Vietnamese group whose petition is
  signed with a name nobody yet knows, Du Bois’s Pan-African Congress two streets from the
  conference, and an Egyptian delegation that is refused passports and deported to Malta
  instead → none of them is heard → and 1919 becomes a single year of mass politics in four
  places at once: Korea on 1 March, Egypt from 9 March, China on 4 May, and India in the year
  of the Rowlatt protests → the petition ends as a method, and the movement replaces it.
- **Lander (linguistic, and it is the best line in the pitch).** The Egyptian delegation that
  was never allowed to travel called itself *al-Wafd* — “the delegation.” It then governed
  Egypt, on and off, for thirty years under that name: a country’s main political party named
  after a trip to Paris that was not permitted. And the promise itself is still in the reader’s
  vocabulary — “self-determination” went from Wilson’s phrase to Article 1 of the UN Charter,
  and it is the term every corpus country used for its own claim, including the ones that were
  refusing everyone else’s in 1919.
- **Causal precision.** Paris did not create these movements — most of them existed and were
  petitioning before the conference sat. What the refusals produced was the end of petitioning
  as a strategy: the same men come back from Paris and organise mass movements instead.
- **Who tells it.** 4 of 11 print one node of the wave (RU the nameless delegations; CN and IN
  the 4 May demonstration; SD the Arab claim; US mentions May Fourth in a sentence). 0 of 11
  print two nodes as one event. 0 of 11 name Korea, Egypt, Vietnam or the Pan-African Congress
  anywhere near 1919.
- **Receipts-lite.** *Revendications du peuple annamite*, Paris, June 1919, signed Nguyễn Ái
  Quốc; Kim Kyu-sik in Paris for the Korean delegation, spring 1919; Saad Zaghloul’s Wafd
  deported to Malta, March 1919; Pan-African Congress, Paris, February 1919; Erez Manela, *The
  Wilsonian Moment* (2007), which is the standard account and is a library book, not a
  revelation. 1 March is still a public holiday in South Korea; 4 May is still Youth Day in
  China. <!-- CHECK: Revendications dated 18 June 1919; Zaghloul deported 8 March 1919; Pan-African Congress 19–21 Feb 1919; Samiljeol and 中国青年节 both current public observances -->
- **Risk.** It is the widest candidate and the easiest to let run past a screen, and the
  temptation to write it as an indictment is strong. Written cold it is the truest answer to
  the chapter’s question; written warm it becomes the register CONTEXT.md bans.

### Pitch 4: Three men, or four *(cheapest to write; seeded directly by the tell)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** The books cannot agree how many men ran the conference. Koshelev:
  the tone was set by Wilson, Lloyd George and Clemenceau, “known as the ‘Big Three’” (BY,
  p. 97). Chubaryan: Wilson, Lloyd George, Clemenceau and Orlando, “dubbed ‘the Big Four’”
  (RU, p. 51). Lowe’s photograph caption prints three; Chubaryan’s prints four;
  Sabbatucci–Vidotto keeps Orlando and demotes him inside the same bracket, “though figuring
  nominally among the ‘four great ones’, in reality he played a marginal role” (IT, p. 296).
- **Loop.** The drafting happens behind closed doors and no minutes go to the public → what
  the public gets instead is a group portrait, most famously the Signal Corps photograph of
  four men on a Paris sofa in May 1919 → the group’s size becomes its name, and the name
  becomes the caption → a century later the caption is what the classroom inherits, so a
  Belarusian student learns a conference of three and a Russian student a conference of four,
  from the same fortnight → and the format outlives the conference: the leaders’ line-up
  photograph, and the argument about who is standing in it, is now the standard grammar of
  every summit.
- **Lander.** The family photo. Every G7 and G20 meeting ends with the same shot Paris
  invented the appetite for, and the reader has already had the argument the textbooks are
  having — who is in the picture, who is at the edge, who did not come.
- **Who tells it.** 4 of 11 print a leader count, and they split 2–2 (BY three; UK’s caption
  three; RU four; IT four with an asterisk). 0 of 11 note that the count is a crop rather than
  a fact; 1 of 11 can see the seam, and it is Italy, because Italy is the fourth chair.
- **Receipts-lite.** Orlando left Paris on 24 April 1919 and returned in early May, which is
  the fortnight both counts are true in; Edward N. Jackson’s Council of Four photograph, US
  Army Signal Corps, May 1919; John Christen Johansen’s painting of the signing, credited in
  OpenStax’s own figure line. <!-- CHECK: Jackson photograph dated 27 May 1919; do not claim Paris "invented" the summit photograph — the claim must stay at the caption and the crop -->
- **Why it is fourth.** It is elegant and it is thin: it explains a disagreement rather than an
  event, the lander is a habit rather than an object, and half of it is already doing its work
  in § 3. Best use is probably two sentences inside whichever thread wins, not the thread.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (Part XIII)** first, and by some distance. It grows out of a number a textbook
printed and a simultaneity a textbook noticed and explained away; the lander is the reader’s
own working day rather than a place they have to imagine; it braids the chapter’s question to
its own answer — the power kept out of the room is why the treaty contains a labour section —
and the causal precision rule makes it *more* interesting rather than less, because “the
strikes won the eight hours, the treaty exported them” is the honest and the better sentence.
**Pitch 2 (the arch)** is the one I would fight for if the guinea-pig reaction is a laugh of
recognition: it is the strongest single find in the chapter’s research and the seed is entirely
inside one corpus book, which is the ch. 2 quinine shape again. Its shared-hook risk is real
and it banks cleanly to ch. 7 if it loses. **Pitch 3 (the petitioners)** if the reaction that
lands is *these are the same year*; it is the most likely to run long and the most likely to
tip into indictment. **Pitch 4** should be harvested for two sentences of § 3 or § 5 whatever
happens here.
