### Pitch 1: The shortcut that grew an army *(my candidate — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** OpenStax states the war's cause as a piece of railway logistics — Vladivostok freezes, so “Russia leased land from China on the Liaodong Peninsula in 1897 and built a new port, Port Arthur” (US, p. 363) — and then, 162 pages later, in a different chapter about a different decade, states the war's product: “Since the end of the Russo-Japanese War in 1905, Japan had held the right to lease the South Manchuria Railway in northeastern China, a privilege previously held by Russia. … In 1931, these troops detonated explosives along the track of the South Manchuria Railway” (US, p. 525). Lowe holds the same two ends four hundred pages apart — “was given Port Arthur and a privileged position in South Manchuria as a result of the Russo-Japanese War (1904–5)” (UK, p. 69). Neither book joins the ends.
- **Loop.** The Trans-Siberian is too long, so Russia buys a shortcut straight across Chinese Manchuria and a warm-water terminus at the end of it → the branch line and the port are the entire reason two empires have armies in a third country's provinces → Portsmouth hands Japan the southern half of the line, plus the treaty right to station guards along it → those guards are a garrison with a railway to protect and no one to answer to, and by 1919 they have a name, the Kwantung Army → on 18 September 1931 that army blows up a few feet of its own track outside Mukden and uses the hole to take Manchuria, which is where the Second World War in Asia starts.
- **Lander.** Harbin. Ten million people live in a city that exists because Russian engineers needed a junction for the shortcut in 1898; its Russian Orthodox cathedral is still standing in the middle of it, and the ice festival every tourist itinerary lists is held in the town the railway built. Nobody's textbook says a city that size is a by-product of a route.
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 classrooms hold both ends (US, UK); 0 of 11 join 1905 to 1931 through the track; 0 of 11 name the Kwantung Army in the 1905 context; 1 of 11 (CN) names the ground and not the war.
- **Receipts-lite.** Chinese Eastern Railway concession, 1896; Portsmouth, 5 September 1905; the Mukden Incident, 18 September 1931 — commemorated at the 9·18 Historical Museum in Shenyang, built on the site; the line still runs as the Harbin–Dalian corridor. <!-- CHECK: CER concession 1896; Kwantung Army formally constituted 1919; 9.18 museum, Shenyang -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** The chapter's question is whose ground it was. This answers it with an object that is still on the ground: the rails. It also braids forward twice — to ch. 4 (Japan enters 1914 “emboldened by its success against Russia”) and to ch. 13.

### Pitch 2: Tsushima made every battleship in the world worthless *(my candidate — the non-obvious one)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Lowe's time chart of the causes of the First World War, p. 7, prints these lines two apart with nothing between them: “1904–5 Russo-Japanese War, won by Japan” / “1905–6 Moroccan Crisis” / “1906 Britain builds first ‘Dreadnought’ battleship.” The causal link is typographically adjacent and textually absent. Four hundred pages later the same book supplies the consequence and still no cause: “the introduction of the powerful British ‘Dreadnought’ battleship in 1906 changed all this because it made all other battleships obsolete. This meant that the Germans could begin building ‘Dreadnoughts’ on equal terms with Britain” (UK, §1.4 “What caused the war, and who was to blame?”, p. 13 — printed index: “Dreadnoughts 7, 9, 13, 25–7”). And the Russian volume — the one whose fleet was sunk — puts the Dreadnought in a footnote with a different father entirely: «созданный в ответ на морскую программу Германии», “created in response to Germany's naval programme” (RU, extract p. 26).
- **Loop.** At Tsushima, Japanese ships hit at ranges where secondary guns are useless and only the biggest guns matter, and two fleets settle a war in an afternoon → the lesson, read closely in London, is that a battleship should carry nothing but big guns → HMS *Dreadnought* is laid down and launched in a year, and on the day she floats every existing battleship in every navy, including Britain's own overwhelming lead, is second-rate → the naval race that Lowe calls “the main bone of contention between the two right up to 1914” is only runnable because a defeat in the Yellow Sea reset the scoreboard to nearly zero.
- **Lander.** The weakest of the four, and it should be judged on that. The honest candidate: the wrecks of the German fleet scuttled at Scapa Flow in 1919 are one of the world's few sources of steel smelted before 1945, and it is still salvaged for instruments that have to measure very faint radiation. The steel that was poured because of Tsushima is the steel we now use precisely because it has never seen a bomb. <!-- CHECK: low-background steel; German High Seas Fleet scuttled 21 June 1919; salvage of Scapa Flow wrecks for pre-atomic steel -->
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 classrooms print the Dreadnought (UK, RU); 1 gives it a cause and the cause is Germany; 0 of 11 connect it to Tsushima, which 2 of 11 name at all (IT, and IN as “Japanese navy defeats Russian fleet”).
- **Why it fits this chapter.** It is the cleanest thing this corpus has ever handed us: a missing causal edge visible as white space on a printed page, in the book that is most confident about the causes of 1914. It braids directly into ch. 4 and it turns the chapter's “end of the myth of European supremacy” into something mechanical rather than atmospheric.

### Pitch 3: Five flags over one harbour *(strongest lander)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Belarus is the only book in the corpus that prints both names of the place: Japan received “the right to lease the Chinese ports of Port Arthur and Dalny (now — Lüshun and Dalian)” (BY, extract p. 71). China's own volume names the same ground and only the ground: “Russia forcibly leased Lüda,” with a footnote — “Lüda refers to the Lüshunkou and Dalian Bay area” (CN, printed p. 115) — and then never says who took it in 1905. And Ukraine's volume, which begins in 1945 and has no business in this chapter at all, turns out to hold the last chapter of the harbour's story: “Military bases in Port Arthur (China) and Porkkala-Udd (Finland) were liquidated” (UA).
- **Loop.** One harbour, chosen because it does not freeze: Chinese to 1898 → leased to Russia for 25 years and renamed Port Arthur and Dalny → besieged for eleven months and taken by Japan, the lease transferred at Portsmouth in 1905 → Japanese for forty years, the base from which Manchuria is administered → taken by the Soviet Army in August 1945 and held as a Soviet naval base → handed back to China in the mid-1950s, which is the sentence the Ukrainian textbook prints without knowing it is an ending → today Lüshunkou is a district of Dalian, a city of about seven million and one of the busiest container ports in the world.
- **Lander.** Open a map application and type Dalian. Then zoom to the tip of the peninsula and read the district name. The war has no monument in five of the six textbooks and a postcode in all of them.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 gives both the old and current names (BY); 1 of 11 names the ground and omits the war fought on it (CN); 1 of 11 names the harbour only at the moment the last foreign flag leaves (UA, out of scope for everything else here); 0 of 11 run the sequence.
- **Receipts-lite.** Russo-Chinese Convention, 27 March 1898; Treaty of Portsmouth, 5 September 1905; Sino-Soviet agreement of 1950 and the handover completed in 1955; the Russian and Japanese cemeteries at Lüshun are still there and open. <!-- CHECK: Soviet withdrawal from Port Arthur completed May 1955; Lüshun Russian cemetery extant -->
- **Caution.** This is a place-story, not a causal loop, so it risks reading as a list. It may work better as a three-sentence echo inside Pitch 1 than as the thread itself.

### Pitch 4: The phrase that got its date stamped *(linguistic)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Sabbatucci–Vidotto do something no other book in the corpus does: they give a slur a birth certificate and a boom year. “A ‘yellow peril’: an expression coined by the emperor of Germany William II at the time of the Boxer revolt and become topical above all after the Russo-Japanese war of 1904-5” (IT, p. 235). One page later the same book says what 1905 destroyed — “the myth of the military and technological supremacy of the old continent and that of a presumed superiority of the ‘white race’” (IT, p. 236). Lowe makes the identical historical claim with neither the phrase nor the race in it: “the first time that an Asian country had defeated one of the European great powers” (UK, p. 336).
- **Loop.** A defeat that broke one myth manufactured demand for its inverse: an emperor's coinage from 1895 sits unused until Tsushima makes it feel like a forecast → in the decade after 1905 it moves out of chancelleries and into legislation across the settler world — school segregation orders and a “gentlemen's agreement” in the United States, the dictation test that ran the White Australia policy, and finally the exclusion clauses of the US Immigration Act of 1924 → in 1919 Japan proposes a racial-equality clause at Versailles and it is voted down, a rebuff OpenStax reaches back for when it explains why Japan fought the way it did in the 1930s (US, p. 525).
- **Lander.** The phrase itself, still legible in English, Italian, Russian and German with the same two words. It is the rare case where the object the reader can hold is a piece of vocabulary — and this book has already argued that vocabulary is what textbooks nationalize.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 dates the phrase and names its author (IT); 1 of 11 names the “white race” myth (IT); 1 of 11 makes the same claim with the race removed (UK); 1 of 11 carries the Versailles rebuff forward (US); 0 of 11 join them.
- **Caution.** This is the pitch most likely to slide into the “look what they hid from you” register CONTEXT.md bans. Written cold it is a straight etymology with dates; written warm it becomes a sermon. Only worth writing if the wonder is genuinely in the mechanism — a myth and its mirror image manufactured by the same defeat.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (the railway)** first. It answers the chapter's own question — whose ground — with an
object still bolted to that ground, it explains the war's cause and its longest consequence with the
same piece of iron, and Harbin is a lander every corpus country can look at without needing to care
about 1905. **Pitch 2 (Tsushima → Dreadnought)** is the most original finding in the set and the best
braid to ch. 4; its lander is thin, and if the pitch round likes the finding but not the tonic-water
test, the right move is to run it as two sentences inside Pitch 1 rather than force the steel.
**Pitch 3** has the best lander per word and the least new history in it. **Pitch 4** is the one to
bank rather than write — it belongs closer to ch. 5, where Versailles gives it a second act.
