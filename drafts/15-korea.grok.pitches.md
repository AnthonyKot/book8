### Pitch (a): The half-hour border
- **Seed**: Every covering book that narrates the war needs the 38th parallel as furniture (UA: division “along the 38th parallel”; CN scare-quotes the “38th Parallel” as the line the US crossed; IT/UK/US build invasion and counter-crossing on it). None of them tell how the line was chosen.
- **Loop**: On the night of 10–11 August 1945, with Soviet troops already entering the peninsula and Japan collapsing, two US Army colonels (Dean Rusk and Charles Bonesteel) were given about thirty minutes to propose a US–Soviet occupation boundary; they picked the 38th parallel off a National Geographic map because it put Seoul in the American zone. <!-- CHECK: Rusk/Bonesteel 38th parallel decision Aug 10–11 1945; National Geographic map; ~30 minutes — standard diplomatic history --> Temporary occupation hardened into two states (1948), a war (1950–53), an armistice line, and the DMZ — among the most fortified borders on earth.
- **Lander**: The line is still on every news map; a large share of the world’s smartphones and consumer electronics are built in factories clustered south of it (Seoul–Suwon–Gyeonggi corridor, ~50 km from the DMZ). <!-- CHECK: major Samsung/LG/etc. production and HQ geography relative to DMZ --> The reader’s pocket is wired to a border drawn in half an hour.
- **Corpus count hook**: 0 of 7 covering classrooms tell the Rusk–Bonesteel story; all of them use the parallel.
- **Risk**: Enrichment is general history (correct for a thread), not a textbook silence about a fact they “hid” — tone must stay wonder, not sneer.

### Pitch (b): The war that never ended
- **Seed (verified)**: UA p. 172 — «Хоча його вдалося зупинити, сторони досі перебувають у стані війни.» / EN discovery: “Although it was possible to stop it, the parties are still in a state of war.” IN and CN stop at “armistice” / “forced to sign”; BY has only the date span; only UA among the seven states the legal–political unfinishedness in plain language.
- **Loop**: July 1953 produces a ceasefire and a Demilitarized Zone, not a peace treaty; two Korean states, US forces still in the South, and periodic crises keep the armistice as the operating system of the peninsula. The “end” of the war in school timelines is a freeze, not a settlement.
- **Lander**: “Still at war” is not metaphor — it is the status UA alone among these books says out loud, and it is why Korea remains a permanent item in any adult’s news diet (missile tests, summits, DMZ theater).
- **Corpus count hook**: 1 of 7 (UA) states ongoing state of war; the armistice date appears more widely.
- **Risk**: Strong shared hook (everyone sees Korea in the news); weaker “object in the hand” than tea or a phone. Works as lander if the news-habit is accepted as the cup-of-tea equivalent.

### Pitch (c): Koryo-saram — 1937 → Tsoi
- **Seed**: KZ national history (not a ladder column) alone in the corpus treats Far Eastern Koreans as a Soviet deportation story: mass relocation to Kazakhstan from 1937, Korean theater at Kzyl-Orda, later “South Korea” as investor. The war dossiers show KZ has zero 1950–53 campaign pages — the Korean story in that classroom is 1937, not 1950.
- **Loop**: Stalin’s NKVD deports Soviet Koreans from the Far East to Central Asia (1937) on espionage pretexts → a Korean-speaking diaspora (Koryo-saram) takes root in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan → late-Soviet culture gets Viktor Tsoi (Kino), the most famous face of that lineage for a generation that lived across the old USSR. <!-- CHECK: Tsoi’s paternal line Koryo-saram; birth 1962 Leningrad; Kino peak 1980s -->
- **Lander**: A song still played in kitchens from Almaty to Kyiv sits on a deportation the war chapters never mention.
- **Corpus count hook**: Signature-topic finding — KZ alone; 0 of 7 war-covering world-history books connect 1937 to 1950.
- **Risk (flag honestly)**: May fail the **shared-across-corpus** test. Tsoi lands hard for readers from post-Soviet space (UA/BY/RU/KZ orbits) and lightly or not at all for IN/CN/IT/UK/US readers. ### Pitch (d) [optional add]: The war-name that is a verdict
- **Seed**: CN’s war title 抗美援朝 (“War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea”) vs IN/BY/UK/US “Korean War” / UA “war in Korea” / IT “guerra di Corea” — the ladder’s vocabulary axis, not only its agency axis.
- **Loop**: Naming the war is already a theory of who aggressed; CN folds cause, enemy, and duty into the official label, then closes with forced US signature and national victory. Other classrooms keep a geographic label and fight over the invasion sentence instead.
- **Lander**: The phrase still appears on Chinese monuments, museums, and anniversary coverage; a reader who only ever met “Korean War” has been using one side’s neutral geography for a war another side names as resistance. <!-- CHECK: 抗美援朝 still official PRC commemorative name -->
- **Corpus count hook**: 1 of 7 (CN) uses the full resistance title as the war’s name of record.
- **Risk**: More linguistic than material; strong for readers who have seen Chinese state media, thinner as a pocket object.
