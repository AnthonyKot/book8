India 1930–1947

At midnight on 14–15 August 1947, British India becomes two dominions: India and Pakistan. In the weeks around that hour, millions of people cross new lines on a map, and the death toll of the crossings will never be agreed. Salt still sits on kitchen tables in every country in this corpus; a tax on that salt was once a pillar of the colonial budget. What seven classrooms do with the long road to that midnight — who acts in the sentence that ends the Raj — is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who made India independent — Britain granting, or who took it?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • The Indian National Congress (INC) is founded in 1885; the All-India Muslim League in 1906. Mohandas K. Gandhi returns from South Africa in 1915 and becomes the central figure of a mass non-cooperation and civil-disobedience movement.
  • 13 April 1919: troops open fire on a crowd in the Jallianwala Bagh enclosure at Amritsar, Punjab. Body counts in later sources and textbooks vary (hundreds to over a thousand killed).
  • 12 March – early April 1930: Gandhi’s Salt March (Dandi March) defies the colonial salt monopoly; tens of thousands are arrested in the civil-disobedience campaign that follows.
  • Government of India Act 1935 expands provincial self-government. In 1937 provincial elections, Congress forms governments in a majority of provinces.
  • August 1942: Congress launches the Quit India movement; British authorities arrest Gandhi and other Congress leaders for most of the remaining war.
  • After 1945, a Labour government in London presses for transfer of power. Negotiations fail to keep a unitary state; the Indian Independence Act partitions British India. Power is transferred in mid-August 1947; communal violence and mass migration accompany partition; Gandhi is assassinated in January 1948.
  • Who made independence — a British decision, Indian struggle, wartime exhaustion, American pressure, or some mix — is not a shared classroom fact. The books below share a year more than a subject.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators who cover the end of British India, ordered from thinnest agency (independence that is simply attained) to thickest (named ministers, a staged debate over gift versus struggle, a viceroy who decides on partition). China’s national history, Germany’s NS booklet, and Sudan’s regional syllabus never open this row as Indian history; Kazakhstan is national history and is never a world-event peer column. Those absences are syllabus boundaries, not empty verdicts.

3 The tell

Nobody in this set denies that British rule in India ends in 1947, or that two states replace one colony. The disagreement is about the subject of the verb that ends the Raj — and about which earlier blood the classroom is willing to count.

Belarus writes the purest agentless close: India and Pakistan attained independence, as weather attains rain. The struggle has already been given its page — satyagraha, shop closures, English troops opening fire, Gandhi insisting on nonviolence — and then the 1947 sentence drops every actor. India is the largest colony; Gandhi is its spiritual leader; independence arrives as a date. NCERT, the self-pole of this chapter, does something colder. In Themes in World History the entire 1930–47 story is a timeline column: Non-Cooperation launched, Quit India, “become independent.” No Amritsar, no Salt March date, no partition death toll, no Mountbatten. The Indian classroom that is supposed to own this story, in the world-history book of this corpus, treats it as a list of labels and points the continuous narrative to a different course. That is not absence of national memory. It is a division of labour that leaves the comparative reader with the thinnest self-account on the board.

Russia and the United States share a grant frame and then diverge on what Britain leaves behind. Чубарьян’s Britain “made a decision to grant”; the next sentence divides the country; the next credits England with leaving democracy — independence as a constitutional bequest. OpenStax’s Britain also “granted,” but the reasons pile up as weakness: anti-British protests, wartime exhaustion, uncertain loyalty of Indian troops, and finally American pressure as creditor and ally that had already freed the Philippines. The United States enters the independence sentence as a second agent. That is a signature move of the American column, not a consensus fact of the set.

Ukraine opens only at 1947 and puts a sharper edge on the metropolis. Congress wants unity; the Muslim League wants a separate state and “found support from the metropolis.” The colonial administration then “announced the granting” of independence while dividing the country. Britain is not a tired landlord leaving a gift; it is a named backer of partition’s political demand. The resettlement dead are “about 100 thousand” — an order of magnitude below Lowe’s Punjab figure, and the kind of gap that is data rather than a problem to smooth away.

“Independence was not a gift from the British, it was ‘the hard-won fruit of struggle and sacrifice’.” UK · Lowe, Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed. · quoting the challenge from Indian historians Sarkar and Inder Singh · ~p. 758

Italy and the United Kingdom put Indians back in the grammar, in different keys. Sabbatucci–Vidotto have Congress wresting a dominion promise from the English; separation is “finally accorded” after conflict; the “epic” of peaceful liberation ends with over 100,000 dead and 17 million transferred. Lowe alone stages the historiographical fight inside the chapter: official British sources as careful preparation for independence; Indian historians answering that the Acts of 1919 and 1935 were designed to postpone it; the quoted verdict that independence was “the hard-won fruit of struggle and sacrifice.” Then the British agents return with names — Bevin, Attlee, Wavell, Mountbatten — and Mountbatten “decided that partition was the only way.” The imperial self-pole is the only column that both names its own ministers and prints the accusation that their gift story is false. That is distance used as method, not as alibi.

One earlier square sorts candor from silence. Amritsar, April 1919, is the known three-way for this chapter. Search Lowe end to end: zero hits for Amritsar or Jallianwala. Search NCERT’s world-history volume: zero. OpenStax writes the fire: “the British Indian Army opened fire on the protestors, who had little chance of escape from the closed-in space. Hundreds were killed and more than a thousand wounded.” Italy has English troops who “bloodily repressed” a demonstration; “the dead were almost 400.” Russia goes higher: “over 1 thousand people were killed and about 2 thousand wounded,” and calls it a “bloody slaughter” meant as a warning. Belarus never names the city; it only has English troops opening fire while Gandhi insists on nonviolence. The imperial self-pole omits the massacre that the American survey and the Italian manual both put on the page. The Indian self-pole’s world-history book omits it too — not because Indian schools never teach Amritsar, but because this corpus edition chose a timeline and left the square to another shelf. CONTEXT expected UK near-zero, US candor, IN as self-pole. The dossier’s receipt is harsher on the self-pole column than the exhibit card: zero in this book, not a fuller national account.

US alone, among these seven, walks the Salt March to the Arabian Sea and numbers the ~60,000 arrests. US alone keeps Quit India as a wartime detention story with leaders held “through most of the war.” The American grant sentence at the end does not erase that earlier Indian agency; it just refuses to let struggle be the only verb in 1947. Lowe names Jinnah’s slogan “Pakistan or Perish” and Calcutta’s 5,000 dead in August 1946; no other column on this ladder needs the slogan. The words that survive in the mouth after school ends are not evenly distributed.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the credit line for this event — against the grain of the imperial self-pole. Not because the book is kind to Indian nationalism, and not because it remembers Amritsar (it does not), but because it does a job no other column attempts: it puts the British gift narrative and the Indian “hard-won fruit” challenge in the same lesson, names the historians, and then still names Attlee, Bevin, Mountbatten, and the Punjab dead at ~250,000. Candor about one’s own state’s story is the scarce resource this book tracks. Here the market survey from the former metropole spends it on 1947 even while it spends none on 1919.

OpenStax (US) takes the depth lead on the road, not on the exit. Salt March, Amritsar with closed-in space, Quit India arrests through the war, Bengal famine as fuel for independence, naval mutiny, American pressure — the only column that makes 1930 and 1942 load-bearing instead of optional colour. Its 1947 verb remains “granted.” The survey is honest about cost and still protective of the transfer as a British act.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns a third nod for refusing a happy ending: peaceful means, then 100,000 dead and 17 million moved, in one breath. Distance without a national ego in the story lets the contradiction stay on the page.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is seven books whose syllabus reaches Indian independence as a world-history topic. It is not “the world,” and it is not seven countries’ full curricula. China’s 纲要 is a national history that meets India as ancient Buddhism, Opium-War commerce, and 1950s diplomacy — not as 1930–47. Germany’s booklet is a National Socialism primer. Sudan’s secondary history is regional; Gandhi appears once as a foreign model admired by Sudanese intellectuals, not as a chapter on India. Those zeros are other syllabuses. Kazakhstan is never a world-event peer column here.

NCERT’s thinness is a genre fact inside the Indian system, not a claim that Indian schools skip the national movement. Class XI Themes in World History is a world survey; the continuous narrative of the freedom struggle lives in the separate Themes in Indian History course. This chapter measures the world-history book that entered the corpus. Calling IN “timeline only” describes that book, not a nation without a memory of Dandi or Amritsar.

Body counts are kept verbatim because errors — or at least disagreements — are data. Amritsar: OpenStax “hundreds” killed; Italy “almost 400”; Russia “over 1 thousand.” Partition: Ukraine “about 100 thousand” in resettlement clashes; Italy “over 100,000” plus 17 million transferred; Lowe “about 250 000” murdered in the Punjab alone. Scholarly ranges for partition deaths run higher still. No column here is a demographic history of 1947.

Machine-translated discovery files led to the pages. Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Italian lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English for the reader. Page numbers for Lowe follow the edition’s independence section (§24.2); form-feed pagination in the extract should be reopened against the printed book before any line becomes a hard public claim. None of these classrooms is a full history of princely states, caste, gender, or the northeast; if you want those, every column will leave you unsatisfied — including the thickest.
