The Great Depression

In late October 1929, on the New York Stock Exchange, share prices that had been climbing for years fall hard enough that textbooks still date a world crisis from those days. Factories slow. Banks close. Unemployment climbs into the millions. What each classroom does with that sequence — whether the crash is weather, a system, a policy failure, or only the fuse for something else — is the subject of this chapter. A later generation still lives with pieces of the answer: deposit insurance, old-age pensions, a federal minimum wage, and the habit of calling a deep slump a “depression.”

The question · causes

What caused the crash — and who is standing in the sentence when the world economy falls?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • October 1929: a sharp collapse in New York stock prices (often taught as Black Thursday, 24 October, and Black Tuesday, 29 October) marks the conventional start of a multi-year economic crisis centered on the United States and spreading through trade, credit, and commodity markets.
  • The crisis years commonly dated 1929–1933 (sometimes into the later 1930s) bring industrial collapse, bank failures, falling farm prices, and mass unemployment in the United States and much of Europe; world trade contracts sharply.
  • Herbert Hoover is U.S. president when the crash arrives (elected 1928). Franklin D. Roosevelt takes office in March 1933 and launches a package of banking, relief, and regulatory measures remembered as the New Deal.
  • Historians treat the crash as a trigger sitting on deeper problems — overproduction, unequal income, credit and speculation, agricultural glut, protectionist tariffs — rather than as a single cause acting alone.
  • Political consequences are part of the same decade’s story in many curricula: radical movements gain ground; the Nazi Party’s rise in Germany is routinely linked to the crisis; Japan’s 1931 move into Manchuria is often linked to depression-era scarcity politics.
  • Most survey treatments date a fuller return toward prosperity to the economic stimulation of rearmament and the Second World War, not to New Deal measures alone.
  • Who or what caused the fall — capitalism as such, a policy choice, a structural “disbalance,” or nobody — is not a shared classroom fact. The books below share a calendar more than a grammar.

3 The tell

Nobody on this ladder denies that something large went wrong after 1929. The disagreement is about the sentence that makes the crash start — and about who, if anyone, is allowed to be a subject once it has.

The top of the ladder is weather. India’s NCERT gives two beats and no mechanism: the exchange “crashes,” the Depression “follows,” twelve million “are out of work.” A later line has the USA “swept by” a depression that “affected all its people.” Nobody overproduces, nobody sells, nobody fails to act. China is equally agentless on the onset — a “worldwide economic crisis broke out” — and then spends the agency it has on a different story: the Japanese ruling clique, eager to launch a war of aggression against China. The crisis is not a chapter; it is a fuse for Manchuria. Belarus keeps the purest passive construction in the full-treatment set: “a crash occurred.” Causes arrive later as overstocking and demand that “did not correspond” to production growth — a structure without a named firm, tariff, or president. Recovery is equally impersonal: “Western democracies… stepped onto the path of state regulation.” No New Deal. No Roosevelt in the recovery paragraph. An exercise asks why the crisis “did not affect the economy of the Soviet state.” The exception is a homework problem, not a named American program.

Sudan is the only column that makes the crisis land somewhere other than New York or “the West.” Cotton is “affected by the capitalist market”; Sudan “was indeed affected” when demand fell; the government cut labor and salaries. The British Administration cuts Sudanese graduates’ pay from eight pounds to five and a half and does not, the book notes elsewhere, cut British and foreign salaries the same way. Capitalism appears as a market the colony is priced by, not as a stage of a system a Russian textbook can diagnose from the center. The patient of the sentence is Gezira cotton and Gordon College — not Wall Street.

«Падение стоимости акций компаний и банкротство банков подорвали „кровеносную систему“ капитализма — финансовый рынок и банковскую систему.»

“The fall in the value of companies’ shares and the bankruptcy of banks undermined the ‘bloodstream’ of capitalism — the financial market and the banking system.”

RU · Чубарьян 2023 · §9 · ~p. 76

Russia is the only full chapter that calls the event a crisis of a stage of the capitalist system and still gives presidents verbs. Shares “fall”; prosperity “collapses and drags”; the cause is a “disbalance” in production–distribution–consumption; concentration of production creates monopoly conditions. Then Hoover “remained inactive,” convinced prosperity was “somewhere around the corner,” and Roosevelt promises a “new deal for the forgotten man.” Later the same book records that conservatives accused Roosevelt of planting socialism, and that Roosevelt himself “considered that he had saved capitalism.” On another page the crisis is a wave: “On this wave, Nazis came to power in Germany.” The grammar does two jobs at once — system diagnosis without a sole villain on the exchange floor, then named American agents for failure and rescue.

Italy and the United Kingdom put policy where Russia put stage. Sabbatucci–Vidotto keep an agentless outbreak (“having broken out”) and then indict the United States for refusing the “responsibilities connected to the role of hegemonic power,” defending production by protectionism, and suspending foreign credits. The recession spreads “with the significant exception of the USSR.” Hoover “had not achieved any success”; Roosevelt announces a New Deal. Lowe is the densest English cause-machine: people “rushed to sell”; Black Tuesday ruins thousands; and then the book’s load-bearing line — the Wall Street Crash “did not cause the depression; it was just a symptom.” Four deeper causes follow (overproduction, unequal income, falling exports/tariffs, speculation). Homeless camps are “Hoovervilles.” The New Deal is pledged and named. War, not the Deal alone, “finally put an end to the depression.” OpenStax refuses single-cause stories (“No economic depression is caused by one element alone”), names speculation, bank failures, credit defaults, overproduction/underconsumption, unequal wealth, and Smoot–Hawley by statute title, puts ordinary families in the patient slot (80 percent had no savings), and then gives Roosevelt a comprehensive New Deal — with a sidebar that FDIC, Social Security, and the federal minimum wage still assist people in the United States today. Both UK and US close the prosperity loop with the war’s economic stimulation. That is not a victory coda for rearmament; it is a limit on what recovery programs are said to have finished.

Labels split the way agency does. Russia and Belarus teach “world economic crisis” and “Great Depression” as a pair. Italy prefers “great crisis” and “great crash,” with “grande depressione” for the aftermath. Lowe stacks “Wall Street Crash,” “Great Depression,” and “world economic crisis.” OpenStax says “Great Depression.” China says “worldwide economic crisis” and never needs Wall Street’s name on the Manchuria page. Sudan says “global economic crisis” and never says Depression. The noun is not neutral inventory. It is a claim about whether the event is a U.S. drama, a world structure, or a local cotton price.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the credit line for treating causes as a syllabus, not a slogan. Only this classroom prints the sentence the comparison needs most: the crash “did not cause the depression; it was just a symptom,” then walks overproduction, unequal income, tariffs, and speculation as separate problems. It names Hoover without turning him into the sole origin, keeps “Hoovervilles” as popular blame, and refuses to let the New Deal close the story alone — “It was the war that finally put an end to the depression.” Distance from 1929 becomes equipment: a market survey that still has to explain why shares fell in someone else’s city.

OpenStax (US) earns the self-pole honesty prize. Multi-cause framing, Smoot–Hawley by name, world GDP and trade figures, and the 80-percent-no-savings line put ordinary households in the grammar. The “New Deal in a New Century” sidebar is the only place in this set that admits the crisis left institutions still running — FDIC, Social Security, federal minimum wage — so the chapter is not only about 1929 but about paperwork a reader may still hold. That is candor about one’s own state’s long answer, not self-congratulation: the same section says only wartime stimulation brought the world closer to prosperity.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) supplies the sharpest international-responsibility clause: the USA as economic hegemon that chose protectionism over system stability, plus the USSR as “significant exception.” Чубарьян (RU) is the system-diagnosis lead — “bloodstream of capitalism,” stage crisis, disbalance chain, Hoover inactive, Roosevelt’s new deal, Nazis “on this wave” — the densest single packaging of structure and agents. MoE Sudan (SD) is the geography lead: the only classroom that makes cotton, Gezira, and a graduate salary the patient of a world crisis instead of a footnote to New York.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight books whose syllabus reaches 1929 as a world economic event. It is not “the world,” and it is not eight countries as eternal types — it is eight pinned editions. Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945; Germany’s federal booklet is a National Socialism primer for 1939–45 reckoning. Against an 8-of-8 in-scope covering baseline (dossier), those two zeros are syllabus boundaries, not avoidance of the Depression. Kazakhstan is national history and is never a peer column on world events.

Machine-translated discovery files led us to the pages. Russian, Belarusian, Chinese, Italian, and Arabic lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English here for the reader. Printed page numbers follow footers in the extracts where visible (RU §9 ~pp. 76–77, §10 ~p. 84; BY ~pp. 123–124; CN ~p. 150; US printed 491–494). UK page numbers for Lowe follow the dossier’s print references (~489–498) and should be re-opened against the PDF before hard citation. Sudanese Arabic is ligature-mangled in extraction; every Arabic quotation here has been reconstructed to standard orthography and must be checked against the printed page before shipping.

Kept as measurement, not as gotcha: unemployment figures do not match across columns — BY “about 13 million” U.S. unemployed by 1933; IT “14 million” U.S. and “15 million” Europe; UK “around 14 million”; IN timeline “by 1932, 12 million.” The books are counting at different dates with different methods; the spread is data about classrooms, not a puzzle for the reader to “solve.” Belarus never names the New Deal in the recovery paragraph that credits “state regulation”; that silence is a finding about what the sentence needs, not proof that the edition denies Roosevelt existed (he appears later for the “good neighbor” policy). Russia’s “saved capitalism” line and the exercise “Why did it not affect the Soviet state?” are both kept verbatim because errors-are-data applies to framing choices as much as to misprints: what a classroom asks is part of what it teaches.

Finally, none of these books is a full economic history of the 1930s. Six of the eight are political narratives with an economic chapter; Sudan is a national syllabus that meets the crisis as cotton and salaries; India’s world-history volume meets it as a timeline tick and a one-line backdrop to indigenous policy. If you want bank balance sheets, Federal Reserve minutes, or village-level price series, every column will leave you unsatisfied — including Lowe’s cause seminar and OpenStax’s still-living-programs sidebar.
