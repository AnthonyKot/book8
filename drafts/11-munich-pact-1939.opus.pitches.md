# Chapter 11 (Munich → Pact → September 1939) — Thread Pitches

Four candidates. #1 is the obvious one and it is genuinely strong; #2 and #4 are the
non-obvious ones the comparison turned up; #3 is a method thread. Guinea-pig gate:
whichever gets a real reaction gets written into the placeholder. No reaction, no thread.

---

### Pitch 1: The only word from 1938 still in daily use — "appeasement"

- **Seed (from the comparison)**: Lowe (UK) is the only book that stops and defines the
  term — "Appeasement was the policy followed by the British, and later by the French, of
  avoiding war with aggressive powers… by giving way to their demands" — and OpenStax (US,
  p. 531) goes further, teaching the word's *afterlife*: using Munich "to warn against the
  perils of allowing one nation to invade another without opposition, whether it be
  Hitler's Germany or Putin's Russia, is known as invoking the Munich Analogy." A 2022
  American school textbook is teaching 1938 as an argumentative move students will meet
  in the news this week.
- **Loop**: Munich was meant to settle a border dispute; what it actually produced was a
  permanent rhetorical weapon. Within two decades "another Munich" was being used to
  justify Suez (1956), then Vietnam, then the Falklands, then Iraq (2003), then policy on
  Ukraine — by all sides, in every direction, including by people who wanted the opposite
  of what the last user wanted. The word outlived every party to the agreement, and it is
  now used far more often as an accusation than as a description.
- **Lander**: The reader has almost certainly heard someone in a comment thread, a
  parliament, or a pub accuse someone else of appeasement — and has now met the room where
  the word was manufactured, and the fact that even the classroom that invented the policy
  keeps a numbered definition of it in the textbook.
- **Shared?** Yes — all six in-scope classrooms carry the word (RU and BY in scare quotes,
  «умиротворение»; DE as "sogenannte Appeasement-Politik"; IT in English inside Italian).

### Pitch 2 (non-obvious): "Poland" as a verb

- **Seed (from the comparison)**: NCERT (IN) is the one in-scope world-history book with
  **zero** mentions of Munich, the Pact or September 1939 — and it is also the only book in
  the whole corpus where the word "Poland" appears as a *verb*. In the China chapter,
  p. 167: "The partition of Poland in the eighteenth century was a much-discussed example.
  So much so that by the late 1890s it came to be used as a verb: 'to Poland us' (bolan
  wo)." The book that skips the fourth partition of Poland is the one that preserves the
  word coined for the first three.
- **Loop**: Poland was carved up three times in the 1790s and vanished from the map for 123
  years → by the 1890s Chinese reformers watching their own coastline being sliced into
  concessions turned the country's name into a verb meaning *to be dismembered by
  neighbours who agreed with each other in private* → 1939 does it a fourth time, under a
  protocol whose text is not published → and in 1945 the Allies do not put the old Poland
  back but slide the entire country roughly 200 km west, keeping the 1939 eastern line and
  paying for it with German territory. <!-- CHECK: Curzon-line-based eastern border and
  Oder–Neisse western border settled at Yalta/Potsdam 1945; roughly 200 km westward shift -->
- **Lander**: Today's map. Wrocław was Breslau, Gdańsk was Danzig, Szczecin was Stettin —
  and Lviv, Hrodna and Brest are on the other side of a border drawn in a secret protocol
  and confirmed by the powers that had condemned it. A reader who has flown into Wrocław
  or watched a news map of Ukraine's western border has been looking at the 17 September
  line the entire time.
- **Why it earns its place**: the "missing piece" is that 1939 was not the first partition
  and not the last redrawing — and the comparison found the evidence in the *silence*: the
  book with nothing to say about 1939 is the one carrying the vocabulary 1939 belongs to.

### Pitch 3 (non-obvious, method): The same defence, in three genres

- **Seed (from the comparison)**: one argument — *the Pact bought the USSR time* — appears
  in three classrooms in three different grammatical costumes. RU (Чубарьян 2023) states it
  as fact: «Пакт о ненападении был вынужденным шагом со стороны СССР» ("a forced step").
  BY (Кошелев 2021) prints it as a **primary source to be compared** — Stalin's radio
  speech of 3 July 1941 ("we secured our country peace for a year and a half") set beside
  Churchill's memoir conceding the policy was "realistic in the highest degree," with the
  student asked why the two assessments differ. UK (Lowe 2013) reports it as **somebody
  else's position**: "Russian historians justify the pact on the grounds that it gave the
  USSR time to prepare its defences."
- **Loop**: how a wartime radio address becomes a school fact. Stalin's 1941 justification
  was written to explain a broken pact to a country already invaded; it was reprinted, then
  taught, then inherited by textbooks in three countries as, respectively, a conclusion, an
  exercise, and a foreign opinion. The same sentence, three jobs.
- **Lander**: weaker and honest about it — the reader's own school memory of "what we were
  told versus what we were asked." Best if the human wants a thread about *how textbooks
  are built* rather than about the war. Could also be banked for the book's synthesis half.

### Pitch 4 (non-obvious): A date that is a day off work

- **Seed (from the comparison)**: the ladder's most agentless row ends with an object no
  other row has — a public holiday. BY (p. 158): «Этот день в Беларуси стал государственным
  праздником — Днем народного единства.» The 17 September that the German booklet calls an
  order to annex is, in the country whose textbook opens this ladder, a date on the
  national calendar. <!-- CHECK: Belarus Day of National Unity (17 September) established
  by presidential decree in 2021, first marked 2021 -->
- **Loop**: a military order in 1939 → a border in 1945 → a state holiday in 2021 → a
  paragraph in a 2021 school textbook that has to make the holiday make sense. The
  causality runs backwards here: the calendar came first and the textbook sentence was
  written to fit it. That is the cleanest live demonstration in the whole book of the
  thesis that textbooks nationalise causality — and it happened three years ago, not eighty.
- **Lander**: a day off work, this year, with a name on it. Every reader has holidays whose
  origin they have never checked; this is one where the origin is on the ladder above.
- **Caveat for the gate**: this lands hardest for readers in the region and lands as
  *observation* rather than *recognition* elsewhere — it may fail AGENT.md's "shared hook"
  test. Strongest as a half-paragraph inside the tell rather than as the thread itself.

## Recommendation

**#2 (Poland as a verb)** if the human wants the thread the comparison alone could have
found — it is built out of an omission, it lands on a map every reader has seen, and it is
the only candidate that makes 1939 legible as one move in a 150-year sequence.
**#1** is the safe, certain-to-land alternative and is the AGENT.md "living vocabulary"
archetype. #4 is the best half-paragraph in the chapter either way; #3 is banked.
