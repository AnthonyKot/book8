Munich → Pact → September 1939

On 30 September 1938 a British prime minister waves a sheet of paper and speaks of peace. On 23 August 1939 two foreign ministers sign another paper in Moscow. On 1 September German troops enter Poland; on 17 September Soviet troops enter from the east. The dates are almost never in dispute. What each classroom needs those papers and those armies to mean is the subject of this chapter — and of the word appeasement, which still walks into ordinary arguments without bringing its passport.

The question · causality

What caused the war — and what does your textbook need Munich and the Pact to be?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. Three stations, one war’s opening. The ladder measures the naming of the third.

  • 29–30 September 1938: Munich Agreement. Britain, France, Germany, and Italy settle the Sudetenland crisis without Czechoslovak representatives at the table.
  • March 1939: Germany occupies the remaining Czech lands (Bohemia and Moravia); Slovakia becomes a client state. Britain and France extend guarantees to Poland.
  • 23 August 1939: German–Soviet Non-Aggression Pact (Molotov–Ribbentrop). A secret protocol allocates spheres of interest in Eastern Europe, including a partition line through Poland.
  • 1 September 1939: Germany invades Poland. 3 September: Britain and France declare war on Germany.
  • 17 September 1939: Soviet forces enter eastern Poland. Late September: German–Soviet boundary treaties seal a new line (often taught with reference to the Curzon Line).

2 Three acts, different arrows

This is the wiring-diagrams chapter. The nodes are shared: Munich, the Pact, two invasions of Poland. The arrows — what caused what — are not.

Act I · Munich. Russia’s state textbook does not call it a conference first. It calls it a collusion:

«Мюнхенский сговор стал синонимом предательства и позора, венцом беспринципных…»

“The Munich collusion became synonymous with betrayal and disgrace, the crown of unprincipled…”

RU · Чубарьян, Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл., 2023 · p. 109

The same page has Chamberlain wave the text: “I have brought peace for our time.” Belarus agrees that London and Paris “betrayed a country that they were obligated to protect,” and adds the eastward hope: they “secretly hoped to direct the point of German aggression to the east, against the Soviet Union.” Italy quotes Churchill’s verdict — dishonor and war — and notes that Moscow was kept outside the room. Germany’s civic booklet records that Chamberlain was greeted with applause as a European politician of peace, then that Czechoslovakia was “sacrificed.” The United Kingdom alone runs a long exam debate on whether appeasement was cowardice, calculation, or both. India, in our corpus, does not stage Munich at all — an absence graded against books that do, not a secret.

Act II · The Pact. Once Munich has failed, the next paper is the German–Soviet non-aggression treaty. Italy calls the announcement one of the great coups de théâtre of diplomacy; the secret protocol, in its account, already “provides for the partition” of Poland. Russia calls the pact a “forced step” and stresses that delimitation of spheres was “initiated by Germany.” Belarus prints the signatures of Ribbentrop and Molotov and later lets Stalin defend the delay of war. Germany’s booklet treats 23 August as the date of “joint division, exploitation, and oppression of Europe by National Socialist Germany and the Stalinist Soviet Union.” Lowe’s Britain folds the pact into a single sentence before the tanks: after signing with Russia, “the Germans invaded Poland.” The arrows diverge: pact as Soviet necessity after Western betrayal; pact as Hitler’s free hand; pact as shared crime.

Act III · September. Everyone who covers the outbreak agrees that German forces crossed the Polish border on 1 September. The fight that organizes this chapter’s ladder is the second army — the one that enters on the 17th — and the noun attached to it.

4 The tell

The chapter’s grammar problem is not whether Germany attacked Poland. It is which prior paper is allowed to explain the war, and which noun is allowed for the second army.

For the Russian volume, Munich is already a moral catastrophe — «сговор», collusion — that isolates the USSR and makes the later pact a forced realignment. September 1 is Hitler’s Weiss plan and, on the same pages, Gleiwitz as German pretext. September 17 arrives as an order to liberate lands lost under the Riga treaty; the sentence does not name who gave the order. Belarus keeps German agency primary on 1 September (“one-on-one with the aggressor”), then converts 17 September into a liberation campaign and a living holiday. The holiday is the tell: the classroom does not only narrate 1939; it still marks it.

Germany’s civic booklet refuses both soft arrows. The pact date is joint oppression; the Soviet move is ordered annexation under the pact with Hitler. The British and American surveys use the ordinary verbs of invasion and dual crush. Italy adds “secret clauses” and “seized.” None of those Western or Italian lines need National Unity Day. The Belarusian line cannot do without it.

Munich’s vocabulary war foreshadows the September one. «Appeasement» is the English classroom’s multi-page problem set. «Collusion» is the Russian crown of disgrace. Churchill’s “dishonor and war,” carried in the Italian manual, is a third arrow: the conference did not buy peace; it bought a delay with a price. Those are not three styles for one fact. They are three causal diagrams that students carry into the next unit — sometimes into the next century’s arguments.

After the September partition, the dual occupation produces prisoners, deportations, and — in only a few books in this corpus — a named massacre in the Katyn forest. That exhibit arrives as its own piece; this chapter only leaves the socket.

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

bpb (DE) earns the lead for refusing euphemism on the dual partition: joint division and an order to annex are stated in the same civic voice that names German crimes elsewhere. That is not balance as symmetry; it is balance as willingness to put both states on the page as agents.

Lowe (UK) earns a nod for making students argue appeasement as a real policy debate with costs, not only as a swear-word. The September line is blunt (“Russians invaded”), which keeps the dual crush visible without the holiday frame.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) credit for chaining secret clauses to seizure in one sentence — the pact is not only a diplomatic surprise; it is operational text.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This chapter is a wiring diagram of six to eight narrators, not a military history of the Polish campaign. Casualty figures, air-war detail at Wieluń, and the Phoney War appear unevenly; absence of a number is not proof it did not happen.

«Сговор» / “collusion” is the Russian textbook’s own label, quoted as such — not an editorial sneer. “Appeasement” is the British and Belarusian vocabulary for the same months.

IN’s zero on September 1939 is graded as avoidance only relative to books that cover the invasion; Munich’s thin or zero coverage in some syllabuses is curriculum choice. KZ remains outside the peer ladder by genre rule.

Katyn is intentionally not argued here. Machine-translated discovery maps led to pages; Russian, Belarusian, German, and Italian lines above are checked against original extractions for this draft.
