Who started 1914

On 28 June 1914, in Sarajevo, an open car takes a wrong turn. A nineteen-year-old named Gavrilo Princip is standing on the pavement with a pistol. Two shots later the heir to Austria-Hungary is dead, and so is his wife. Within five weeks half of Europe is at war. What each classroom does with those five weeks — who fires, who mobilizes, who is “pulled in,” who is guilty — is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who started it — and in which classroom does “everyone” share the guilt before anyone is named as first mover?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 28 June 1914: Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and Sophie are shot in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb connected to a nationalist network with ties into Serbia.
  • 23 July: Austria-Hungary sends Serbia a harsh ultimatum. 28 July: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
  • Russian partial then general mobilization follows (late July). Germany declares war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August; German troops enter Belgium; Britain declares war on Germany on 4 August.
  • German opening strategy on the Western Front is commonly taught under the label “Schlieffen Plan”: a rapid blow at France, often via Belgium, before turning east.
  • Who “started” the war is one of the longest-running disputes in modern history teaching. School books that cover the crisis at all must choose a grammar: shared guilt, alliance chain, Austrian first shot, German “blank check,” or deliberate German provocation.
  • The peace settlement of 1919 will put a formal war-guilt burden on Germany (feeds ch. 5). The classrooms below are already rehearsing that argument in July 1914’s tense.

2 The ladder

Five narrators who actually run the July Crisis, ordered from thinnest agency (Germany “pulled into” a fight; alliances that draw states in) to thickest (a named government’s “initiative” that makes the crisis precipitate). China, Sudan, and India name the war’s existence — as date, Arab aftermath, or timeline cell — and never the crisis or who opened fire; that is an avoidance signal against a 5-of-8 in-scope baseline, and it sits in the receipts as badges, not empty ladder rows. Ukraine (1945–2018) and Germany’s NS booklet are out of period. Kazakhstan is never a world-event column.

3 The tell

Every covering book on this ladder can list the same dates: Sarajevo, the Austrian declaration, Russian mobilization, German war on Russia and France, Belgium, Britain. The disagreement is not the calendar. It is the grammar that turns the calendar into a starter.

OpenStax is the softest finish after the hardest English phrase. Germany extends a “blank check” — unlimited backing, no questions asked — and Austria is “emboldened.” Then the next sentence takes the will out of Berlin: Germany “found itself pulled into the fight whether it wanted to be or not.” The assessment page later does the same work with intransitive verbs: the assassination “caused” Austria-Hungary to undertake a war; that war “drew in” Russia and Germany; treaties “brought” more countries in. Blank check is agency. “Pulled into” and “drew in” are weather. Both live in the same American survey.

Belarus runs the opposite sequence and lands in a different room. The cascade is agentive and unkind to Berlin: Germany sees a “favorable moment” to deal with France, demands that Russia stop mobilizing, and declares war “without awaiting a response.” Then the responsibility paragraph opens with Versailles memory — “Initially, Germany was considered guilty” — and redistributes: most historians, the book says, hold that all the main participants were guilty to one degree or another. The student gets a sharp July and a soft verdict.

«У каждой из сторон была своя доля вины. Однако непосредственно развязывание войны связано с неспровоцированными военными действиями австро-германского блока против Сербии, Бельгии, Люксембурга.»

“Each of the sides had its share of guilt. However, directly the unleashing of the war is connected with the unprovoked military actions of the Austro-German bloc against Serbia, Belgium, Luxembourg.”

RU · Чубарьян 2023 · printed p. 29

Russia’s double move is the finding that does not need a comment to do its job. In one breath: every side had its share of guilt; however, the unleashing of the war is tied to the unprovoked military actions of the Austro-German bloc. Shared guilt is granted. First-mover language is not surrendered. Wilhelm is already on the page pushing the Austrians to “finish off the Serbs,” and the assassination is a “pretext.” The classroom gets structural causes, a named Kaiser, and a bloc that starts the shooting — without having to choose only one of those stories.

The United Kingdom is the only column that turns “who started it?” into a named historiography lesson. Lowe stages four first-mover candidates in one paragraph — Austria, Russia, Germany, Britain — then walks the student through blank cheque, Schlieffen, miscalculation, and Fritz Fischer. The close is not a shrug. “Probably a majority of historians, including many Germans, accept Fritz Fischer’s theory… that the outbreak of war was deliberately provoked by Germany’s leaders.” Italy does not stage the debate; it issues the verdict in one institutional noun phrase: l’iniziativa del governo tedesco — the initiative of the German government — already unconditional in its support to Austria, that made the situation “definitively precipitate.” Austria’s “first move” is the ultimatum; Germany’s initiative is what makes a Balkan crisis a European war. First step and precipitate cause are not the same job, and Italy assigns both.

Three in-scope books never enter this argument. China’s 纲要 uses “the outbreak of the First World War” as a date on which Japan presses the Twenty-One Demands and later as the backdrop to Chinese laborers and the May Fourth crisis at Paris — European who-started grammar is not the lesson. Sudan’s secondary history reaches the war as European camps and the end of the Ottoman order (Sykes-Picot, Allied victory). India’s NCERT puts “First World War (1914-1918)” in timeline cells and moves on. Against five covering narrators out of eight books that could have taught the crisis, those three zeros are avoidance of the starter question, not a shared curriculum silence about the war’s existence.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the credit line for this event. Not because it alone blames Germany — Italy’s “initiative of the German government” is cleaner and shorter — but because it is the only classroom that shows the student the fight among historians as part of the syllabus: named theories, named scholars (Fischer, Zuber, Turner, and others), and an explicit majority claim the reader can check later. “Who started it?” is the chapter’s question because it is already Lowe’s section title. A market survey written in English, far from 1914’s losing capitals, uses that distance to stage an argument rather than to close it early.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) deserves the agency nod: first move (Austria, 23 July), second step (Russia), precipitate cause (German government initiative). No double move, no “pulled into,” no redistribution paragraph that undoes the cascade. The Italian free-market manual is also the one that says the premises of conflict “did not have as obligatory outlet” a European war — decisions by rulers turned a local crisis general — which is the chapter’s causal precision in one sentence.

Чубарьян (RU) is the finding lead, not the fairness lead. Holding “share of guilt” and “unprovoked… Austro-German bloc” in adjacent sentences is the move the comparison is built to catch. Credit for candor about a double grammar is not the same as credit for the cleanest single answer.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is five books that teach the July Crisis as a sequence with agents. It is not “the world.” Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945; Germany’s booklet is a National Socialism primer that uses “July 1914” only as a mood contrast to 1939. Those zeros are syllabus boundaries, not national silence about 1914. Kazakhstan is national history and is never placed as a peer column on world events.

China, Sudan, and India are different. They are in period for a world-history treatment of 1914 and they name the war; they do not name Sarajevo, the ultimatum chain, or a first mover. That is the avoidance signal (5/8 in-scope cover the crisis). It is still not a claim that those classrooms “hide” the war — they use 1914 for other cargo (Japan’s chance in China; Ottoman partition; timeline furniture).
