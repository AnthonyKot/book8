Who started 1914

On 28 June 1914 a car takes a wrong turn in Sarajevo, reverses, and stops in front of a nineteen-year-old with a pistol. Thirty-eight days later, Europe’s great powers are at war with one another. Every book in this chapter prints the same calendar for those thirty-eight days. Not one of them means the same thing by it.

The question · causality

Everybody prints the same month. Which move does your textbook make the one that turned a murder into a world war?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 28 June 1914: Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to Austria-Hungary, and his wife Sophie are shot dead in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip. He and the other conspirators are Bosnian Serbs — subjects of Austria-Hungary — armed and passed across the Serbian border by officers connected to a secret society.
  • 5–6 July: Berlin assures Vienna of support whatever it decides — the assurance later nicknamed the “blank cheque.”
  • 23 July: Austria-Hungary delivers an ultimatum to Serbia with a forty-eight-hour deadline. Serbia’s reply of 25 July accepts most of the demands and qualifies the rest.
  • 28 July: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
  • 29–30 July: Russia orders partial mobilisation, cancels it, then orders general mobilisation.
  • 31 July: Germany demands that Russia stop. 1 August: Germany declares war on Russia. 3 August: on France.
  • 4 August: German troops cross into Belgium; Britain declares war on Germany the same day. 6 August: Austria-Hungary declares war on Russia.
  • 1919: Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles records the responsibility of Germany and her allies. The scholarly argument about who caused the war has restarted at least three times since — most loudly with Fritz Fischer in the 1960s and again around the centenary.

2 The ladder

Five narrators whose syllabus reaches the crisis and who narrate it, ordered from thinnest agency (a war that pulls people into itself) to thickest (a named country, a named historian, a verdict). Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945 and Germany’s is a National Socialism primer; China, Sudan and India carry the war but never open the crisis. All five are in the receipts. Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event column here.

The argument itself, and a verdict

3 The tell

Start with what nobody disputes. All five books name Princip. All five run the same cascade — ultimatum, declaration on Serbia, Russian mobilisation, German declarations on Russia and France, Belgium, Britain. All five print the Schlieffen Plan. There is no hidden fact here and no suppressed date. Everything happens in the open, in the same order, in every classroom — including, in two of the books, the moment the weapons cross an international border, which neither book stops for. The disagreement is entirely about which of those moves is load-bearing, and the grammar is where each book puts its thumb.

OpenStax has the strangest verb in the corpus. Germany found itself pulled into the fight whether it wanted to be or not — a great power as the object of its own sentence, wanting nothing, dragged. The same book has already explained that Germany handed Vienna a cheque with no amount on it, and it will shortly explain that the German army had a plan requiring it to attack France first; neither fact survives into the verb. The section summary finishes the job. There the subject is “it” — the assassination — and “it caused Austria-Hungary to undertake a war.” Treaty obligations “brought more countries into the conflict.” The only sentence in which Germany is called an aggressor puts the word at one remove: the invasion of Belgium “painted Germany as the aggressor nation.” Painted. In the American book, 1914 is a machine that starts itself.

Belarus does something more interesting than it looks. The chapter opens with unnamed men — «Политические деятели, выражавшие их интересы, ввергли мир в войну», “political figures, expressing their interests, plunged the world into war” — and then, on the next page, smuggles a whole verdict into one adverb. Austria-Hungary and Germany presented the Entente “only ultimatums.” Not proposals, not notes, not conferences: только ультиматумы. One word does the work an entire paragraph does elsewhere. Then comes the paragraph that undoes it. Germany was “at first considered the guilty one” — and the book lists why, at length: it broke Belgian neutrality, which it had itself guaranteed; it was first to declare war on France and Russia; Versailles said so. Every clause points one way. The next sentence turns: the problem “is much more complicated than was thought at first,” and the majority of historians hold that all the main participants were guilty to one degree or another. The evidence stays on the page. The conclusion walks away from it.

Russia writes both verdicts in consecutive sentences and lets them stand. «У каждой из сторон была своя доля вины. Однако…» — each side had its share of guilt; however. What follows the however is one adjective: the unleashing of the war is connected with the unprovoked military actions of the Austro-German bloc. That single word is the whole finding. Chubaryan’s July also convicts by subordinate clause — “although peaceful means of settling the conflict were far from exhausted, on 28 July Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia” — and it is the only book on the ladder that shows a named man pushing: Wilhelm II urging the Austrians to “finish off the Serbs.” The assassination itself is not a cause in this book. It “was used as a pretext,” in the passive, by users the sentence declines to name until the following clause supplies them.

The Italian manual is the only one that numbers the moves. Austria made the first move; Russia took the second step; and the third is a cleft sentence, which is Italian’s way of pointing: «Fu dunque l’iniziativa del governo tedesco … a far precipitare definitivamente la situazione.» It was the initiative of the German government that made it fall. Sabbatucci and Vidotto also do the one thing no other column attempts — they say out loud that the war was avoidable, twice. The tensions of 1914 “did not have a European conflict as their obligatory outlet.” It was Sarajevo that made latent tensions explode, and “the decisions taken by rulers and military chiefs” that turned a local crisis into a general one. Structural causes are listed and then explicitly denied the last word. That is a university manual’s move, and it costs Italy nothing, because Italy is not in the July Crisis; it declared neutrality and joined the other side ten months later.

“No wonder Bethmann, when asked how it all began, raised his arms to heaven and replied: ‘Oh – if I only knew!’”

UK · Lowe, on the German Chancellor of July 1914 — printed in a school textbook as a serious answer.

Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed. · §1.4, p. 18

And then there is Lowe, who does something none of the others do: he makes the argument itself the content of the lesson. Section 1.4 is not called “the causes of the war.” It is called What caused the war, and who was to blame? — and it answers by naming the people who have disagreed. George Kennan on the alliance system. A. J. P. Taylor on railway timetables. Fritz Fischer, who “caused a sensation.” H. W. Koch, who dismissed him. L. C. F. Turner’s “tragedy of miscalculation.” Terence Zuber, Samuel Williamson, John Rohl, William Mulligan, two edited collections with their editors named and dated. A fourteen-year-old is being handed a bibliography and told the professionals are still fighting. The book then takes a side anyway — a majority accept Fischer, the war “was deliberately provoked by Germany’s leaders” — and one of the four theories it stages is that Britain was to blame, for not saying in time whose side it was on. A market textbook in the country with the most to lose from that theory prints it fourth in the list and does not knock it down.

One date splits the corpus, and it is not an accident which one. Lowe: the Russians “ordered a general mobilization (29 July).” Sabbatucci–Vidotto: the day after the declaration on Serbia, so also the 29th. Chubaryan: on 29 July Russia declared partial, “then general,” mobilisation. OpenStax: “Russia ordered mobilization of its troops on July 30 in response.” Belarus gives no date at all — Russia “began mobilisation.” The real sequence is messier than any of them: partial on the 29th, cancelled, general on the 30th. But look at what the compression does. Under the shared logic of 1914, mobilisation was not a threat but the first act of the war — Lowe prints A. J. P. Taylor’s argument that the plans, “based on precise railway timetables,” cut the time available for negotiation “almost to nil,” and quotes Moltke refusing to redirect the troop trains because there was “no time to change all the railway timetables.” If Russia mobilised on the 29th, Russia moved before the German ultimatum of the 31st. If Russia mobilised on the 30th, the two are nearly simultaneous. The one number these books disagree about is the number that decides who moved first.

Three books in this corpus reach 1914 and never open the crisis. The interesting thing is that none of them is silent about the war. China’s volume gives the outbreak a single grammatical function — a window: 「日本看到袁世凯大权在握，1914 年 8 月，利用第一次世界大战爆发，欧洲列强无力东顾的时机，向袁世凯提出……“二十一条”要求。」 “Japan, seeing that Yuan Shikai held full power, in August 1914 took advantage of the moment when the outbreak of the First World War left the European powers unable to attend to the East, and put to Yuan Shikai the demands known as the ‘Twenty-One Demands’.” The war is weather over Europe, and what matters is what it makes possible in Shandong. Sudan’s book gives it a shape rather than a start: the conflict between the colonial and European powers “reached its peak” with the outbreak. India’s NCERT gives it a box on a timeline — “1914-18 First World War” — three rows below “1904-05 War between Japan and Russia,” which gets two named combatants. The war with a first mover is nameable in that timeline. The war of 1914 is not.

«وصل الصراع بين القوى الاستعمارية والأوربية قمته باندلاع الحرب العالمية الأولى (1914 – 1918م)، والتي انقسمت فيها الدول الأوربية إلى معسكرين…»

“The conflict between the colonial and European powers reached its peak with the outbreak of the First World War (1914–1918 CE), in which the European states divided into two camps…”

SD · التاريخ للصف الثالث (2009) · “The Arab World in the Wake of the First World War,” p. 188

That is not ignorance of how wars begin. The Sudanese book contains a July 1914 of its own, ten years later and in another city: the Governor-General is shot in the streets of Cairo on 19 November 1924, and “immediately Britain sent an ultimatum to the Egyptian government of Saʿd Zaghlūl” with a twenty-four-hour deadline. Assassination, ultimatum, deadline, escalation — the grammar is fully available. It is spent on the crisis that happened to Sudan. The same book’s account of the Battle of Kosovo in 1389 even supplies a killer who stabs a sovereign after the battle, on the anniversary that brought an archduke to Sarajevo. Three classrooms out of eleven decline to open the July Crisis, and all three of them are elsewhere on the map. What they are declining is not the war. It is the premise that a European quarrel is where the world’s history starts.

One thread does run back through this book’s own Part I. Three of the five columns explain the German plan by Russian slowness — Chubaryan’s Schlieffen Plan “proceeded from the fact that Russia was weak,” OpenStax’s “rested on the belief that Russia would be slow to mobilize,” Belarus’s Germany moving while “slow-moving Russia” had not deployed. Chubaryan dates the plan to 1905. Lowe dates it to 1905–6 and, three sections later, prints William Mulligan’s argument that “Russia’s defeat by Japan in 1905 had fatal consequences for the peace of Europe” — it broke the government, and it turned Russian ambition away from the Far East and back onto the Balkans. The chapter before this one is about that defeat. The war plan that opened the Western Front in 1914 was written in the year the Russian fleet went down at Tsushima, and it assumed the loser would still be slow nine years later.

4 The thread

Two classrooms on this ladder say how the pistols reached Sarajevo. OpenStax says it in a main clause: “a small band of would-be Bosnian Serb … assassins armed with weapons from the Serbian military crossed the border from Serbia into Bosnia-Herzegovina.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto says it in a relative clause, and lets it go.

«L’attentatore faceva parte di un’organizzazione irredentista che aveva la sua base operativa in Serbia e godeva di una certa tolleranza da parte del governo di quel paese.»

“The assassin was part of an irredentist organisation that had its operational base in Serbia and enjoyed a certain tolerance on the part of the government of that country.”

IT · Il mondo contemporaneo (Laterza 2004) · §13.1, p. 269

A base in one kingdom, a killing in another: young men move pistols and bombs across the frontier of a great power, and no column on the ladder mentions a document, a check or a guard. They were in fact smuggled — an underground channel run by sympathetic Serbian frontier officers took them over the Drina at night — but that was about the weapons in the bag. The thing a modern reader reaches for was not being evaded, because it was not there. Most of Europe in June 1914 travelled without passports: the requirement had lapsed across the west and centre of the continent during the nineteenth century, and the conspicuous exceptions were the Russian and Ottoman empires, where papers were needed to leave and, in Russia, to move about inside. A frontier was a customs post. It taxed what crossed. It did not ask who did.

Then thirty-eight days end, and movement becomes a security question in every belligerent capital at once. Britain’s answer was the Aliens Restriction Act — introduced, carried through every stage and given royal assent on 5 August 1914, the first full day of its war — taking power over the entry, departure, registration and residence of foreigners. The document issued under the wartime rules from 1915 then added the two things a Victorian letter of request had never carried: a photograph, and a physical description set out as a table — forehead, eyes, nose, shape of face. A person had become a set of readable fields.

The war ended and the controls did not. In October 1920 the League of Nations convened a Conference on Passports, Customs Formalities and Through Tickets in Paris, and wrote the booklet down: 15.5 by 10.5 centimetres, thirty-two pages, the photograph inside the front, the rest left for visas, printed in French and one other language. Two years later the same body issued the Nansen passport, a document for people whose state would no longer issue one — statelessness having just become common enough to need stationery.

The precision matters, because the punchy version is false. The war did not invent the identity document; safe-conducts are medieval, and the empire that lost most at Sarajevo had neighbours who had been checking papers for a century. What the war did was narrower and larger. It made the temporary universal — every belligerent, within months — and then it made the universal permanent, by agreeing in peacetime on the shape of what had been improvised as an emergency. Emergencies expire. Standards do not.

So the booklet in the drawer is that emergency, in the form the Paris conference gave it: the photograph page, the description, the pages left blank for other countries’ stamps. The queue at the airport is the measure still being enforced. And the ladder never joins the two ends — 0 of 11 classrooms connect the modern passport to this war. Two of eleven describe the crossing that begins the chapter, one in a main clause and one in a relative clause; 0 of 11 remark on it. There was nothing yet to remark on, which is the whole of the finding.

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) takes the credit line, and it is not close. No other book in this corpus tells a school student that the professionals are still arguing, names eleven of them, dates their books, and lets four incompatible answers stand on the page before choosing one. It prints the theory that blames its own country. It prints the counter-evidence to its own verdict — the Kaiser and Bethmann urging Vienna to negotiate on 30 July, Zuber’s claim that the Schlieffen Plan as usually described was never the only option, Williamson’s claim that Vienna decided for itself and German pressure did not matter much. It ends by naming three states, not one. Whatever else this is, it is a lesson in how a historical claim is made and unmade, and the student who reads it has been given the tools to disagree with it.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns the second nod for the sentence that costs a determinist account everything: the tensions of 1914 “did not have a European conflict as their obligatory outlet.” Rival blocs, an arms race and warlike pressure at home are all listed — and then denied the power to decide. What decided was a set of choices by named offices in a known order: Vienna’s first move, St Petersburg’s second step, Berlin’s initiative. It is the only column that both refuses inevitability and assigns responsibility, which are usually thought to be opposite moves.

Chubaryan (RU) earns a narrower and more surprising one. This is the state-approved book of a country that is a belligerent in the story, and it puts its own government’s war aims on the page in plain type — control of the Bosphorus and Dardanelles, and the annexation of Slav-populated territory, “in particular Galicia.” It states that the just resistance of Serbia, Montenegro and Belgium “did not change the general predatory character of the war on the part of the two blocs” — both blocs, its own included. Its verdict then favours its own side, in one adjective. But the material a reader would need to argue with that adjective is printed two paragraphs above it.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is five books, not five countries and not eleven. Ukraine’s volume covers 1945–2018 and Germany’s is a National Socialism booklet; both are outside the period by syllabus, not by choice. That leaves eight books whose reach includes 1914, of which five narrate the crisis and three do not. Under this project’s own rule of thumb — a zero counts as an avoidance signal only when most narrators cover the topic — five in eight is a majority but a thin one, and the three zeros are better read as a different unit of analysis than as an evasion. All three books carry the First World War. None of them treats a Balkan quarrel as the place a world begins.

Kept verbatim, because errors are data. Lowe’s closing sentence reads: “Perhaps the most sensible conclusion is that Germany, Russia and Austria–Hungary must both share the responsibility for the outbreak of war in 1914.” Three countries, and a word that can only hold two. It is the last line of a section about how hard the question is, and the sentence itself cannot count the answer.

A second keep: Sabbatucci–Vidotto date Britain’s declaration of war on Germany to 5 August, both in the narrative and again in the chapter summary, where the German declarations on Russia and France are also compressed onto a single date, 3 August. The other four books on this ladder say 4 August for Britain, and 1 and 3 August for Russia and France. The summary is the part a student revises from.

Lowe dates Fischer’s sensation to 1967, which is the year of the English translation; the German book appeared in 1961. For an English classroom that is a defensible shorthand rather than a mistake, but the date a reader would look up is not the date on the page.

The Sudanese lines here are the weakest evidence in the chapter and are marked accordingly. That PDF’s Arabic extraction is ligature-mangled — letters decompose and reorder, so the strings cannot be searched or copied reliably. Both Arabic passages above were reconstructed to standard orthography from the mangled page with a machine translation used only for discovery, and both must be read against the printed page before this chapter ships. Sudanese page numbers are printed-page numbers, eight less than the PDF’s.

Finally, none of these books is a history of July 1914. The longest account on this ladder is a few pages; the documentary record of those thirty-eight days runs to tens of thousands of pages, most of it published by the belligerent governments themselves in the 1920s while they were arguing about Article 231. If you want to know what was said in the room in Vienna on 7 July, every column here will leave you unsatisfied.
