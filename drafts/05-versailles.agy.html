Versailles, 1919

On 28 June 1919, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, delegates of a defeated German Republic sign a peace treaty 436 pages long. Outside the palace, Allied crowds celebrate the end of four years of global war. Inside the textbooks written decades later, seven national narrators construct six different peacemaking events. What each classroom does with that afternoon in Paris is the subject of this chapter.

The question · space

Whose peace was it — and where on the map was a deal signed that wasn’t a deferred war?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 18 January 1919: The Paris Peace Conference opens at the French Foreign Ministry (Quai d’Orsay) with representatives from 32 states; defeated Central Powers and Soviet Russia are excluded from conference negotiations.
  • Executive decisions are directed by the Supreme Council of the Allied and Associated Powers, dominated by Woodrow Wilson (US), David Lloyd George (UK), Georges Clemenceau (France), and Vittorio Emanuele Orlando (Italy) — conventionally termed the "Big Four" (or "Big Three" when Italy is omitted).
  • 28 June 1919: The Treaty of Versailles is signed with Germany. Key provisions: Alsace-Lorraine returned to France, Saar basin placed under League of Nations administration for 15 years, Rhineland demilitarized, German army capped at 100,000 men, and loss of roughly 13% of European territory alongside all overseas colonies.
  • Article 231 (the "war guilt" clause) affirms German responsibility for Allied damages; the Reparation Commission in April 1921 fixes total German reparations at 132 billion gold marks (£6,600 million / ~$33 billion).
  • Companion peace treaties are signed with the remaining defeated powers: Saint-Germain-en-Laye (Austria, Sept 1919), Neuilly-sur-Seine (Bulgaria, Nov 1919), Trianon (Hungary, June 1920), and Sèvres (Ottoman Empire, Aug 1920).
  • April 1920: The San Remo Conference formalizes League of Nations Class A mandates over former Ottoman Arab provinces, assigning Syria and Lebanon to France, and Palestine, Transjordan, and Iraq to Great Britain.
  • 4 May 1919: Demonstrations erupt in Beijing after Allied powers award Germany's former rights in Shandong province to Japan, initiating the May Fourth Movement; Chinese delegates refuse to sign the Treaty of Versailles on 28 June.
  • 19 March 1920: The United States Senate fails to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, leaving the US outside the League of Nations.

Books outside the main world-history coverage — Ukraine’s 1945–2018 volume, Germany’s National Socialism booklet, India's single May Fourth line, and Kazakhstan's national history — are in the receipts, not on the ladder. That placement is curriculum design, not an avoidance tag.

3 The tell

Every narrator on this ladder agrees that statesmen gathered in Paris in 1919 and drafted documents that reshaped borders. The disagreement begins on the first line of the guest list. Belarus names the "Big Three" — Wilson, Lloyd George, and Clemenceau — leaving Italy outside the room entirely. Russia and Italy name the "Big Four," but Sabbatucci–Vidotto immediately add a parenthetical qualification: Vittorio Emanuele Orlando "in reality played a marginal role." The head count is not a trivial discrepancy. Whether the table has three seats or four determines whether Italy's postwar resentment is framed as a victor's greed or a minor partner's exclusion.

«Жёсткие и унизительные условия мира, тяжёлые репарации и „вина за войну“… являлись бомбой замедленного действия в самом центре Европы.»

“The harsh and humiliating conditions of peace, heavy reparations, and ‘war guilt’… were a time bomb in the very center of Europe.”

RU · Чубарьян 2023 · p. 55

For the British and American textbooks, Versailles is fundamentally an argument about German numbers and moral blame. Lowe details the internal Allied debate over Article 231 and contrasts the official £6,600 million reparation demand against John Maynard Keynes's £2,000 million warning. OpenStax converts the sum to "over $30 billion in 1919 dollars" and notes the "war guilt" clause as the origin of decades of German instability. Yet OpenStax stands alone among Western surveys in recording what the conference suppressed: Japan's proposed racial-equality clause, vetoed by Anglo-American opposition, and the complete omission of African casualties from Allied damage tallies.

巴黎和会上，强权战胜了公理

“At the Paris Peace Conference, power triumphed over justice.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 (2019) · p. 141

Outside Europe, the name of the treaty itself disappears or transforms into a mechanism of colonial control. In China's state textbook, the word "Versailles" is nowhere in the lesson title or narrative. The event is exclusively the "Paris Peace Conference," staged not as a German settlement, but as a moral collapse where "power triumphed over justice" by handing Shandong rights to Japan. The climax of the chapter is not a signed document, but the Chinese delegates' refusal to sign — a refusal that serves as the fuse for the May Fourth Movement and the birth of modern Chinese political mobilization. Sudan similarly reroutes 1919 through the 1920 San Remo Conference, where Wilsonian self-determination was converted by "shrewd English politicians" into European mandates — "a new link in the chain of colonialism." For London and Washington, 1919 was a flawed European peace; for Khartoum and Beijing, it was the formalization of imperial subjection under a new legal vocabulary.

4 The thread

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

MoE Sudan (SD) earns the credit line for structural perspective. By linking the 1919 Paris Peace Conference directly to the 1920 San Remo Conference, it exposes the operational mechanism of the post-war settlement in the non-Western world. Where Western books treat mandates as a side arrangement to German reparations, the Sudanese classroom shows how Wilson's principle of self-determination was legally adapted into a new framework of colonial control.

中外历史纲要 (CN) takes the depth lead for diplomatic agency. It does not treat the Paris conference as a passive background event, but explicitly details the delegation's正义要求 ("just demands"), the transfer of Shandong, the formula "power triumphed over justice," and the final refusal to sign. That refusal turns a diplomatic defeat into the foundational spark of modern Chinese political identity.

OpenStax (US) deserves credit among Western surveys for recording what Allied diplomacy excluded. It explicitly flags the rejection of Japan's racial-equality clause and notes that Allied reparation tallies omitted African lives lost during the war, acknowledging the racial boundaries embedded in the 1919 settlement.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder represents seven pinned textbook editions whose world-history syllabi cover the 1919 settlement. It is not "the world." Ukraine’s secondary volume covers 1945–2018, Germany’s federal booklet is a National Socialism primer focused on 1939–1945, and Kazakhstan’s volume is national history. Those omissions reflect curriculum boundaries rather than deliberate avoidance of 1919.

India’s NCERT volume touches the post-war peace conference in a single sentence within its unit on modern China ("On 4 May 1919, an angry demonstration was held in Beijing to protest against the decisions of the post-war peace conference"), but omits Versailles, reparations, Article 231, and the League Covenant as standalone topics. It is therefore placed in the receipts badge rather than on the main comparison ladder.
