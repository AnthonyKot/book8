### Pitch 1: Salt on every table *(seed from US depth — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder / tell).** OpenStax alone walks the Salt March: “On March 12, 1930, Gandhi began a protest known as the Salt March, in which he led his supporters on a two-hundred-mile, twenty-four-day march to the Arabian Sea to collect salt from the seawater” (US, p. 504–505); ~60,000 arrested. No other covering book dates Dandi or treats salt as the hinge of civil disobedience. Lowe, the thickest 1947 column, never needs the march. NCERT’s world-history timeline never names it.
- **Loop.** Colonial law monopolises a dietary staple and taxes it → Gandhi chooses salt because every household understands the insult without a pamphlet → the march turns a fiscal monopoly into a mass theatre of non-cooperation → arrests scale into the tens of thousands → the 1930s campaigns and the later Quit India detention story make “granted in 1947” a sentence that sits on years of ungovernability, not on a sudden British change of heart.
- **Lander.** Table salt. The same mineral is in kitchens across every corpus country. The protest was not about a rare spice; it was about something nobody can opt out of eating.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms narrates the Salt March with date and distance (US); 0 of 11 join salt monopoly → mass arrest → ungovernability → transfer of power as one causal loop; IN world-history zero on the march is the self-pole gap against a story Indian national history courses elsewhere treat as central.
- **Receipts-lite.** Salt March / Dandi March, 12 March – 6 April 1930; British Salt Acts / salt tax; Dandi beach memorial and the Sabarmati–Dandi route still marked for visitors. <!-- CHECK: salt tax revenue share in colonial budgets; Gandhi’s own writings on salt as universal grievance -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** Seeded in the lede; earned by the US column’s exclusive depth; shared lander (salt, not an Indian-only holiday). Causal precision: the tax did not “cause” 1947 — it made the struggle legible in every kitchen, which is what the classroom comparison can show.

### Pitch 2: The line still on the news *(map lander — partition deaths as seed)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Three books count partition’s dead and do not agree: UA “about 100 thousand” in resettlement clashes (p. 132); IT “over 100,000 dead” and “17 million” transferred (p. 530); UK “about 250 000 people were murdered” in the Punjab alone (p. 762). UA alone says the Muslim League “found support from the metropolis” for a separate state. The border is not a shared number; it is a shared unfinished object.
- **Loop.** Congress wants a unitary India; League demands Pakistan → British exit timetable compresses under exhaustion and local violence → Mountbatten (UK) / colonial administration (UA) / English accord (IT) deliver partition as the available settlement → mass migration along religious lines produces death tolls no classroom pins the same way → Kashmir and the international border remain active dispute zones into the reader’s present.
- **Lander.** Any news map that still colours India and Pakistan as nuclear neighbours, or names Kashmir in a headline — the 1947 line is not archive ink. <!-- CHECK: Line of Control post-1949; both states nuclear-armed from 1998 -->
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 11 give a partition death figure (UA, IT, UK); 1 of 11 names metropolis support for the League (UA); 1 of 11 stages gift-vs-struggle (UK); 0 of 11 reconcile the body-count gap or treat the disagreement as the finding.
- **Receipts-lite.** Indian Independence Act 1947; Radcliffe Award, mid-August 1947; UN mediation on Kashmir from 1948; migration memoirs and the Partition Museum (Amritsar). <!-- CHECK: Radcliffe arrived in India July 1947; awards published around 17 Aug 1947 -->
- **Why it fits.** Material map hook shared across corpus countries via news, diaspora, and geopolitics. Risk: easier to overclaim (“partition caused today’s wars”) — keep punchline to the line and the unresolved count, not to every later conflict.

### Pitch 3: “Hard-won fruit” vs “left it democracy” *(linguistic — two bequest sentences)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** UK alone prints the challenge: independence “was not a gift from the British, it was ‘the hard-won fruit of struggle and sacrifice’” (Lowe quoting Sarkar / Inder Singh, p. 758). RU alone prints the opposite bequest: “England, having left India, left it democracy” (Чубарьян, ~p. 125). Same exit, two moral objects — fruit of struggle vs democracy as luggage left on the verandah.
- **Loop.** Empire must narrate its departure as preparation or as loss of value → nationalist historiography answers that delay was the policy and struggle the cause → a Russian world-history page, far from both, converts the exit into a civilisational compliment (democracy left behind) → classrooms train students to hear “independence” as either trophy of resistance or proof of liberal tutelage, a split that still organises arguments about aid, institutions, and “who built what.”
- **Lander.** The everyday fight over the word **gift** — “they gave us X” vs “we took X” — in politics, workplaces, and family histories of migration. The chapter’s two sentences are the school version of a phrase people already use without the footnotes.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 stages the gift/struggle debate (UK); 1 of 11 writes democracy-as-bequest (RU); 0 of 11 put the two sentences side by side; BY/IN agentless “attained / become” avoid the fight entirely.
- **Receipts-lite.** Sumit Sarkar and Anita Inder Singh as named in Lowe; Government of India Acts 1919 and 1935 as the disputed “preparation”; Indian Constitution 1950 for the democracy that followed dominion status. <!-- CHECK: republic 26 Jan 1950; Lowe’s page cites Sarkar / Inder Singh by name -->
- **Why it fits.** Pure comparison hinge; linguistic lander travels. Weaker cup-of-tea sensory hit than salt or the map unless the reader cares about argument words.

### Pitch 4: Five weeks and a lawyer *(my candidate — non-obvious material)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Lowe alone puts a clock on the exit: government will leave “no later than June 1948,” then “Mountbatten brought the date for British withdrawal forward to August 1947,” and “within six weeks Mountbatten had worked out a plan for dividing the country” (UK, pp. 760–761). IT and UA narrate division without the compressed calendar. Nobody names the man who drew the line.
- **Loop.** Labour government wants a fast, face-saving transfer → violence in 1946 (UK’s Calcutta 5,000) destroys confidence in a unitary handover → viceroy advances the date by months → a boundary commission under Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a lawyer with no prior Indian service, is given weeks to partition provinces he has barely seen → awards land as millions are already moving → the speed of the paperwork becomes part of the death toll’s conditions (not its sole cause).
- **Lander.** Any border a reader has crossed that felt arbitrary — a straight line, a river that suddenly means a visa — is the same species of object. The Radcliffe Line is the extreme case still running through Punjab and Bengal’s successor maps. <!-- CHECK: Radcliffe Commission; time in India often cited as ~5–6 weeks; awards 12–17 Aug 1947 -->
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 names the accelerated timetable and six-week plan (UK); 0 of 11 name Radcliffe; 0 of 11 connect commission speed to migration deaths as mechanism rather than tragedy-colour.
- **Receipts-lite.** Mountbatten Plan, 3 June 1947; Indian Independence Act, 18 July 1947; Radcliffe awards; Partition Museum / cartographic exhibits of the line. <!-- CHECK: standard accounts e.g. von Tunzelmann, Indian Summer; Talbot & Singh on partition -->
- **Why it fits.** Non-obvious, material, checkable in two minutes of searching “Radcliffe Line.” Causal precision mandatory in draft: haste was a condition that shaped how partition killed, not a cartoon “one lawyer caused 1947.” Pairs with Pitch 2 if the user wants map + mechanism in one thread.
