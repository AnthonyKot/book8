China 1911–1949

On the afternoon of 1 October 1949, Mao Zedong steps onto the red balcony of the Tiananmen Gate Tower in Beijing and speaks into a microphone before a crowd of hundreds of thousands. Behind that single microphone lie thirty-eight years of collapse, warlord rule, purges, a 6,000-mile march, and world war. What eight national curricula do with those thirty-eight years is the subject of this chapter.

The question · naming

What do you call thirty years of war — a civil conflict, or a revolution that stood a nation up?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 10 October 1911: The Wuchang Uprising triggers the Xinhai Revolution; the Qing dynasty abdicates on 12 February 1912, ending two millennia of imperial rule. Sun Yat-sen becomes provisional president, succeeded shortly by Yuan Shikai in Beijing.
  • 4 May 1919: Student demonstrations in Beijing protest the Versailles Peace Conference decision to transfer German rights in Shandong to Japan, launching the May Fourth Movement.
  • 1921: The Chinese Communist Party (CCP) is founded in Shanghai. 1924–1927: First United Front between Kuomintang (GMD) and CCP enables the Northern Expedition (1926–1928) against regional warlords.
  • 12 April 1927: Chiang Kai-shek launches a bloody purge of Communists in Shanghai; the Nationalist government is established at Nanjing, opening a decade of civil conflict (1927–1937).
  • October 1934 – October 1936: Encircled by Nationalist encirclement campaigns, the Red Army executes the Long March from Jiangxi to Yan'an in Shanxi; Mao Zedong's leadership is consolidated following the Zunyi Conference (January 1935).
  • 1937–1945: Second Sino-Japanese War; GMD and CCP form a Second United Front; full-scale conflict merges into the Asian theater of World War II after 1941.
  • 1946–1949: Chinese Civil War resumes; People's Liberation Army (PLA) forces defeat Nationalist armies; Chiang Kai-shek and GMD remnants evacuate to Taiwan.
  • 1 October 1949: Proclamation of the People's Republic of China (PRC) in Beijing.

3 The tell

This chapter is unique in our corpus: it is China's home turf. In seven foreign classrooms, distance law applies — the farther a textbook is written from Beijing, the freer it feels to treat thirty-eight years of Chinese history as a military campaign or a Cold War preliminary. In China's own state-compiled textbook, distance collapses to zero. Self-pole grammar is the object of study.

Where seven foreign books see a "civil war" (гражданская война, guerra civile, civil war), the Chinese state volume reserves that term strictly for the opponent's posture. Chiang Kai-shek's government "persisted in the policies of dictatorship and civil war." The Communist campaign that won the country in 1949 is never merely a civil war: it is the People's War of Liberation (解放战争). The label carries the verdict. A civil war is a tragedy of divided brothers; a liberation war is an expulsion of usurpers.

Observe how the turning points are named across the rows. For Belarus, a "split" occurs, and in 1949 the PRC "was formed" — passive verbs with no visible human hand. For Ukraine, the war is a proxy duel where "the USSR supported the CPC, and the USA supported the Kuomintang." For Russia's unified textbook, the Long March is not a legendary epic but a military setback: the "Soviet movement in China suffered a defeat," though the Red Army preserved a "bridgehead" at Yan'an. Russian grammar retains the Comintern lens: the Chinese revolution is a regional sector of a global Soviet movement that hit a temporary obstacle.

1927 年 4 月 12 日，蒋介石在上海发动反革命政变，大肆捕杀共产党人和革命群众。

“On April 12, 1927, Chiang Kai-shek launched a counter-revolutionary coup in Shanghai, wantonly arresting and killing Communists and the revolutionary masses.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 · Lesson 22, p. 145

In China's own pages, moral grammar is absolute. Chiang Kai-shek's April 1927 purge is a "counter-revolutionary coup" (反革命政变). The May Fourth Movement of 1919 is not just a student rally against Versailles; it is the grand solemn threshold of the "anti-imperialist and anti-feudal New Democratic Revolution" (新民主主义革命). The Zunyi Conference of January 1935 during the Long March is the moment that "established the leading position of the correct Marxist line represented by Mao Zedong." Every noun comes equipped with its permanent historical adjective.

India's NCERT offers the most sophisticated conceptual contrast to Beijing's self-narrative. Where Western books focus on battlefield maps and Russian books search for Soviet influence, NCERT zeroes in on theory. It highlights Sun Yat-sen's "Three Principles" (San min chui) and Mao's choice to base revolution on the peasantry rather than urban factory workers. Crucially, NCERT notes that the 1949 state was built on "New Democracy" — an alliance of all social classes — explicitly contrasting it with the Soviet Union's "dictatorship of the proletariat." India reads China's revolution not as a proxy war, but as an alternative Asian path to modernity.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

NCERT (IN) earns the credit line for this chapter. It provides the clearest theoretical framework in the entire corpus, explaining how Sun Yat-sen's Three Principles set the initial agenda, how Mao shifted the revolutionary base from urban workers to rural peasants, and how "New Democracy" differed from Soviet proletarian dictatorship. It treats Chinese political thought as serious history rather than administrative backdrop.

OpenStax (US) deserves recognition for social and economic candor. It is the only Western survey here that connects student anger at Versailles directly to the May Fourth Movement, tracks the middle-class alienation caused by GMD hyperinflation, and explicitly records that the US refused to recognize the PRC while maintaining Taiwan as the "real" China until 1971.

Lowe (UK) is credited for precise quantitative reporting: it alone provides the estimated toll of the 1927 purge ("as high as 250 000") and details the brutal attrition of the Long March (almost 100,000 setting out, only 20,000 surviving 6,000 miles to Yan'an).

中外历史纲要 (CN) is the depth lead, not the fairness lead. As the self-pole narrator, it provides matchless detail on internal party history, military campaigns, and foundational speeches. Its self-narrative is the object of study itself.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder compares eight textbooks whose syllabi cover the 1911–1949 period. It does not represent "world opinion," but eight specific ministry-approved or market-dominant editions. Germany's federal booklet (focused on NS 1939–1945) and Sudan's secondary history (focused on regional Ottoman/Mahdist/Condominium history) are out of scope. Kazakhstan's textbook is national history and is not used as a world-history narrator here.

Translations from Chinese, Russian, Ukrainian, and Italian were executed from text extractions and verified against original corpus files (`corpus-orig/`). Line breaks in extracted text have been joined to match the continuous prose of the printed page.

None of these school textbooks is an exhaustive military or political history of China's transformation. Readers seeking complete operational histories of the Northern Expedition, the Long March, or the Huaihai Campaign will need specialized monographs beyond secondary school curricula.
