Russo-Japanese War, 1905

On the night of 8 February 1904, Japanese destroyers run into the outer roadstead at Port Arthur and put torpedoes into the Russian Pacific squadron lying at anchor. Declarations of war follow the attack. The port is Chinese. Its Chinese name is Lüshunkou, and Russia has held it on a twenty-five-year lease from the Qing since 1898. For the next eighteen months two empires fight for it across the fields of Manchuria, and the empire that owns the fields declares neutrality. What six classrooms do with that is the subject of this chapter.

The question · omission

The war is named after two countries and was fought inside a third. Whose textbook says whose ground it was?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • April 1895: the Treaty of Shimonoseki ends the war between Japan and Qing China. Days later Russia, Germany and France press Japan into handing back the Liaodong Peninsula.
  • 1898: Russia takes a twenty-five-year lease on southern Liaodong from the Qing — the ports of Lüshunkou (Port Arthur) and Dalian (Dalny) — and builds a rail branch south to reach them from the Chinese Eastern Railway, its own shortcut across Manchuria to Vladivostok.
  • 1902: Britain and Japan sign an alliance. Negotiations over Manchuria and Korea between Tokyo and St Petersburg run through 1903 and fail.
  • 8–9 February 1904: the attack at Port Arthur. The Qing court declares neutrality; the land campaigns are fought in Manchuria and Korea, over Chinese and Korean ground.
  • 2 January 1905: Port Arthur surrenders after a siege of roughly eleven months. February–March 1905: Mukden (today Shenyang), the largest land battle of the war. 27–28 May 1905: the Baltic fleet, having sailed most of the way around the world, is destroyed at Tsushima.
  • 5 September 1905: the Treaty of Portsmouth, mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt, who receives the Nobel Peace Prize for it the following year. Japan takes over the Liaodong lease and the southern half of the Manchurian railway, gets southern Sakhalin and a free hand in Korea, and no money. News of the terms brings rioting in Tokyo. Korea is annexed in 1910.
  • Inside Russia, 1905 runs from Bloody Sunday in January to the October Manifesto and an elected Duma. Inside China, the Qing abolish the thousand-year-old imperial examination system in September 1905, as part of the reforms that follow the Boxer settlement.
  • Military dead are usually estimated in the tens of thousands on each side, with wide ranges between authorities; Chinese civilian losses in the war zone were never securely counted.

2 The ladder

Five narrators who actually narrate the war, ordered from thinnest agency (a war that turns into a catastrophe by itself) to thickest (a named fleet attacking without a declaration). Ukraine’s volume starts in 1945, Germany’s is a National Socialism booklet, Sudan’s syllabus is regional; they are in the receipts. Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event column here. The two classrooms standing closest to this war — Russia’s and China’s — are in the box below the table, and they are there for different reasons.

The two closest classrooms — and why they are off the ladder

Russia (Чубарьян 2023) — a scope boundary. This volume covers 1914–1945, so the war is before its first page. It reaches back for it exactly once, in a subordinate clause about how the Entente was assembled: «…которая, в свою очередь, стремилась к сближению с Великобританией после поражения в Русско-японской войне» — “…which, for its part, strove for a rapprochement with Great Britain after the defeat in the Russo-Japanese War.” No date, no place, no Japan doing anything, no ships. The defeat is a timestamp on a diplomatic move. Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл., Просвещение 2023 · extract p. 26 (printed 25)

China (中外历史纲要 上, 2019) — an in-scope zero. 0 mentions This volume’s late-Qing unit is the one that would carry the war, and it does not name it. Stems searched in the original: 日俄 · 俄日 · 日俄战争 · 对马 · 旅顺 · 朴茨茅斯 · 朴次茅斯 · 满洲 · 沙俄 · 俄国 · 奉天 · 1904 · 1905. What comes back: 对马 appears once, inside 对马克思列宁主义 (“towards Marxism-Leninism”); 旅顺 appears four times — in the 1894–95 war, in a Boxer-era passage, in a Liang Qichao quotation, and on a map — but never in 1904–05; 1904 is the date of a school-system decree and of a Sun Yat-sen letter; 1905 is the founding of the Tongmenghui; 奉天 is a city on a list of places that protested the Twenty-One Demands in 1915; 沙皇俄国 is Russia arriving on the Amur in the seventeenth century. Five of the six in-scope books cover this war. This one is the sixth.

3 The tell

Every book here agrees on the result. Japan won; Russia lost; something in the world order moved. The disagreement starts one step earlier, in the sentence where the fighting begins, and it does not stop there — because the sentence where the fighting begins is also the sentence that has to decide where the fighting is.

Belarus writes the most interesting version of the agentless opening, because it is agentless in only one half. The first half has a subject and a purpose: the ruling circles of Russia believed; a small but victorious war would help strengthen; the government went to war. A war is being started here, in a room, for a domestic reason. Then the second half loses its subject entirely — the war «обернулась катастрофой», turned into a catastrophe, as though it had turned over in its sleep. Japan is not in either sentence. It arrives thirteen pages later, in the chapter about Japan, where it “inflicted defeat on Russia.” The one book that explains why Russia wanted a war is also a book in which the war, once started, has no opponent in it.

Lowe’s version is the shortest causal chain in the corpus: Japan decides to occupy neighbouring territories, “first Korea and then Manchuria were ‘colonized’, but this caused two wars.” The scare quotes are around colonized, not around anything Russian. Nobody attacks anybody; a policy causes wars, and then Japan is “victorious in both.” On page 7 the war is a single line in the time chart of the causes of the First World War, one line below “Britain and Japan sign alliance” and two lines above “Britain builds first ‘Dreadnought’ battleship.” It is furniture again, moved into place so that 1914 can be arranged around it — the same move the Opium Wars got in the same book.

At the bottom of the ladder the two market survey books both name the attack, and they weight it differently in the same number of words. OpenStax puts a Russian refusal in front of it — “When Russia refused to compromise, Japan attacked” — so that the first Japanese verb arrives already answered. Sabbatucci–Vidotto build the opposite emphasis with an Italian cleft: Furono però i giapponesi a prendere l’iniziativa — “it was however the Japanese who took the initiative.” They have already said the Russians refused every negotiation; the però concedes it and moves on. Then they add the one clause no other book in the corpus adds: senza alcuna dichiarazione di guerra. Of five classrooms that tell this war, one mentions that it started without a declaration.

«Затем нанесла поражение России в русско-японской войне 1904—1905 гг. Согласно Портсмутскому мирному договору Япония закрепила за собой Корею, получила от России Южный Сахалин и право на аренду китайских портов Порт-Артур и Дальний (ныне — Люйшунь и Далянь).»

“Then it inflicted defeat on Russia in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905. According to the Treaty of Portsmouth, Japan secured Korea for itself, received from Russia Southern Sakhalin and the right to lease the Chinese ports of Port Arthur and Dalny (now — Lüshun and Dalian).”

BY · Всемирная история, 11 кл. (БГУ 2021) · extract p. 71 (printed 70)

Read that sentence for its grammar rather than its content. The verb belongs to Japan. The direct object belongs to Russia. And China is an adjective — китайских, “Chinese,” attached to two ports that are handed from one empire to another without their owner appearing as a noun. Belarus is being more exact than most: it is the only book in the corpus that prints both the imperial names and the names on today’s map, Люйшунь and Далянь, so a student can find the place. It is still a sentence in which a country’s ports change hands and the country is a modifier.

Now line up the whole ladder by that one question — what nationality does the ground have? OpenStax: Russia “leased land from China,” so China is a party you sign with, three sentences before the war and absent inside it. Lowe: “South Manchuria,” a region with privileges attached, and in the 1931 chapter “the province.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto: Manchuria, the North-East, the “agonizing Chinese Empire” being carved into spheres — the most detailed geography here, in which China is the thing being divided rather than the place being fought over. And then NCERT, in twelve words inside a parenthesis: a war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory. That is the entire finding of this chapter, and it is a parenthesis in an Indian textbook.

The books that do the geography also do the myth, and they do it at different temperatures. Lowe: “this was the first time that an Asian country had defeated one of the European great powers.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto say the same thing and then name what broke.

«Per la prima volta nell’età moderna un paese asiatico batteva in un’autentica guerra una grande potenza europea, distruggendo in un sol colpo il mito della supremazia militare e tecnologica del vecchio continente e quello di una presunta superiorità della “razza bianca”.»

“For the first time in the modern age an Asian country was beating, in an authentic war, a great European power, destroying at a single stroke the myth of the military and technological supremacy of the old continent and that of a presumed superiority of the ‘white race’.”

IT · Il mondo contemporaneo (Laterza 2004) · p. 236

“In an authentic war” is doing quiet work: 1905 counted, in a way colonial campaigns had not been allowed to count. And the myth is named in two parts, military and racial, with the second in quotation marks. The same book has set this up a page earlier by dating a phrase — «un “pericolo giallo”: un’espressione coniata dall’imperatore di Germania Guglielmo II ai tempi della rivolta dei boxers e diventata d’attualità soprattutto dopo la guerra russogiapponese del 1904-5», “a ‘yellow peril’: an expression coined by the emperor of Germany William II at the time of the Boxer revolt and become topical above all after the Russo-Japanese war of 1904-5.” One classroom of eleven tells a student that the phrase had an author and a launch date.

One more count, because it sorts the books the same way the ground does. Not one of the five gives a number of dead. Not for Port Arthur, not for Mukden, not for Tsushima, not for the villages the armies fought across. There are treaty terms in every column and casualties in none. Ch. 2 found the opposite pattern in Africa — numbers present, but always the European army’s. Here the numbers are not printed at all, in a war that killed people on a scale the boring facts above had to estimate in ranges.

Which brings the chapter back to the empty column. China’s volume is not thin on this ground; it is unusually thorough about it. It prints the map of the spheres of influence, it prints the 1903 cartoon of the bear and the tiger and the frog reaching for China, and it states the lease in the plainest available verb: 「各帝国主义国家纷纷在中国划分势力范围，在从渤海到南海的中国沿海地区强租租借地：俄国强租旅大，英国租威海卫，德国租胶州湾……」 — “The imperialist countries one after another divided spheres of influence in China, and forcibly leased concessions along the Chinese coast from the Bohai Sea to the South China Sea: Russia forcibly leased Lüda, Britain leased Weihaiwei, Germany leased Jiaozhou Bay…” A footnote on the same page explains the abbreviation: 「旅大，指旅顺口、大连湾地区。」 — “Lüda refers to the Lüshunkou and Dalian Bay area.” The book walks the student onto the exact ground, names it twice, and then the years 1904 and 1905 arrive carrying other business.

The clearest way to see the shape of the gap is to put two textbooks’ sentences about the same Chinese reform side by side. NCERT: “In 1905, just after the Russo-Japanese war (a war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory) the centuries-old Chinese examination system … was abolished.” 中外历史纲要 上, in its box on the late-Qing New Policies: 「其四，推行教育改革，废除科举，兴办学堂，建立起一套较为完整的学校制度。」 — “Fourth, carry out educational reform, abolish the examination system, establish schools, and build a relatively complete school system.” Same reform, same year. The Indian book dates it by the war. The Chinese book lists it without one.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

NCERT (IN) earns the credit line for twelve words in brackets. “A war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory” is the shortest entry on this ladder and the only one that gives the battlefield a nationality; it is also the only line in the corpus that makes the war do explanatory work inside Chinese history rather than European or Japanese history. The Indian book has no national stake in that sentence — which is the thesis of this book stated in one parenthesis, and a reminder that distance is a resource, not a virtue.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) takes the depth lead and part of the honesty lead. It is the only book with a campaign: the Manchurian quarrel, the British treaty of 1903 signed to keep Russia busy in the Far East, the undeclared attack, the eleven-month siege, Mukden, the Baltic fleet’s circumnavigation, Tsushima, American mediation. It is the only one that says the attack came without a declaration of war, the only one that names the racial myth the defeat destroyed, and the only one that dates “yellow peril” to a man and an occasion. It also runs the consequences both ways — into the Russian revolution of 1905 and into “a powerful impulse to the national and anti-colonial struggles of the Asian peoples.”

Кошелев (BY) gets a narrower but real credit: it is the only book that asks what the war was for, domestically, and the answer it gives is unflattering to the government it stands closest to. “A small but victorious war would help strengthen the position of the tsarist regime” is a phrase out of Russian political memory rather than a neutral summary , and the book prints it without quotation marks and without attribution, as the plain motive. It is the only place in this corpus where a war is started to fix a domestic problem — and it is the seed of the ch. 6 chapter, where the same book has to explain 1917.

OpenStax (US) is the clearest on the object of the quarrel. It builds Port Arthur before it destroys it — the frozen harbour at Vladivostok, the search for an ice-free port, the lease, the new town — so that a student can see what two empires were fighting over. It is also the only book that keeps the thread alive across three chapters: the war on p. 363, Japan “emboldened by its success against Russia” in 1914 on p. 441, and the railway lease it won showing up under the feet of the Japanese army in 1931 on p. 525.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is five books, and the corpus baseline behind the finding is six. Ukraine’s volume runs 1945–2018, Germany’s is a National Socialism booklet, Sudan’s secondary history is regional; those zeros are syllabus boundaries and say nothing about this war. Kazakhstan’s book is a national history and is never a world-event column here. Russia’s volume begins in 1914, which is why its single retrospective clause sits in a box rather than on the ladder: it is evidence about what a 1914–1945 textbook reaches back for, not evidence about how Russia teaches 1905. The corpus wants a Russian world-history volume that covers the period, and does not have one.

The Chinese zero is the chapter’s load-bearing claim, so state its limits precisely. It is a zero in this volume — 中外历史纲要 上, the first of two, whose late-Qing and 1911 units are exactly where the war would sit. The searches above were run on the original Chinese extraction, and every non-zero stem was opened and read. A second-volume or a supplementary-reader mention would not be caught by this method, and the Chinese curriculum is not one book. There is also a competing explanation that does not involve avoidance: this volume’s late-Qing unit is organised around Chinese actors — Lin Zexu, the Taiping, the reformers, Sun Yat-sen — and a war between two foreign powers on a neutral Qing’s territory has no Chinese protagonist to hang a lesson on. Against that: the same unit narrates other impositions in which China is the object at length, including the lease of the very port the war was fought for. Under this book’s rule — a zero counts as an avoidance signal when most in-scope narrators cover the topic — five of six is over the line. It remains a signal, not a verdict, and the honest form of the finding is the one printed above: the ground is the omitted party, and the classroom standing on it is the one that omits it.

Kept verbatim because errors are data. OpenStax dates the Liaodong lease to 1897; the Russo-Chinese convention is of March 1898, and 1897 is the year the Russian squadron wintered there. Sabbatucci–Vidotto prints “Fort Arthur” once and “Port Arthur” two lines later, across a page break, and says Portsmouth recognised a Japanese protectorate over Korea “which it already held de facto since 1895” — a strong claim about 1895 that the printed page should be checked for. It also gives Japan “southern Manchuria” at Portsmouth without saying that what changed hands was a lease and a railway.

Machine-translated files were used to find the pages and never to quote them. Every line above was verified against the original-language extraction; the Russian, Belarusian, Italian and Chinese are printed here in the original with a translation for reading. Belarusian and Russian page numbers are given as extract page with the printed page in brackets — both books’ PDFs run one ahead of the printed folio. Chinese printed pages run four behind the extraction; the passages cited are printed pp. 115 and 124.

Finally, none of these books is a history of this war, and one of them is not a history at all in the school sense — the Italian volume is a university manual, which is part of why it has room for a siege. If you want the war as it was experienced from a village in Liaoning, or from the lower deck at Tsushima, every column here will leave you unsatisfied.
