India 1930–1947

On 12 March 1930, a sixty-year-old man in a spun cotton dhoti steps out of his ashram at Sabarmati toward the Arabian Sea, beginning a two-hundred-mile march to gather untaxed salt from the coastal water. Seventeen years later, at midnight on 14 August 1947, the British Empire in India divides into two independent dominions. What seven classrooms do with those seventeen years is the subject of this chapter.

The question · causes

Why did Britain leave India — as a planned gift of democracy, a hard-won victory of popular struggle, or a retreat under wartime exhaustion and American pressure?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 13 April 1919: Jallianwala Bagh (Amritsar) massacre. British Indian Army troops under Brigadier General Reginald Dyer fire on an unarmed crowd in a enclosed garden; official Hunter Committee reports 379 dead; Indian National Congress estimates over 1,000 killed.
  • 12 March – 6 April 1930: Salt March (Dandi Satyagraha). Gandhi leads a 240-mile march to Dandi to break the British salt monopoly, launching a nationwide civil disobedience campaign; approximately 60,000 people are arrested.
  • August 1935: Government of India Act 1935 passed by British Parliament, granting provincial autonomy and expanding the voter franchise to roughly 10–15% of the adult population.
  • August 1942: Indian National Congress adopts the "Quit India" resolution demanding immediate British withdrawal; Gandhi and major Congress leaders are arrested and detained for much of World War II.
  • 1943: Bengal Famine. An estimated 2.1 to 3 million people die of starvation and disease in Bengal during World War II under British wartime grain requisitions and transport priority policies.
  • 16 August 1946: Muslim League calls for "Direct Action Day" to demand Pakistan; communal riots in Calcutta result in an estimated 4,000 to 5,000 deaths.
  • 14–15 August 1947: Indian Independence Act takes effect. British India is partitioned into the independent Dominions of Pakistan (14 August) and India (15 August); subsequent mass population transfers trigger communal violence with death tolls estimated between 200,000 and 1,000,000.
  • 30 January 1948: Mahatma Gandhi is assassinated in New Delhi by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators who cover the end of British rule in India, ordered from thinnest agency (independence as an abstract historical attainment) to thickest (a staged historiographical debate between British goodwill and Indian struggle). Books outside the period or genre — China’s national history, Germany’s NS booklet, Sudan’s regional syllabus, and Kazakhstan’s national history — are in the receipts, not on the ladder.

3 The tell

Every narrator on this ladder agrees that British rule ended in August 1947 and that two independent states emerged from the partition. The disagreement is about the grammar of departure: who decided, who yielded, and who carried the bill.

The top three rows rely on administrative passive verbs. Belarus states that India and Pakistan attained independence, framing non-violent resistance as a factor that played a role after the Second World War. Ukraine frames departure as an announcement by the colonial administration, noting that the Muslim League found support from the metropolis in establishing a separate state. Russia attributes departure to a British decision («приняла решение предоставить»), ending its chapter with an explicit verdict: England, having left India, left it democracy.

The core divergence of this chapter appears when comparing the British textbook against the rest of the corpus. Norman Lowe’s UK survey devotes four pages to the 1945–1947 negotiations, detailing Clement Attlee, Ernest Bevin, Lord Mountbatten, and M. A. Jinnah’s demand for Pakistan. Yet search the text for the 1919 massacre at Amritsar: it is absent. The event that converted Indian anti-colonial protest into a mass national movement does not appear in the British survey’s section on Indian independence. By beginning the narrative in 1945, the UK classroom frames departure as an act of Labour goodwill, stating that the government wanted to show that it disapproved of exploiting the Indians and was anxious to press ahead with independence, on both moral and economic grounds.

“Attlee maintained that Britain could not be blamed for the violence; it was caused by the failure of the Indians to agree among themselves.” UK · Norman Lowe, Mastering Modern World History (2013) · p. 762

The American textbook, OpenStax, presents a different set of causal factors. It records the 1919 Amritsar shooting in detail—naming the closed square, the British Indian Army opening fire, and the hundreds killed—and treats it as the catalyst for Gandhi’s leadership. When explaining 1947, OpenStax names two factors absent from the British text: Britain was exhausted from fighting World War II, and its ally, the United States, pressured the nation to grant India its independence. What the UK textbook presents as a moral decision by London, the US textbook presents as military exhaustion and American diplomatic leverage.

Lowe’s text remains unique on the ladder for another reason: it explicitly stages a historiographical debate. After presenting the British narrative of planned constitutional devolution, Lowe cites Indian historians Sumit Sarkar and Anita Inder Singh, who argue that independence was never a long-term goal of the British but rather the hard-won fruit of struggle and sacrifice. Lowe includes the counter-argument, then closes with Prime Minister Attlee’s verdict that Britain could not be blamed for the violence. The textbook acknowledges the debate while preserving the exculpation of the imperial state.

Finally, India’s NCERT Class XI survey acts as a self-pole narrator by organizing the period through campaign milestones: Non-Cooperation (1921), Quit India (1942), Independence (1947), and Republic (1950). In this world-history volume, the detailed narrative of colonial administration is minimized, delegating the full history of struggle to the national history curriculum.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

OpenStax (US) earns the primary credit line for this chapter. It covers the full arc of the movement—from the 1919 Amritsar massacre to the 1930 Salt March, the 1942 Quit India movement, Subhas Chandra Bose’s Indian National Army, and the 1943 Bengal Famine—while candidly naming American diplomatic pressure and wartime economic exhaustion as drivers of British withdrawal.

Lowe (UK) deserves recognition for historiographical honesty. Although its narrative minimizes pre-1945 colonial violence, it explicitly introduces students to Indian historians (Sumit Sarkar and Anita Inder Singh) who challenge the official British narrative of voluntary self-government.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) is credited for structural causality, linking the 1919 Amritsar massacre directly to the breakdown of British moral authority and providing an unvarnished account of the human toll of partition.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder comprises seven textbooks whose scope includes twentieth-century world history. It does not represent all national curricula. China’s national history textbook treats India primarily in ancient times and post-1950 diplomacy; Germany’s volume is restricted to National Socialism; Sudan’s syllabus focuses on regional history (though it notes Gandhi as an intellectual inspiration on page 98); and Kazakhstan’s volume represents national history rather than a general world-history survey.

Casualty figures vary significantly across sources. Partition death tolls are cited as 100,000 in Ukrainian and Italian texts, 250,000 in the British text, and estimated by modern historians between 200,000 and 1,000,000. Amritsar casualties range from 379 in British official reports to "almost 400" in Italian texts, "hundreds" in US texts, and "over 1 thousand" in Russian texts. These variations reflect differences in primary reporting between colonial authorities and nationalist organizations.
