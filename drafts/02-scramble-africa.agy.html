Scramble for Africa

In Khartoum in January 1885, followers of the Mahdi break through defensive perimeter lines and capture the governor’s palace, ending joint Ottoman-Egyptian and British control over the city. In London, three thousand miles away, the loss of the city dominates parliamentary debates and newspaper front pages for months. What seven national classrooms do with the African conquest that followed—and who gets to hold the story’s steering wheel—is the subject of this chapter.

The question · space

How many pages does a conquest take — and does the country being carved get to name its own revolution?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 1881–1885: The Mahdist uprising in Sudan defeats Egyptian garrisons, capturing Khartoum in January 1885 and establishing an independent Mahdist state under Muhammad Ahmad al-Mahdi.
  • 1884–1885: The Berlin Conference, convened by German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck, establishes European ground rules for claiming African territory under the principle of "effective occupation."
  • 1885–1908: King Leopold II of Belgium operates the Congo Free State as a private personal domain, extracting wild rubber and ivory through forced labor before international outcry leads to transfer to the Belgian state in 1908.
  • 1896: Battle of Adwa: Ethiopian forces under Emperor Menelik II defeat an invading Italian army, securing Ethiopian independence by treaty.
  • 1898: Fashoda Incident: British and French military expeditions meet at Fashoda on the White Nile; France withdraws its force, avoiding war and enabling later Anglo-French alignment.
  • 1899–1902: Second Boer War: Britain annexes the South African Republic and Orange Free State after fighting Afrikaans-speaking Boer republics.
  • By 1914, European powers claim control over approximately 90 percent of the African continent, leaving only Ethiopia and Liberia as independent states.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators who cover the partition of Africa, ordered from thinnest agency (passive background takeover without European names or conference) to thickest (active African resistance and liberation as primary subject). Books outside the period or syllabus scope—Ukraine 1945–2018, China’s national history volume, and Germany’s NS booklet—are absent from the ladder rows. That absence reflects syllabus boundaries, not a historical verdict.

3 The tell

Every narrator in this set agrees that by 1914 nearly the entire African continent had passed under European rule. The disagreement lies in the page allocation and grammatical subject assigned to the conquest.

Norman Lowe’s UK textbook presents the Scramble for Africa in a single paragraph embedded within Chapter 1 as background context for the origins of the First World War. In this single paragraph, no African countries are named, no European statesmen or explorers appear, and the Berlin Conference is unmentioned. The partition occurs in the passive voice: "Most of Africa was taken over by the European states." The nation whose empire held the largest share of the continent describes the partition as a background setup of markets and raw materials necessary to explain European rivalries in 1914.

India’s NCERT text similarly compresses the partition into a brief timeline entry and a side box, noting that European countries "reached an agreement to divide up Africa as colonies for themselves." The Russian and Belarusian volumes frame the partition through systemic economic vocabulary: Chubaryan defines it as the essence of "new imperialism," while Koshelev labels Africa a "continent of classical colonialism" where territory was divided among capitalist powers to secure raw materials and high profits for monopolies.

وجاءت هذه العوامل كلها مجتمعة في واقع السودان في عام 1881م عندما اندلعت الثورة المهدية لتبلغ قمة نجاحها باكتساح الخرطوم وتحريرها في يناير 1885م.

“And all these factors came together in the reality of Sudan in the year 1881 AD when the Mahdist Revolution erupted, to reach the peak of its success with the sweeping of Khartoum and its liberation in January 1885 AD.”

SD · التاريخ (History for the Third Grade), Ministry of Education 2009 · p. 14

Sudan’s state textbook flips both the space allocation and the narrative perspective. Across twenty-eight pages (pages 14–41), the curriculum details the Mahdist Revolution, celebrating the January 1885 capture of Khartoum as the moment Muhammad Ahmad al-Mahdi "liberated the country" from foreign rule. Where European textbooks see an administrative backdrop or a field of colonial rivalry, Khartoum’s syllabus sees a struggle for national liberation. When the textbook later describes the British reconquest of 1898 and the 1899 Condominium Agreement, it refers to British forces as "the invaders" extending control over Sudan.

Italy’s volume by Sabbatucci and Vidotto provides the most granular diplomatic and geographic breakdown among the European books. It stages Bismarck’s 1884–85 Berlin Conference as giving the "first sanction to the partition of Africa," highlights the September 1898 Fashoda Incident on the Nile between British and French military columns as a signature turning point, and describes King Leopold’s Congo Free State as a "paradoxical euphemism" masking rapacious dominion. Crucially, the Italian authors record that European diplomats drew borders on maps "without taking any account of tribal divisions and of pre-existing ethno-linguistic realities."

The US OpenStax text introduces explicit quantitative metrics and moral framing: it records that European control expanded from 10 percent to 90 percent of the continent, emphasizes that partition was conducted "without input from Africans," and details the atrocities committed under Leopold II, including the cutting of hands by the Force Publique to maintain tallies of forced laborers killed.

A striking finding across the corpus is the casualty counting asymmetry. Western and Eastern European textbooks tally European military losses with numerical precision: OpenStax notes 6,000 Italian soldiers killed at Adwa, while Koshelev records 11,000 Italian losses in the same battle and 28,000 British dead in the Boer War. Yet African battlefield casualties during the conquest of the continent go uncounted across all non-African textbooks. The sole volume in the eleven-country corpus that counts African fallen is Sudan’s textbook, which tallies ten thousand Ansar killed during the 1882 siege of El Obeid.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Sudan (SD) earns the credit lead for center-staging African agency and anti-colonial resistance. It alone in the corpus devotes substantial page volume (28 pages) to an African state-building project and military victory, transforming what other curricula treat as passive colonial terrain into a narrative of national liberation against foreign rule.

Sabbatucci & Vidotto (IT) deserves credit for diplomatic and cartographic candor. It alone among European state-approved texts explicitly highlights the arbitrary nature of straight-line borders drawn across African ethnic boundaries, while providing detailed operational coverage of key turning points like the 1898 Fashoda confrontation.

OpenStax (US) merits recognition for quantitative clarity and sequence honesty. It clearly measures the rapid expansion of European control from 10% to 90% and explicitly records that European powers allotted African territory among themselves "without input from Africans."

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This comparison covers seven national textbooks that include the partition of Africa within their general world history or national history framework. It does not represent every textbook published in these countries, but rather the specific state-approved or dominant market editions in our corpus.

Three volumes in our corpus are out of chronological or thematic scope for the Scramble: Ukraine’s 11th-grade textbook covers 1945–2018; China’s state volume (*中外历史纲要 上*) focuses on national Chinese history from antiquity to 2017; and Germany’s BPB volume focuses strictly on National Socialism and the Holocaust (1939–1945). Their absence from the ladder is a matter of volume scope, not syllabus avoidance.

Kazakhstan’s 11th-grade volume (*История Казахстана*) covers national Kazakh history and is excluded from world-history peer ladders under our corpus rules, remaining tracked in the receipts.

One finding kept verbatim because errors are data: Sudan’s textbook twice lists "Berlin Conference 1878 AD, Berlin Conference 1884 AD," conflating the 1878 Congress of Berlin (Balkan crisis diplomacy under Bismarck) with the 1884–85 Berlin Conference (African partition). In addition, Sudan’s Arabic PDF extraction contains ligature formatting artifacts and requires an eye-check against the physical printed page before final publication.
