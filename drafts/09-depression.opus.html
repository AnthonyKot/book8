The Great Depression

In 1931 men in overcoats and hats queue along a Chicago pavement for free food; the kitchen handing it out was paid for by Al Capone. In the same years, on another continent, a government is buying food in order to destroy it: Brazil takes its own coffee off the market and burns it in the open, dumps it at sea, and shovels it into locomotive fireboxes, to keep the price of the rest from falling. The last chapter ended in 1943 with rice on sale a district away from people who could not pay for it; this one begins fourteen years earlier, with food destroyed to defend what it costs. Between the queue and the fire there is a fall in share prices: thirteen million shares change hands on the floor at Wall Street on Thursday 24 October 1929, sixteen million the following Tuesday, and by the middle of November the quoted prices are roughly half what they were in September. Nothing has been shelled, nobody has crossed a border, and no government has fallen.

The question · agency

When does an economic collapse stop behaving like weather on the page and become a decision made by somebody?

1 What was done with the coffee

Coffee was roughly seven-tenths of Brazil’s export earnings in 1929, and for a generation the state had held the world price up by warehousing its own surplus and borrowing abroad against the stock. Then American demand collapsed and New York banks began calling in their foreign loans, so Brazil could neither sell the crop nor borrow against it. It bought the crop and destroyed it: roughly 78 million sixty-kilo bags between 1931 and 1944, while men queued for free food in Chicago. In October 1930 the coffee oligarchy’s hold on the presidency broke and Getúlio Vargas took power on 3 November; in 1933 the Departamento Nacional do Café was created to run the destruction as policy, with a staff and a budget.

The burning was not madness, and it was not wickedness. It was price-support arithmetic — buy the surplus, hold the price — and it had worked for twenty-five years against ordinary harvest swings, because a good harvest is a temporary problem and the borrowed money comes back when the price recovers. What the arithmetic could not survive was a collapse in demand, because the lender has to believe the world will start drinking again. When the lenders stopped, the only lever left was the fire. A country that owned no shares in New York had its national income cut in half by a fall in share prices in New York, and answered with the one instrument it controlled: the supply of its own crop.

Three classrooms put the coffee on the page, and none of them follows it out of the warehouse. Russia lists what the world already could not sell before the crash — «Мировой рынок сельскохозяйственных продуктов: пшеницы, сахара, кофе, каучука, шерсти, жиров — был переполнен уже в 1928 г.», “the world market in agricultural products — wheat, sugar, coffee, rubber, wool, fats — was already overstocked in 1928.” Italy puts numbers on who was hurt worst: between 1929 and 1932 world output of manufactures fell 30 per cent and of raw materials 26, while prices fell sharply in industry and, «soprattutto», in agriculture, “where the drop was more than 50 per cent”; in its Latin America chapter the crisis strikes a whole continent by “making the prices of raw materials and foodstuffs collapse.” Belarus gets one sentence further than anyone else. It names what Brazil sold — «фрукты, кофе и сахар», “fruit, coffee and sugar” — and then, of the collapse in demand that followed: «В Бразилии и на Кубе произошли революции», “in Brazil and in Cuba revolutions occurred.”

That is the revolution. It occurred in the same grammar the rest of this chapter is about — произошли, they happened, nobody made them — and what happened was that the men who grew the coffee lost the presidency to a man who would burn it. Coffee is on the table in every country in this book, and the price its grower is paid is still set somewhere else. That is the same arrangement that let a government in 1931 decide the crop was worth more as locomotive fuel than as coffee.

2 Through the customs house

The crash was eight months old when the United States raised its tariff. Herbert Hoover signed the Smoot–Hawley Act on 17 June 1930 over a public petition from 1,028 economists asking him to veto it; other states retaliated; the value of world trade fell by roughly two-thirds in three years. The ladder is unusually generous about this. OpenStax hands the mechanism to its own government by name — Smoot-Hawley “was the highest tariff the U.S. government has ever enacted. It helped stifle world trade, which decreased 30 percent by the early 1930s” — and notes that “an increased tariff of nearly 40 percent had already been enacted in 1922.” Lowe names that earlier one, the Fordney–McCumber tariff, and works the return leg: it “helped to keep foreign goods out,” but it also stopped European states “making much-needed profits from trade with the USA,” without which they could not buy American goods or pay American war debts, “and many states retaliated.” Italy makes it a decision rather than a statute: the United States, “rather than taking on the responsibilities attached to the role of hegemonic power,” hardened protectionism. Russia records the outcome with nobody in the subject slot at all — barriers “in the form of customs duties” were raised across the path of goods.

The tariff did not cause the Depression, and the chapter’s own spine depends on saying that exactly. What made the slump so deep is mostly a monetary story: banks failed and the money supply contracted, and the gold standard obliged every government that wanted to keep its currency convertible to answer a slump by tightening rather than loosening — which is why the countries that left gold early, Britain in September 1931, recovered earlier than the countries that held on, France until 1936. What the tariff did was travel. It shut the channel through which other countries earned the dollars they bought American goods with, and it made the same move rational for every government that traded, so the contraction propagated country by country through the customs house rather than through the stock exchange. That is why it reached a cotton budget in Khartoum and a coffee warehouse in Santos, neither of which owned a share.

The men who designed the postwar economy treated that spiral as the thing to be engineered against, and wrote the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade — Geneva, 30 October 1947, twenty-three countries — whose entire purpose was to stop 1930 happening again; it became the World Trade Organization in 1995, and chapter 14 is where that order gets built. The classrooms that teach GATT at all teach it in a later unit — Lowe in his United Nations chapter, OpenStax at § 15.1, Sabbatucci–Vidotto at § 22.2 — and none of them names the tariff it was written against. OpenStax comes within a word: GATT “was designed to prevent the reemergence of prewar-style trade barriers.” Whose barriers, it does not say, on a page 135 pages after the one where it named them. Whenever tariffs come back into the news, the question the 1930s answer is not whether a country can protect its own producers. It is who loses the market in which they earn the money to buy.

3 Three bands

  • Share prices on the New York Stock Exchange peak in early September 1929. Heavy selling on 24 October (“Black Thursday”) and 29 October (“Black Tuesday”) is followed by a decline that runs, with rallies, until July 1932.
  • American output falls by roughly a third and unemployment reaches about a quarter of the labour force by 1933. Around nine thousand US banks suspend operations between 1930 and 1933.
  • Prices of primary commodities — wheat, cotton, coffee, rubber, sugar — fall further and faster than prices of manufactured goods, so producer countries outside Europe and North America are hit through the price of what they sell rather than through a stock exchange.
  • Britain suspends the gold convertibility of the pound on 21 September 1931; the United States devalues in 1933; France holds on until 1936.
  • Germany’s registered unemployed pass six million in early 1932; the NSDAP becomes the largest party in the Reichstag that July; Hitler is appointed chancellor on 30 January 1933. Japan’s Kwantung Army seizes Mukden on the night of 18 September 1931. Franklin Roosevelt is inaugurated on 4 March 1933.
  • Economists still argue about the causes and about how much the New Deal did. The chronology above is not in dispute; the wiring is.

The narrators sort into three bands. The first treats the Depression as weather — something that occurs, and after which nobody in particular does anything. The second turns it into policy and blame, and names an institution. The third follows it out of the financial pages to a payroll and an invasion, where somebody decides what happens to identifiable people. Read the middle column for the sentence that comes after the fall.

A clique, a motive, a war

Every narrator here writes the fall itself without a human subject. A fall occurred. A crash occurred. The exchange crashes. A crisis broke out. Buying “began to slow down.” In the whole ladder there is not one sentence of the form “X crashed the market,” and unlike chapters 1, 4 and 8, that empty slot is not a dodge: there is no plausible name to put in it. So the interesting sentence is the next one.

In the first band the next sentence lets nobody act, or lets one man act by omission. NCERT’s cell is a chain of intransitive verbs — the exchange crashes, the Depression follows, twelve million are out of work — and the book’s only other mention of the slump is a subordinate clause in a chapter about the dispossession of Native Americans; Roosevelt, Hoover and the New Deal appear nowhere in the volume. Belarus fills the gap with an abstraction: what comes out of the crisis is “the Western democracies,” which had “stepped onto the path of state regulation.” The words «новый курс» — New Deal — are not printed anywhere in Koshelev’s volume, and neither is «Уолл-стрит»; Roosevelt is in the book three times and never as the man who ended a depression. Russia is the one book in this band that puts a person in the subject slot, and gives him a verb that means the absence of action: Hoover, expecting prosperity “somewhere around the corner,” бездействовал — did nothing. Nobody acts; democracies act; a president acts by omission. Three books, three ways of writing an empty chair.

The second band names an institution, and each one names an institution at home. Lowe blames a party, in a heading — § 22.6(d) is titled “Who was to blame for the disaster?” — and then refuses the easy target, clearing “the unfortunate President Hoover” personally and indicting the governing party instead; he is also the only narrator who takes the famous event off the causal chain, demoting the crash to “just a symptom,” and the only one who prints the figures underneath his own argument, industrial wages up 8 per cent and profits up 72 between 1923 and 1929. OpenStax names a law. The American survey opens by refusing single agency and then hands the transmission mechanism to its own government by name — the highest tariff ever enacted, world trade down 30 per cent — while Herbert Hoover is not mentioned once in the whole volume. That is a swap of a person for a policy, and the policy is the one with foreign victims. Sabbatucci and Vidotto name a state and a duty it declined: the planetary effects were made worse because the USA, “rather than taking on the responsibilities attached to the role of hegemonic power,” chose to defend its own production. Three narrators, three defendants, none of them a foreigner.

The last two rungs change the shape of the chapter.

Sudan’s ministry textbook never uses the words “Great Depression,” “Wall Street” or “Roosevelt.” It has no crash at all. The crisis enters as a price: the Gezira scheme had made cotton about 40 per cent of government income and two-thirds of exports, demand fell, the price fell, “and the government resorted to strict economic measures, so it reduced employment and salaries.” A government is the subject, and what it does is subtract. Sixteen pages on, the same event is told again from the other end — the 1931 Gordon College strike, led by Makki al-Manna, against a cut in Sudanese graduates’ salaries from eight pounds to five and a half — and the book adds the clause that no other column has any use for: but the government did not reduce the salaries of the British and the foreigners. Most of these classrooms measure the Depression in millions out of work. This one measures it as the gap between two payrolls in the same office.

China gives the crisis its only fully transitive sentence on the ladder. In 中外历史纲要 the world crisis is not a topic; it is a subordinate clause, four characters of scale — 严重影响日本, gravely affecting Japan — and then the main clause arrives with a subject that wants something: the Japanese ruling clique, “in order to ease internal contradictions and escape its predicament, was impatient to launch a war of aggression against China.” Three sentences later the Kwantung Army is blowing up a railway near Liutiaohu. There is no unemployment figure, no bank, no tariff, no Roosevelt. The Depression is not an economy in this classroom. It is a motive.

The bands do not sort by ideology. Belarus and Russia are the two books most willing to call the crisis a crisis of capitalism as a system — «кризис определённого этапа развития капиталистической системы» — and they are the two least willing to let anyone act. What the descent tracks is something narrower: once a textbook follows the Depression out of the financial pages to a payroll or an invasion, the abstractions give way to officials making choices about identifiable people. In the first two bands the victims are aggregates — thirteen million, twelve million, 80 per cent of families with no savings, one in eight farmers. In the third they have addresses: Sudanese graduates as against British and foreign staff, the Chinese Northeast. Both halves are true. Only one half can be argued with, because only one half says who was standing where.

Two books close the story with almost the same sentence. Lowe, at the end of his New Deal section: “It was the war that finally put an end to the depression.” OpenStax: “only the economic stimulation of the onset of World War II brought the world closer to a return of prosperity.” That is chronology, not a verdict on the New Deal — which reduced the pain and rewrote the social contract without closing the output gap, still 14 to 17 per cent unemployment in the late 1930s and under 2 per cent by 1943. And before the wartime spending arrived, the war was rehearsed. Spain is where the aircraft, the credit lines and the paper neutralities of the coming war were tried out — which is chapter 10.

The coffee kept burning through all of it, into 1944, and the arrangement behind it is still standing. The bag in the kitchen names a country and a roaster; neither of them set the price on it. That is quoted on an exchange in New York or London, and the people who grew the beans read it the way everyone else reads the weather — which is how a crop came to be worth more in a firebox than in a cup.

4 Credit where due

Fairness

MoE Sudan (SD) is the shortest text on the ladder and the only one that turns the Depression into a decision about named groups of people. Two paragraphs and a strike, no crash, no Wall Street, no Roosevelt — and one clause that no other book in the corpus contains: the government cut Sudanese salaries and did not cut British and foreign ones. Every other narrator here reports the crisis as something that happened to a population. A ministry textbook, published by the state that succeeded the administration doing the cutting, reports it as something an administration chose to do to one half of its own staff. That is the sentence this whole chapter was looking for, and it is in the book with the fewest pages to spare.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight pinned editions whose syllabus reaches 1929, not eight countries and not “the world.” Ukraine’s volume covers 1945–2018 and Germany’s booklet is a National Socialism primer; their zeros are syllabus boundaries, not avoidance. On this book’s own rule the topic is high-salience — every in-scope narrator mentions the crisis — so there is no avoidance signal anywhere in this chapter, in either direction. Kazakhstan is not a column here and would not be even if the genre allowed it: its book is national history, and its 1929 is collectivisation and revolt.

None of these eight books is an economic history, and the gap is exactly where this chapter had to go outside them. Not one of the eight explains the gold standard; the mechanism most economists put near the centre of the story — monetary contraction, and convertibility obliging governments to tighten into a slump — appears in this whole corpus as a single line about the pound in 1931. The § 2 account of why the slump was so deep is general history, not a textbook’s. If you want to know why the fall went as far as it did, every column here will leave you unsatisfied.

«Почему он не затронул экономику Советского государства?»

“Why did it not affect the economy of the Soviet state?”

BY · Всемирная история, 11 кл. (БГУ 2021), p. 124 — end-of-section exercise 3. The book does not answer it.

Italy supplies the observation the exercise rests on — the recession spread everywhere “with the significant exception of the USSR” — and no book on the ladder supplies the answer, which is that an economy trading little at world prices is not reached by a collapse in world prices. What the exercise also does not say is what those years were in the country it is asking about: the same span is forced collectivisation and, in Kazakhstan alone, a famine usually estimated at 1.5 million dead or more. A question printed beside a table of Western unemployment, with the local answer left off the page.

The treaty written against it: the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, Geneva, 30 October 1947, twenty-three signatories, is published in full by the WTO.

Now look up what your textbook calls it.
