Spain, 1936–1939

In July 1936, part of the Spanish army rises against the Republic from Morocco. Three years later Madrid falls and General Franco holds the state. Between those dates, Germany, Italy, and the Soviet Union put men and machines into a Spanish war, while Britain and France sign a neutrality paper that only they keep. Some classrooms call that paper “non-intervention.” One calls the same war a military general rehearsal. What each textbook needs the student to believe about those foreign hands — and which foreign words it will print — is the subject of this chapter.

The question · vocabulary

What do you call the foreign part of Spain’s war — non-intervention, volunteers, Condor Legion, or a general rehearsal?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • December 1931: Spain is a republic after the fall of the monarchy; the 1930s bring sharp left–right conflict, land and church fights, and regional autonomy disputes.
  • February 1936: a Popular Front coalition wins elections. July 1936: army officers launch a rising in Spanish Morocco; General Francisco Franco becomes the principal Nationalist leader. The conflict becomes a three-year civil war.
  • Italy and Germany supply arms, aircraft, and troops to the Nationalists; the Soviet Union supplies arms and advice to the Republic; tens of thousands of foreign volunteers fight in International Brigades on the Republican side.
  • Britain, France, and other states subscribe to a Non-Intervention Agreement (summer 1936); Germany, Italy, and the USSR also sign while continuing to arm clients.
  • 26 April 1937: the Basque market town of Guernica (Gernika) is destroyed by aerial bombardment; the German Condor Legion is the force named in several standard accounts. Picasso’s painting Guernica (1937) becomes the war’s best-known image.
  • March 1939: Madrid falls; Franco establishes a long authoritarian dictatorship (dies 1975). War dead in survey books often cluster near half a million; postwar repression adds many more victims.
  • Historians treat the war as both a Spanish social and political collapse and an international proxy: a place where fascist and anti-fascist coalitions practiced methods later used on a larger European scale. Who gets which foreign word in school is not a shared classroom fact.

Ukraine’s volume starts in 1945; China’s is a national history; those zeros are syllabus boundaries. Sudan and India are in scope for interwar Europe and still print zero lines on this war — avoidance against a 6-of-8 covering baseline, filed in the receipts. 3 The tell

Every covering book agrees that Spaniards fought Spaniards from 1936 to 1939, and that Franco won. The disagreement is about which foreign words the student is allowed to own — and which foreign actors get a proper name.

OpenStax opens with weather grammar: a coup “erupted.” Guernica is a civilian target that makes people “shudder,” not a German unit with a name. The democracy side gets a policy label — “nonintervention” — framed as prudence next to Japanese expansion and U.S. neutrality. No Condor Legion. No International Brigades. No Italian headcount. The foreign war is a phone call Franco was “able to” make, and a shudder.

Belarus has verbs for the rising — the army “raised a rebellion” — and a three-way help list: Italy and Germany to the nationalists, the USSR to the republicans. It never needs the word non-intervention. What it needs instead is a consequence sentence: democratic refusal to help, then the “Berlin—Rome Axis.” Spain is the place where the Axis name is earned. Guernica is absent. The Condor Legion is absent. The vocabulary is aid and alliance, not rehearsal and raid.

Russia prints the policy in scare quotes — «политику невмешательства» — and fills both foreign sides: international brigades of volunteers, Soviet support, a 125-thousand Italian expeditionary corps, German and Italian air strikes and arms. The Spanish interior is thick: Popular Front, Falange, a “second civil war” of communists against anarchists in the rear. The war “ended as a struggle between two Spanish authoritarian regimes.” What Russia will not spend space on, in the war narrative itself, is Guernica or the Condor Legion — Picasso’s Герника appears later as a caption, not as a named German operation.

“Mussolini sent to Spain a contingent of 50,000 ‘volunteers’ (but they were in reality regular units)”

IT · Il mondo contemporaneo, Laterza 2004 · §18.10

Italy is the classroom that teaches the student to distrust a word. “Volontari” arrives in quotation marks and is corrected in the same breath: regular units. The non-intervention agreement is subscribed by Italy and Germany and “respected only by France and Great Britain.” The USSR is “the only State to bring effective aid to the Republic,” and the International Brigades carry names the other columns do not need — Hemingway, Malraux, Orwell, and the slogan of the Italian antifascist emigration: “Oggi in Spagna, domani in Italia.” Guernica is still not a place-name in this section; the prelude is methodological — bombings of inhabited centers as techniques Europe will soon meet “on a much larger scale.” Italian agency for Italy’s own intervention is high; the German raid that other books treat as emblem stays offstage.

Lowe’s United Kingdom chapter is the first on this ladder to put both the broken neutrality grammar and the Condor name in one run of paragraphs. All the powers “had given an undertaking to remain neutral, but only Britain and France kept the agreement.” Mussolini’s 70 000 troops and Hitler’s air force that “practice[s] bombing undefended civilian targets” lead to “around a hundred bombers of the German Condor Legion” and Guernica “killing 1600 people.” The International Brigades are “organized and deployed by the Russians.” Foreign help for the Nationalists is “probably decisive.” That is survey candor with British non-intervention still in the frame — and a body count for one town that no other column in this set matches.

Germany’s bpb booklet is not a world-history survey; it is a National Socialism civic text, and Spain is a German verb. Hitler “decided” during the Olympic Games. The Condor Legion is dispatched “secretly.” The war is a “testing ground” (Versuchsfeld) and, in the page summary, a “military general rehearsal” (militärischen Generalprobe). Guernica is dated — 26 April 1937 — and owned: the Legion Condor bombarded it; “several hundred civilians were killed”; Göring’s Nuremberg testimony is printed as a primary source about transport fleets and testing “under live fire.” No other narrator in this corpus makes Spanish soil the subject of a German self-incrimination paragraph. Distance from the actor collapses; vocabulary thickens until the war’s foreign name is a German military program.

Whose volunteers are heroes is a smaller tell inside the same vocabulary fight. Russia and Italy put International Brigades in the main grammar as aid that matters; the UK credits Russian organization from Paris; the US and Belarus never need the word. Italy alone adds the antifascist emigration’s moral surplus over military weight — and the Italian book is also the one that must unmask its own state’s “volunteers.” Hero and fraud share a root.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

bpb (DE) earns the credit line for this event’s foreign vocabulary. Not because it is a full Spanish history — it is not — but because it does the job no peer column does: it puts German hands, German unit names, and German purpose words (Versuchsfeld, Generalprobe) on the same page as Guernica, and it lets Göring speak the testing grammar out loud. Self-distance is the German booklet’s job in this corpus; here that job and the finding coincide.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) deserves the second nod for the non-intervention anatomy and the scare-quoted “volontari.” Italy is a principal intervenor; the market manual still marks its own state’s euphemism and names the International Brigades as Comintern-organized antifascist force, not as a blur. That combination of self-marking and republican-side precision is rare on this ladder.

Lowe (UK) is the survey depth lead for Guernica’s Condor Legion sentence and the “only Britain and France kept the agreement” line. Чубарьян (RU) is the interior-politics lead — Popular Front, Falange, rear war of communists and anarchists, ending as two authoritarian regimes — for readers who need Spain as more than a German practice field. Depth is not the same as the vocabulary finding; the chapter needs all three angles.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is six books that print 1936–39 as an event. It is not “the world.” Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945 (Franco appears only as post-1975 democracy and Basque inheritance); China’s is a national history that never reaches this interwar European war. Those zeros are syllabus boundaries, not avoidance of Spain.

Sudan and India are different. Both are in scope for the 1930s in this corpus’s reading; both return zero hits on Spanish Civil War stems in English and original searches (dossier). Against a baseline of six covering books out of eight in-scope narrators, those zeros are avoidance signals, not other syllabuses. They sit in the receipts badge line, not as empty ladder rows pretending to be peers.

Machine-translated discovery files led us to the pages. Russian, Belarusian, German, and Italian lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English here for the reader. Before any of them becomes a hard claim in a later rewrite, the printed PDF page should be opened again. Belarus’s Axis-from-Spain page number (~149 vs dossier 150) and Italy’s Laterza page mapping deserve a second look.

Kept as data, not smoothed: death totals do not agree. Italy and the UK round near 500,000 war dead; the US says “as many as 500,000 casualties”; Russia stresses postwar prisons (“from 0.5 to 2 million political prisoners”) rather than a battlefield total; Belarus gives no casualty figure; Germany gives Guernica’s “several hundred” and no whole-war toll; the UK alone pairs Hugh Thomas’s mutual murder tallies (Nationalists 75 000 / Republicans 55 000) with Guernica’s 1600. Errors and ranges are data when the books print them.

Finally, none of these classrooms is a full Spanish social history. Five are European or world surveys with a Spain section; one is a German NS primer that needs Spain as rehearsal. If you want Catalonia, anarchist collectives, or the war as lived in Badajoz or Bilbao, every column will leave you unsatisfied — including the thick ones.
