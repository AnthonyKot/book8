# Chapter 13 (Pacific & Hiroshima) — Thread Pitches

Four candidates. Each seed is something the ladder actually surfaced — a quote or a
conspicuous silence — and each loop is general history, never attributed to a textbook.
Pick one; the rest get banked or dropped.

---

### Pitch 1: Hiroshima Stopped Being a City and Became a Unit

- **Seed** — the corpus's own arithmetic. Kazakhstan's volume, which is the only book here that reaches for the bombs as a measuring stick rather than an event: «Общая мощность их равна более чем 2,5 тысячам бомб, скинутых на Хиросиму» — "their total yield is equal to more than 2.5 thousand bombs dropped on Hiroshima" — and, on the next page, «За 40 лет здесь взорвались тысячи Хиросим», "over 40 years, thousands of Hiroshimas have exploded here" (История Казахстана, 11 кл., 2015, extract pp. 219–220).
- **Loop** — the device that destroyed Hiroshima released roughly fifteen kilotons; within seven years the first thermonuclear test was releasing several hundred times that, and there was no ordinary word for the difference. So the city became the ruler: every arsenal, every test site and every reactor accident since 1945 has been priced in Hiroshimas, by governments announcing yields and by campaigners opposing them, in Russian, Chinese, Italian, Ukrainian and English alike. The word travelled further as a unit than the event did as a lesson — Kazakhstan's schoolbook uses it to describe a steppe two thousand kilometres from Japan, in a paragraph that never explains the war.
- **Lander (linguistic, shared)** — "X times Hiroshima" is a phrase every one of these classrooms' newspapers still prints, and it is the only unit of measurement in common use named after a place where people died. A reader who has heard a bomb described that way has been quoting August 1945 without noticing.
- **Why it might fail** — the seed is KZ, which is not a ladder column; the thread has to stand on the shared usage rather than on the Kazakh page.

### Pitch 2: Nine Days That Are Still on the Map

- **Seed** — Russia's compound subject, «Атомная бомбардировка США Японии **и** вступление СССР в войну вынудили японские власти пойти на капитуляцию» (Чубарьян 2023, printed p. 201), read against the American book's version of the same week: "keeping the promise made at Yalta, after the bombing of Hiroshima the Soviets broke their nonaggression pact with Japan and invaded Manchukuo and Korea" (OpenStax 2022, p. 560). Two classrooms, one calendar, and the argument is entirely about the order of the entries.
- **Loop** — 6 August, Hiroshima; 8 August, the Soviet declaration; 9 August, Manchuria and Nagasaki; 15 August, the broadcast. In the days after the surrender was announced, Soviet forces were still landing — on South Sakhalin, on the Kuril chain, and down the Korean peninsula to a line two American staff officers had drawn across a small-scale map in a Washington office overnight. The end of the Pacific war was not a stopping point but a race, and the finish lines it left are borders.
- **Lander (map, shared)** — Russia and Japan have never signed a peace treaty ending the Second World War, because of four islands taken in those nine days; the 38th parallel is in the news of every country in this corpus. A reader looking at either line today is looking at where the clock ran out in August 1945.
- **Why it might fail** — it hands ch. 15 its own opening, and the map lander is more geopolitical than domestic; a reader has to care about a border to feel it.

### Pitch 3: The Dead of 1945 Are the Reason Your X-Ray Has a Limit

- **Seed** — the second number every counting narrator prints. Russia: «десятки тысяч человек умерли позднее от лучевой болезни и ран». India: "Many more were to die later through the effects of radiation." Italy: «gli effetti di lungo periodo su quanti erano stati contaminati dalle radiazioni». Britain: "thousands more slowly dying of radiation poisoning." Four classrooms that agree on almost nothing agree that the dying continued, and none of them says what was done with that fact.
- **Loop** — from 1947 a joint American–Japanese commission began following the survivors, and its successor, the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, is still doing it in Hiroshima and Nagasaki today; its Life Span Study has tracked roughly 120,000 people for more than seventy years. Because those people had a measurable dose and a measurable lifetime, that cohort became the main empirical basis for estimating cancer risk from radiation anywhere. The millisievert limits that govern reactor workers, airline crews and the dose on a hospital consent form are calibrated, at bottom, against the survivors of those two mornings.
- **Lander (material, shared)** — the lead apron at the dentist, and the exposure figure printed on the form before a CT scan, in every country in this corpus. The safety rule in the reader's own hospital is written in the survivors' data.
- **Why it might work** — it is the only candidate where the reader touches the history in a room they have personally sat in, and it is wonder rather than accusation: the most destructive event of the war produced the standard that limits everyone's exposure since.

### Pitch 4: Nobody's Textbook Says Where the Uranium Came From

- **Seed** — a silence, shared by all eight columns. Every book that narrates the bombing names the actor, the aircraft, the president or the date; not one sentence in the corpus says where the material came from. OpenStax names the Manhattan Project, Trinity, *Enola Gay*, Little Boy and Fat Man, and still leaves the supply chain off the page.
- **Loop** — the highest-grade uranium ore then known came from Shinkolobwe in Katanga, in the Belgian Congo, where the seams ran percent-rich rather than trace-rich; its Belgian operator had already shipped over a thousand tonnes of it to a warehouse on Staten Island before the American programme existed, and sold it on in 1942. The wars of ch. 2 put Katanga under Brussels; ch. 2's mining concessions put the ore in a New York warehouse; that warehouse fed the reactors and the separation plants that made August 1945 possible. The chapter about the end of the war is wired to the chapter about the scramble for Africa, and neither classroom draws the wire.
- **Lander (material, shared)** — the same Katanga copper belt supplies most of the world's cobalt, which is in the battery of the phone the reader is holding, in every country in this corpus; Shinkolobwe itself was officially sealed in 2004 and is still worked informally.
- **Why it might fail** — the seed is an absence rather than a phrase, which is harder to dramatise; and the causal claim needs careful sourcing before it can be stated as flatly as the loop above states it.
