# Chapter 10 (Spain, 1936–1939) — Thread Pitches

Four candidates. The thread beat in `drafts/10-spain-civil-war.opus.html` is left as
`<!-- THREAD BEAT GOES HERE AFTER PITCH ROUND -->`. Pick one; the rest get dropped or
banked. Every seed below is a passage verified by grep against `corpus-orig/<id>.txt`
and cross-checked against `corpus-en/<id>.txt`.

The chapter's finding, for reference: **all six covering books agree that German and
Italian aircraft flew in Spain; what varies is how many proper nouns the act is given —
and the ordering tracks neither guilt nor distance. Germany names the unit, the town, the
date and the man who signed the order; Italy, the other state whose air force was there,
never prints the town at all.** A winning thread should grow out of that, not sit beside it.

### Pitch 1: The first airlift *(my candidate — recommended, non-obvious)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Four of the six books start this war in Africa and none of
  them says who was standing there. Russia: «Мятеж начался 17 июля 1936 г. в Марокко» —
  "The rebellion began on 17 July 1936 in Morocco" (RU, p. 102). Italy is more specific
  and stops one word short: «la ribellione ebbe il suo punto di forza nelle truppe
  coloniali di stanza nel Marocco spagnolo» — "the rebellion had its strong point in the
  colonial troops stationed in Spanish Morocco" (IT, p. 413). Lowe: "they began a revolt
  in Morocco, where General Franco soon assumed the leadership" (UK, p. 345). And the
  German booklet prints the perpetrator explaining the logistics problem: «Man darf nicht
  vergessen, Franco stand mit seinen eigentlichen Truppen in Afrika. […] Das Entscheidende
  war, dass zunächst seine Truppen nach Spanien kamen.» — "One must not forget, Franco was
  standing with his actual troops in Africa. […] The decisive thing was that his troops got
  to Spain first" — Göring at Nuremberg (DE, p. 8). Two books independently name the
  solution. Göring: «Ich sandte mit Genehmigung des Führers einen großen Teil meiner
  Transportflotte» — "With the Führer's permission I sent down a large part of my transport
  fleet." Lowe, in his own list of why the Nationalists won:
  "Mussolini provided the transport aircraft to bring Franco's army across to Spain from
  Morocco, after Franco had decided it was too risky to bring them by sea" (UK, p. 346).
- **Loop.** July 1936: the rising succeeds in Spanish Morocco and fails in Madrid and
  Barcelona, so the best troops in the Spanish army are on the wrong continent and the
  navy has stayed loyal → Franco asks Berlin and Rome for aircraft, and about twenty
  Junkers Ju 52 transports plus Italian Savoia-Marchettis start shuttling the Army of
  Africa over the Strait of Gibraltar, ten to twelve minutes a crossing, day after day
  → roughly 13,000–20,000 men are flown into Europe by October, the first time in history
  an army is moved by air, and the war that everyone else's textbook treats as a bombing
  rehearsal is decided in its first ten weeks by a freight service → the lesson taken is
  not about bombs: the Ju 52 becomes the aircraft that drops paratroops on Rotterdam and
  Crete → and in June 1948 the same technique, run in reverse by the other side, keeps
  West Berlin fed for eleven months.
- **Precise punchline (causal care).** The airlift did not win the war — Franco needed
  three more years, and Italian divisions, and German fuel. What it did was prevent the
  rising from failing in August 1936, which is a different and smaller claim, and the only
  one the evidence supports.
- **Lander.** The word **airlift**, which arrives on the news in every corpus country
  whenever there is an earthquake, a siege or an evacuation. It was invented in July 1936
  to move colonial troops out of Morocco into Europe, in German and Italian aeroplanes, on
  behalf of a Spanish general. Second, quieter lander: the men in those aircraft were
  Moroccan Regulares and the Spanish Legion — the Scramble's soldiers (ch. 2), flown north
  to decide a European civil war.
- **Who tells it.** 4 of 6 covering books put the war's opening in Morocco; 2 of 6 name
  the transport aircraft; 0 of 11 say who the soldiers were, and 0 of 11 note that this was
  the first military airlift in history.
- **Receipts-lite.** Göring's testimony, IMT Nuremberg, 14 March 1946, reprinted in
  Hans-Christian Kirsch (ed.), *Der spanische Bürgerkrieg in Augenzeugenberichten* (Karl
  Rauch 1971), pp. 101 f., and printed on p. 8 of the bpb booklet in this corpus; the Ju 52
  in the Deutsches Technikmuseum, Berlin. <!-- CHECK: Operation Feuerzauber, from 29 July 1936; ~20 Ju 52s plus Italian SM.81s; ~13,000–20,000 troops of the Army of Africa flown across the Strait by October 1936; commonly described as the first large-scale military airlift; Göring testified at the IMT on 14 March 1946 -->
- **Why it fits.** It answers the chapter's own question from the other end: the ladder is
  about which classroom names the bombing, and the thread names the thing none of them
  bothers with — the aircraft that mattered were the ones with no bombs on them. It is
  material, it braids back to ch. 2 and forward to ch. 14, and the loop is checkable in an
  afternoon.

### Pitch 2: What the aid cost *(my candidate — the ladder's shared silence)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Every column uses the same verb and not one of them prices
  it. Belarus: "Italy and Germany helped the nationalists… while the USSR supported the
  republicans" (BY, p. 149). Russia: "The government of the Popular Front was also
  supported by the USSR" (RU, p. 102). Italy: «L'unico Stato a portare un aiuto efficace
  alla Repubblica fu l'Urss» — "The only State to bring effective aid to the Republic was
  the USSR" (IT, p. 414). Lowe: "The Republicans received some help from Russia in the
  form of troops, tanks and planes" (UK, p. 345). *Help*, *aid*, *support* — six books,
  three languages, no invoice.
- **Loop.** The Spanish Republic holds the fourth-largest gold reserve in the world and,
  under the non-intervention embargo, cannot buy weapons with it in Paris or London →
  in October 1936 most of the reserve, roughly 510 tonnes, is trucked to Cartagena and
  shipped to Odessa; Moscow prices the arms, the freight, the advisers and the exchange
  rate → by 1938 the account is spent and Soviet deliveries slow → meanwhile Franco is
  buying on credit from Berlin and Rome against Spanish iron and pyrites, and Spain is
  still paying that debt off into the 1960s → the Republic's "aid" and the Nationalists'
  "aid" were both sales, and the two sides financed their war with the same country's
  minerals and savings.
- **Precise punchline (causal care).** The gold did not lose the Republic the war. It
  bought real aircraft that flew real missions. What it did was make the Republic a
  customer of a single supplier, at prices only the supplier could see.
- **Lander.** Central-bank gold. Where a country's reserves are physically stored is a live
  argument in most of the corpus countries right now, and the phrase "the Moscow gold" is
  still a working insult in Spanish politics. Anyone who has read a headline about a
  central bank repatriating bullion has read a footnote to 1936.
- **Who tells it.** 6 of 6 covering books say one side or both received aid; 0 of 6 say
  what it cost, who paid, or in what; 0 of 11 mention the Bank of Spain's reserve.
- **Receipts-lite.** The Bank of Spain's own published accounts of the 1936 transfer; the
  Negrín decree of 13 September 1936; Ángel Viñas, *El oro de Moscú*. <!-- CHECK: ~510 tonnes (about 72–73% of the Bank of Spain reserve) shipped from Cartagena to Odessa, late October 1936, on the authority of a decree of 13 September 1936; Spain's debt to Germany from the civil war was settled in instalments after 1945 -->
- **Risk.** The Republic-side half is far better documented than the Franco-side half, so
  the loop has to be written carefully to avoid landing as a one-sided charge.

### Pitch 3: The word for a bombed town *(the obvious seed, developed)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** The chapter already found that the two books that name
  Guernica best both reach for it again, a whole war later, in the same argument. Lowe
  quotes a peace campaigner: the Allied raids on Germany "probably were war crimes, but…
  the Nazis themselves were the first to begin bombing innocent civilians in Guernica
  (during the Spanish Civil War), Warsaw and Rotterdam" (UK, § 6.5(b)). The German booklet,
  on the bombing of German cities from 1942, writes «ebenso wie deutsche Luftangriffe zuvor
  Städte wie Guernica, Coventry, Warschau, Belgrad, Leningrad, Minsk und Rotterdam zerstört
  hatten» — "just as German air raids had earlier destroyed cities like Guernica, Coventry,
  Warsaw, Belgrade, Leningrad, Minsk and Rotterdam" (DE, p. 4). Both put Guernica first.
- **Loop.** A market town with no anti-aircraft guns is bombed for three hours by an air
  force testing equipment → within four days the name is in the world's newspapers, and
  within six weeks it is the title of the most reproduced painting of the century → the
  name becomes the unit of measurement for what an air force does to a town, applied in
  turn to Coventry, Rotterdam, Minsk, Hamburg, Dresden, Tokyo → after 1945 the argument
  moves into law: the deliberate targeting of civilian populations is written into the
  1949 Geneva Conventions and, in detail, into Additional Protocol I of 1977 → a woven copy
  of the painting hangs at the entrance to the UN Security Council chamber, which is where
  the argument is still had.
- **Lander.** The painting. Most readers in every corpus country know the town only because
  of a picture — and Russia's textbook is proof, since the only Guernica in the whole
  volume is a caption on a colour plate giving the museum and the room. The object on the
  reader's side is a poster, a postcard, a phone wallpaper of a painting whose subject
  their textbook may never have mentioned.
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 6 covering books name the town as an event; 2 of 6 use it as a
  precedent in the argument about bombing German cities; 1 of 6 prints the painting without
  the raid; 0 of 11 connect it to the law of armed conflict that followed.
- **Receipts-lite.** George Steer's dispatch, *The Times* and the *New York Times*,
  28 April 1937; Picasso's *Guernica*, Museo Reina Sofía, Madrid; the tapestry copy at UN
  headquarters, New York. <!-- CHECK: George Steer's report published 28 April 1937; Picasso began the canvas 1 May 1937, shown at the Paris International Exposition July 1937; painting moved to Spain 1981 and to the Reina Sofía 1992; Nelson Rockefeller commissioned the tapestry, on loan to the UN from 1985 -->
- **Note.** The strongest reader-recognition of the four and the weakest as a *discovery* —
  it mostly deepens what the ladder already says. Take it if the others get no reaction.

### Pitch 4: The fifth column *(my candidate — a total silence)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Russia is the only book that narrates the war *inside* the
  republican city: «Тыл республиканской Испании был охвачен своего рода второй гражданской
  войной» — "the rear of republican Spain was engulfed by a kind of second civil war"
  (RU, p. 103). That is the exact subject of a phrase this war coined — and the phrase is
  not in any of the eleven books, in any language. Searched: *fifth column*, «пятая
  колонна», «п'ята колона», *quinta colonna*, *fünfte Kolonne*, 第五纵队, الطابور الخامس.
  Eleven zeros.
- **Loop.** Autumn 1936, four Nationalist columns converge on Madrid and a broadcast credits
  a fifth one already inside the city → the phrase is picked up by foreign correspondents
  covering the siege, and by Hemingway, who titles his only play with it in 1938 → it
  crosses into every language in the corpus, where it is used for the neighbour, the
  minority, the party, the newspaper suspected of belonging to the other side → and because
  it is an accusation that requires no evidence, it is used most heavily wherever a
  government is losing → readers in all eleven countries have heard it in the last year,
  and none of their textbooks says it was born in a besieged Spanish city in 1936.
- **Precise punchline (causal care).** The phrase did not create the suspicion — cities
  under siege have always suspected their own. What Madrid produced was the *word*, and a
  word is portable in a way that a suspicion is not.
- **Lander.** The phrase itself, live in the politics of every corpus country, in Russian,
  Ukrainian, Italian, German, English, Chinese and Arabic. A reader has probably used it,
  or been called it.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 6 describes the war behind republican lines at all (RU); 0 of 11
  print the phrase in any language; 0 of 11 attribute it to this war.
- **Receipts-lite.** Ernest Hemingway, *The Fifth Column* (1938); the phrase in *The Times*
  and the Spanish press from October 1936. <!-- CHECK: the coinage is traditionally attributed to General Emilio Mola, autumn 1936, but the attribution is contested and other origins (including a Madrid communist source) have been proposed; the phrase is documented in the international press from October 1936 — do not state Mola as fact without a source -->
- **Risk.** The origin story is the contested part, and this book cannot print a contested
  attribution as a fact. Written honestly ("the attribution is argued about; the date and
  the city are not"), the uncertainty is itself interesting. Written lazily, it is a myth.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (the airlift)** first. It grows straight out of a silence the ladder made visible
— four books open the war in Africa and none of them looks at who is standing there — it is
carried by two independent seeds in books at opposite ends of the ladder, and it replaces
the chapter's famous image (bombers over a town) with the aircraft that actually decided
the thing. The lander is a word every reader hears on the news, and the causal claim can be
kept honest and small.

**Pitch 2 (what the aid cost)** is the strongest as comparison: one verb, six books, and a
question none of them asks. Take it if the reaction to the airlift is "so what."

**Pitch 4** is the most portable and the riskiest; it needs the contested attribution
handled in the open. **Pitch 3** is the safest and the least surprising — bank it for
ch. 13 (Hiroshima), where the argument about bombing cities has to be had anyway.
