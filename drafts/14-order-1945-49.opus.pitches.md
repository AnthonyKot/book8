### Pitch 1: The money that never left America *(recommended — grows straight out of the chapter's convergence)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** The one thing four books with four different approval mechanisms agree on. BY (state monopoly): aid «оговаривалось политическими требованиями… В итоге США получали мощные рычаги воздействия на Европу» — “was conditioned by political demands… In the end the USA obtained powerful levers of influence over Europe” (p. 167). UA (ministry list): the plan was meant to let the United States «позбавитися надлишку капіталів і створити в Європі платоспроможні ринки для збуту своєї продукції» — shed a surplus of capital and create solvent markets in Europe for its own goods (p. 16). UK (market): “A prosperous Europe would provide lucrative markets for American exports.” US (market): the aid “helped ensure that the U.S. economy did not slide into an economic depression now that the war had ended” (p. 576). Four classrooms, identical description, four different verdicts.
- **Loop.** Europe in 1947 has factories and no dollars — the “dollar gap,” and without dollars it cannot buy the American coal, wheat and machine tools it needs → Marshall aid is issued as dollars that must be spent in the United States, so American ships carry American goods and the money completes a circle back into American payrolls → recipients pay for the goods in their own currency into counterpart funds, which their governments may spend only with American agreement, which is the “lever” the Belarusian book names → and underneath it sits Bretton Woods, which had already pegged every Western currency to a dollar convertible into gold, so the aid is not only cash, it is enrolment in a monetary system → in 1971 Washington ends the gold convertibility and the system's floor drops out, but the dollar's job as the world's settlement money survives the system that created it, and is still doing that job.
- **Lander.** Look at any currency-exchange board, in any of the eleven countries in this corpus, and the first line is the dollar. Oil, freight, wheat and central-bank reserves are still priced in the money that was shipped to Europe in 1948, and the reason is a fortnight of meetings in a New Hampshire hotel in 1944.
- **Who tells it.** 4 of 11 classrooms name Bretton Woods (IT, UA, US, and RU as a bare timeline entry, «1944 — Бреттон-Вудские соглашения»); 2 of 11 explain what the dollar's role actually was — IT: «L'insieme di queste riforme, e più ancora il ruolo internazionale del dollaro, misero nelle mani degli Usa leve di azione formidabili sulle economie occidentali»; UA: the replacement of the gold standard by a «система регульованого золотодоларового стандарту», a regulated gold-dollar standard, and the American exit from it in 1971 (p. 20). 0 of 11 join Bretton Woods to the Marshall Plan — including UA, which prints them four pages apart.
- **Receipts-lite.** Marshall's Harvard address, 5 June 1947 (full text on the George C. Marshall Foundation site); Economic Cooperation Act, April 1948; Bretton Woods Agreements, July 1944, and the IMF and World Bank, both still in Washington and still open to visitors; Nixon's announcement of 15 August 1971. <!-- CHECK: Marshall aid was predominantly grants, largely tied to purchases of US goods; counterpart-fund mechanism under the ECA; Bretton Woods pegged the dollar at $35/oz gold; convertibility suspended 15 August 1971 -->
- **Why it fits.** It is the chapter's own convergence turned into a mechanism, so it cannot be accused of importing a thesis; the ladder earned it. It also stays off the blame question the chapter deliberately leaves open — it explains a machine, not a villain.

### Pitch 2: A bridge, or a lift *(the linguistic candidate)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** The same eleven months, four languages, two metaphors. UA: «У відповідь держави Заходу організували з ним повітряний міст» — the Western states organised an air *bridge* with it (p. 17). IT: «Gli americani organizzarono un gigantesco ponte aereo per rifornire la città» — a gigantic air *bridge*. German, outside this corpus, has *Luftbrücke*. English has **airlift**, and both English-language books use it: OpenStax indexes “Berlin Airlift 577,” Lowe heads the section “The Berlin blockade and airlift.” A bridge presumes two banks and something in between that must be spanned. A lift presumes one place, going up.
- **Loop.** The blockade is not a battle, it is an arithmetic problem: something on the order of four and a half thousand tons a day must reach two million people, and the majority of it is not food but coal, because a city that cannot heat or generate cannot be held → the West answers with a conveyor belt in the sky, aircraft stacked on fixed altitudes and fixed intervals, unloaded without shutting down engines → on one twenty-four-hour showpiece in April 1949 the operation lands nearly thirteen thousand tons in a single day, which is more than the railways used to bring → Stalin lifts the blockade in May 1949, and both German states are declared within months, so the line the aircraft flew over becomes a border, and the border becomes a wall in 1961. The siege that was meant to remove the West from Berlin is the reason the West stays for forty-one years.
- **Lander.** Tempelhof's runways are a public park. The airfield closed in 2008 and Berliners now cycle, skate and fly kites down the strips the aircraft used, past the hangars; entry is free, and it is one of the largest open spaces in any European capital. The other lander is the word itself: if your language calls it a bridge, you were taught that there were two sides.
- **Who tells it.** 4 of 11 classrooms narrate the blockade (UA, IT, UK, US); 2 of them call it a bridge (UA, IT) and 2 call it a lift (UK, US); the German booklet in this corpus has no blockade at all, so *Luftbrücke* is a fact about the language, not about that book; 0 of 11 give the daily tonnage the city actually needed, which is the number that makes the operation legible. BY, which has the Berlin Wall of 1961 and its fall in 1989, has no 1948 blockade at all.
- **Receipts-lite.** Tempelhof Feld, Berlin, open daily; the Luftbrückendenkmal outside the old terminal with the names of the seventy-odd airmen killed; the Allied Museum in Dahlem. <!-- CHECK: ~4,500 tons/day minimum requirement, majority coal; “Easter Parade,” 15–16 April 1949, 12,941 tons in 1,398 flights; Tempelhof closed October 2008, opened as Tempelhofer Feld May 2010; airlift fatalities ~78–101 depending on the count -->

### Pitch 3: The banknote that drew the border *(my non-obvious candidate)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Four books make a currency the trigger and none of them stops to say what a currency reform is. US: “In June, the United Kingdom and the United States introduced a new currency, the Deutschemark, into their zones as well as into the western portions of Berlin they controlled, in order to dispense Marshall Plan funds. Infuriated, the Soviet Union cut off all ground routes into West Berlin” (p. 577). UA: «У червні 1948 р. в західних та радянській зонах почали впроваджувати окремі грошові реформи» — separate monetary reforms began in the western and the Soviet zones (p. 17). UK: “In June 1948 the West introduced a new currency and ended price controls in their zone and in West Berlin.” IT: the western zones were merged, «attuandovi una riforma monetaria». Four narrators, one trigger, and the trigger is a printing press.
- **Loop.** Occupied Germany runs on a Reichsmark nobody believes in, so the real money is the American cigarette — wages, rents and black-market prices are quoted in packs → the new Deutschmark is printed in the United States and shipped into Germany in crates under an operation codename, because a currency has to arrive all at once or not at all → issuing it on 20 June 1948 in the western zones, and three days later in the western sectors of Berlin, creates two price systems in one city and threatens to flood the Soviet zone with repudiated old notes → on 24 June the roads close. The first frontier of the Cold War is drawn by a note-issuing decision, not by an army, and it hardens in stages: two currencies in 1948, two states in 1949, one wall in 1961.
- **Lander.** Money is the flag people actually carry, and the corpus is full of people who have watched one be replaced overnight — the D-Mark itself was retired for euro notes in 2002, and readers in Kyiv, Minsk, Moscow and Almaty have their own dates in the 1990s for the morning the money in the drawer stopped being money. The 1948 reform is the template: new notes, an exchange ratio that wipes out savings, and a border where the ratio changes.
- **Who tells it.** 4 of 11 classrooms name the currency reform as the immediate cause of the blockade (US, UA, UK, IT); 1 of 11 names the new currency (US, “the Deutschemark”); 0 of 11 say what happened to anybody's savings, and 0 of 11 mention the cigarette economy the reform ended.
- **Receipts-lite.** Operation Bird Dog, the printing of the new notes in the United States, 1947–48; the currency reform of 20 June 1948 and the Soviet reform of 23 June; the Deutsche Bundesbank's money museum in Frankfurt displays the 1948 notes; the euro replaced D-Mark cash on 1 January 2002. <!-- CHECK: “Operation Bird Dog” — new Deutschmark notes printed in the US and shipped to Germany; western currency reform 20 June 1948, extended to the western sectors of Berlin 23 June; Soviet zone reform 23 June; blockade from 24 June; D-Mark cash withdrawn 2002 -->
- **Risk.** The mechanism is genuinely a bit technical, and the chapter must not turn into a monetary-policy lesson. It works only if the cigarette does the explaining.

### Pitch 4: The Cold War's first payment was an amnesty *(seeded from the out-of-scope column)*

- **Seed (from a conspicuous asymmetry).** Five classrooms print the promise. RU: the allies agreed at Potsdam on political and economic principles «(демилитаризация, демократизация, денацификация, декартелизация)» (p. 200). BY: Potsdam adopted «демилитаризация, денацификация и демократизация Германии» (p. 162). US: “a program of denazification for Germany and Austria was confirmed that included punishment of war criminals” (p. 558). DE: at Potsdam the three «bestimmten … dass Deutschland entnazifiziert, demokratisiert und entmilitarisiert werden sollte» (extract p. 68). Exactly one book says what became of it, and it is the German one: «erschien die Praxis der Entnazifizierung im sich verschärfenden Kalten Krieg zwischen den Westalliierten und der Sowjetunion nicht mehr so wichtig, da die Deutschen nicht als Gegner behandelt, sondern jeweils Bündnispartner werden sollten» — in the intensifying Cold War, denazification no longer seemed so important, since the Germans were not to be treated as opponents but were to become alliance partners. And the verdict, in the book's own quotation marks: «„Die Kleinen hängt man, die Großen lässt man laufen“» — the small ones are hanged, the big ones are let go (extract p. 70).
- **Loop.** Potsdam commits every occupier to strip Germany of its Nazi personnel → the freeze converts Germany from the defendant into the prize, and both sides need Germans who can run an army, an intelligence service and a rocket programme → the Western zones' denazification tribunals sink under their own caseload and start issuing exoneration certificates that Germans nickname after a brand of laundry powder; the Soviet zone runs its own version and reaches the same practical destination by a different route → the men who built the V-2 with concentration-camp labour at Mittelbau-Dora are recruited by both victors, and one of those teams designs the rocket that lands people on the Moon twenty-four years after Potsdam.
- **Lander.** The Saturn V is in every classroom's picture of the twentieth century, and the tunnels where its ancestor was assembled by prisoners are a memorial in Thuringia that anyone can walk into. The linguistic half is smaller and sharper: *Persilschein*, still ordinary German for a clean bill of health, is the name of a washing powder still on supermarket shelves.
- **Who tells it.** 5 of 11 classrooms print the denazification promise (RU p. 200, BY p. 162, US p. 558, DE extract p. 68, IT at Yalta); 1 of 11 says it was abandoned and names the Cold War as the reason (DE); 0 of 11 name Paperclip, Gehlen or von Braun — the only hits for those strings anywhere in the corpus are false friends.
- **Receipts-lite.** Potsdam Protocol, 2 August 1945, section III; the Spruchkammer records in German state archives; Mittelbau-Dora Concentration Camp Memorial, Nordhausen, open to visitors; the Gehlen Organization became the Bundesnachrichtendienst in 1956. <!-- CHECK: US Operation Paperclip; von Braun's team at Fort Bliss then Huntsville; Soviet transfer of Gröttrup's group to Gorodomlya, 1946; BND founded 1956 from the Gehlen Organization; ~20,000 deaths at Mittelbau-Dora -->
- **Caveat for the human.** DE is an out-of-scope column in this chapter and sits in the receipts, not on the ladder. Using its sentence as the thread's seed is defensible — the thread is general history and the seed is a phrase a book in the corpus printed — but it does put an out-of-scope book on stage, and it also overlaps ch. 12's territory. ## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (the money)** first. It is the only candidate whose seed is the chapter's *convergence*
rather than its disagreement, which makes it the strongest evidence in the book so far for the
thesis that these narrators agree when they are describing a machine and split when they are
assigning a motive. The lander is on every exchange board in every corpus country.

**Pitch 3 (the banknote)** second, and the most surprising per sentence — four books name a
currency reform as the trigger and not one explains it — but it needs the cigarette to stay
readable, and it is the one most likely to drift into economics.

**Pitch 2 (bridge or lift)** is the cheapest to write and the safest, and its linguistic half
would work as a two-sentence echo inside whichever thread wins; it is already half-said in the
tell and could simply be promoted. **Pitch 4** is the most morally loaded and the most likely to
slide into the banned register; it is also partly ch. 12's. Bank it unless the Holocaust chapter
left the denazification question conspicuously open.
