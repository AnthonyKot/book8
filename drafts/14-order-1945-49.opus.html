The 1945–1949 order

On 24 June 1948 the road, rail and canal links into the western sectors of Berlin close, and the power station in the Soviet sector stops feeding the western grid. Rather more than two million people are inside the line. Two days later the first transport aircraft land at Tempelhof, and they keep landing for eleven months. Four of the eleven classrooms in this corpus describe that winter; six build the years around it. None of the six disagrees about what happened. They disagree about which sentence, in the three years before, belongs at the beginning.

The question · causes

Who started the Cold War — and which event does your textbook put first?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 4–11 February 1945: the Yalta (Crimea) conference. Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin agree occupation zones for Germany, a Declaration on Liberated Europe promising free elections, Soviet entry into the war against Japan, and a founding conference for a new world organisation.
  • 25 April – 26 June 1945: San Francisco. The UN Charter enters into force on 24 October 1945 with fifty-one original members.
  • 17 July – 2 August 1945: Potsdam. Truman has replaced Roosevelt; Attlee replaces Churchill during the conference. Germany is to be demilitarised, denazified, democratised and decartelised, and is divided into four occupation zones. Berlin, inside the Soviet zone, is divided the same way.
  • 5 March 1946: Churchill, out of office, speaks at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, and uses the phrase “iron curtain.”
  • 12 March 1947: Truman asks Congress for $400 million for Greece and Turkey. 5 June 1947: Marshall’s Harvard speech. The Economic Cooperation Act passes in April 1948; roughly $13 billion goes to western Europe by 1952.
  • February 1948: the communist takeover in Czechoslovakia. June 1948 – May 1949: the Berlin blockade and the airlift.
  • 4 April 1949: twelve states sign the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington. May 1949: the Federal Republic of Germany. October 1949: the German Democratic Republic. Also 1949: the first Soviet atomic test, and the proclamation of the People’s Republic of China.
  • Historians have never settled the responsibility question. The orthodox account blames Stalin, the revisionist account blames Washington’s use of its economic and atomic advantage, and the post-revisionist account distributes it. All three are still in print.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who actually build the postwar order, ordered from the thinnest account of how it froze (nobody starts anything) to the thickest (a named actor, a deliberate adverb, and a blame question put to the student). China’s volume is the national-history half of its course, India’s world-history book stops short of the topic, Germany’s is a National Socialism booklet and Sudan’s syllabus is Arab and national; they are in the receipts, not on the ladder. Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event column here.

Systematically — and then the question, reopened

3 The tell

Nothing on this ladder is a disagreement about what happened. All six books have Yalta and Potsdam, five have Fulton, five have the Marshall Plan, five have NATO in 1949, four have the Berlin blockade. Lay the six accounts side by side and the events line up like a railway timetable. What does not line up is the first entry.

The Russian volume does not have one, because in its grammar nothing is started. Its section heading is «Распад антигитлеровской коалиции» — the disintegration of the anti-Hitler coalition, a noun for something that comes apart rather than something anyone takes apart. Then the contradictions between the two states «предопределялись» — were predetermined — and the thing that predetermines them is a pair of ideas. A passive verb, and behind it an agent that cannot hold a pen. The words «холодная война» do not occur anywhere in the book’s 241 pages. That is not the calendar running out: the same chapter carries the reader to the two German states of 1949, to the San Francisco treaty with Japan in 1951, and to the Austrian settlement of 1955. There is room for 1955. There is no room for the phrase.

Belarus supplies the corpus’s only starting gun, and declines to sign the sentence that fires it. Churchill’s Fulton speech «положило начало „холодной войне“» — laid the beginning of the Cold War — with the hedge «как считается», as is considered, sitting between the speech and the consequence. Read the preceding sentence and the grammar is doing something finer still. The Soviet Union does take six countries under its control; it does so in a participle, attached to a noun, inside a sentence whose main verb belongs to the United States, and whose subject is American fear. The seizure is true, present, and grammatically subordinate to somebody else’s emotion.

Ukraine, the ministry-approved book closest to the events, is the only column that gives each side its own stated motive in that side’s own terms — Stalin’s remark that whoever occupies territory can impose his social order on it, and, three pages later, an American plan built to shed surplus capital and to manufacture solvent markets. Then the chapter’s conclusions flatten both into a single line: the confrontation «було історично обумовленим», was historically conditioned. Two agendas, fully named, filed under a verdict that removes the choosing.

Italy dates the change earlier than anyone: April 1945, the month Truman takes office, «un brusco cambiamento del clima». It is the only book in the corpus that hangs the freeze on a change of person rather than a change of policy — and it does so while writing, in the same paragraph, that the «disegno staliniano di assoggettamento» was already taking shape. Both first moves, on one page, neither cancelling the other. That is what a manual written for undergraduates can afford and a schoolbook mostly cannot.

Lowe’s adverb is the most agentive word on the ladder. Not “the Russians set up governments” but “the Russians systematically interfered” — an adverb that converts a set of events into a plan. And then, a few pages earlier in the same book, the section that asks our question by name and refuses to answer it: Which side was to blame? Three schools, in order — Kennan and the orthodox account blaming Stalin, the revisionists blaming an American drive for hegemony, the post-revisionists dividing it — with the revisionist case given its own paragraph and its leading proponent named. A British market textbook prints the Soviet historians’ argument at length, on the way to a chapter that will not adopt it.

«Організація Північноатлантичного договору (НАТО) — міжнародна міжурядова організація, військово-політичний союз 29 держав Північної Америки та Європи, які прагнуть досягти мети Північноатлантичного договору 1949 р. У 2018 р. за Україною визнано статус країни-аспіранта на шляху приєднання до союзу.»

“The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) — an international intergovernmental organisation, a military-political union of 29 states of North America and Europe that seek to achieve the aims of the North Atlantic Treaty of 1949. In 2018 Ukraine was recognised as an aspirant country on the path to joining the alliance.”

UA · Всесвітня історія, 11 кл. (2019) · the definition box printed beside the account of 4 April 1949, p. 18

That box is the clearest single answer to the question of whose grammar is doing the work. It is a definition of a 1949 alliance, and it ends in 2018, in the present tense, with the reader’s own country on the waiting list. The same paragraph tells the student that the USSR regarded NATO as an «агресивного блоку» — an “aggressive bloc,” the words printed inside quotation marks as somebody else’s description. Belarus, three hundred kilometres away, has NATO in a diagram opposite the Warsaw Pact and asks the class whether those organisations still exist and whether it matters today. Same alliance, same year, two live questions pointing in opposite directions.

Then the convergence, which is the more surprising half of this chapter. Four books, four different approval mechanisms — a state monopoly, a ministry list, and two market publishers — describe the Marshall Plan’s purpose in nearly the same words. Belarus: aid «оговаривалось политическими требованиями», conditioned by political demands, so that «США получали мощные рычаги воздействия на Европу» — the USA obtained powerful levers of influence over Europe. Ukraine: the plan was meant to help the United States «позбавитися надлишку капіталів і створити в Європі платоспроможні ринки для збуту своєї продукції» — shed a surplus of capital and create solvent markets in Europe for its own goods. Lowe: “A prosperous Europe would provide lucrative markets for American exports; but its main aim was probably political.” OpenStax: because the goods had to be bought in America, the aid “helped ensure that the U.S. economy did not slide into an economic depression now that the war had ended.” Four classrooms agree completely about what the money was for. What they disagree about is whether that is an indictment.

One more thing the books do to the same eleven months. In English it is an airlift: the cargo goes up. In Ukrainian it is a «повітряний міст», in Italian a «ponte aereo», in German a Luftbrücke — an air bridge: the cargo goes across, and a bridge implies two banks and something in between them that has to be spanned. The operation is identical in every account. The metaphor is not, and the metaphor is the argument in miniature — which is also true of the borrowed piece of stage machinery that five of these books send the student to Missouri to hear.

Finally, the space where the order is not built. China’s volume fills 1945–1949 completely — with the civil war, lesson 25, and the founding of the People’s Republic — and the words for Yalta, Potsdam, Marshall, Truman, iron curtain, NATO and Cold War appear in it zero times. India’s world-history book does have Yalta and Potsdam in it. They appear once, as the names of conferences that Korean independence campaigners sent delegations to petition: not a world order, a set of doors that somebody knocked on. And Sudan’s syllabus, which runs to 1948 and so overlaps these years, contains the name Truman exactly twice — both times in Palestine, and the second time standing beside the Soviet Union, the two of them recognising Israel within a day of each other (receipts below). The corpus contains a classroom in which the superpowers of 1948 are not opposed at all.

4 The thread · where the name came from

Five of the eleven classrooms send the reader to Fulton. Only three of them print the words. Italy gives the sentence in full — «Da Stettino, sul Baltico, a Trieste, sull’Adriatico, una cortina di ferro è calata sul continente», “From Stettin, on the Baltic, to Trieste, on the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the continent” — and Lowe gives the same line in English, with six words no other book has: Churchill “repeated a phrase he had used earlier.” Belarus sends the reader to the town and not the sentence: Churchill delivered «воинственной речью в г. Фултон», a warlike speech in the city of Fulton. Ukraine names the speech three times, summarises what Churchill said about expansion and the “fifth column,” and never prints the phrase at all. Lowe is the only column that hints the words had a life before March 1946. It does not say what that life was.

It began as a fire door. From 1794 the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane advertised an iron curtain that could be dropped between stage and auditorium, because the dangerous half of a theatre is the half with the gas, the ropes and the painted canvas, and the other half is full of people in rows. Through the nineteenth century the fires kept proving the point — the Vienna Ringtheater burned on 8 December 1881 with several hundred dead — and the Austrian and German building codes turned the device into law under the name eiserner Vorhang: a real sheet of iron, on a counterweight, tested regularly, between the performance and the audience.

The metaphor was in politics decades before Fulton, and it did not travel a clean route. Ethel Snowden, a British Labour delegate who went to Soviet Russia in 1920, wrote of crossing into it: “We were behind the ‘iron curtain’ at last!” On 25 February 1945, in the weekly Das Reich, Joseph Goebbels wrote that if Germany surrendered, an iron curtain would come down over the territory the Soviet Union took. On 12 May 1945, four days after the German surrender, Churchill cabled Truman that “an iron curtain is drawn down upon their front. We do not know what is going on behind.” Ten months later he said it out loud, in Missouri, with a president on the platform, and it became the name of the age. That is the whole pedigree, stated flatly: the phrase reached Fulton by way of a Nazi propaganda minister, among others, and none of the classrooms that quote Churchill carries any of it. This is not a charge against the books. It is a description of what a phrase is — a used object, picked up by whoever finds it useful.

The era got its other name in the same eighteen months, and the corpus disagrees about who supplied it. Ukraine credits Orwell: the writer, in the article «Ти та атомна бомба», “published in October 1945, for the first time called the confrontation of that time between the USA and the USSR a ‘cold war,’” and then Bernard Baruch used it officially in April 1947. Italy credits the journalist: it was Walter Lippmann who «battezzò efficacemente guerra fredda», effectively baptised it cold war. Both are half right, and the halves are the point. Neither word divided Europe. Armies divided Europe, and then treaties wrote the division down. What the words did was make the division sayable in one breath — and once it was sayable, each classroom got to choose who is named as having drawn it: a speaker at a lectern, an American president’s change of temperament, or nobody, because it was predetermined.

Who teaches the pedigree. Five of eleven send the student to Fulton — Belarus, Italy, Ukraine, the United Kingdom, the United States. Three of eleven print “iron curtain” at all. One of eleven, Lowe, notes that the phrase was second-hand. Orwell is in four books and is four different people in them: the man who named the era (Ukraine), a writer in Spain (Italy), a quotation about empire (Lowe), a name in a list of dystopians (Russia). Lippmann appears once, in Italy. Snowden, Goebbels and the 1945 telegram appear zero times, and zero of eleven classrooms mention that the words name a piece of fire-safety equipment. In the Ukrainian book, which sends its reader to Fulton three times, the word «завіса» appears exactly once in 240 pages — two hundred pages later, in Brecht’s epic theatre, changing the scenery with the curtain open. And the object itself is still working. In traditional houses across Europe the iron curtain is lowered and checked as a matter of licence, and at the Vienna State Opera the panel has carried a different commissioned artwork every season since the 1990s, raised before the music starts. Anyone who has sat in an old theatre and watched a great iron slab go up has watched the Cold War’s name lift out of the way.

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the credit line. It is the only book in the corpus that prints the chapter’s question as a heading — Which side was to blame? — and then answers it three ways, naming the historians on each side and giving the case against the West its own paragraph. It also does the one piece of arithmetic that deflates its own side’s best story: Marshall Aid “averaged only about 2.5 per cent of the total national income of the 16 countries involved,” which is Lowe telling a British examinee that the most celebrated act of American generosity may not have been the thing that saved Europe. Teaching the controversy is more expensive than teaching the answer, and this is a book written to be examined on.

OpenStax (US) takes the second nod for auditing its own doctrine. On Greece it writes that “Truman was mistaken in assuming all communists acted under the direction of the Soviet Union,” and then: “There is little to suggest that Stalin violated this agreement about Greece — until the United States got involved. Once the fate of Greece became of interest to Truman, Stalin took note of it as well.” That is an American textbook saying that an American containment policy manufactured the Soviet involvement it was written to contain. It also prints Churchill’s Percentages Agreement and his wish that Stalin burn the paper afterwards, which is the least flattering document in the Western file.

Гісем–Мартинюк (UA) is the depth lead and the balance lead among the school books, which is not the same prize. It runs the whole arc across three sections with the money, the tonnage, the dates and the founder lists, and it is the only column that lets both superpowers state a purpose in their own words before the conclusions box files the whole thing under historical necessity. It is also the column with the most at stake, and it shows: the NATO definition ends in the reader’s decade.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) is the depth lead overall — Bretton Woods, the dollar as reserve currency, the Cominform, the currency reform, the blockade, the Atlantic Pact, in one causal sequence. It is a university manual and it is unfair to hold the schoolbooks to it. It is fair to notice what a schoolbook drops when it compresses.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is six books, not eleven countries and not “the world.” Three of the five absences are syllabus boundaries rather than avoidance. The Chinese volume in this corpus is 中外历史纲要 上, the Chinese-history half of the course; the companion world-history volume 下 is not in the corpus, so its zero is a limit of our shelf and not a verdict on a curriculum. The German source is a federal civic-education booklet on National Socialism, and it stops where its subject stops. Sudan’s book is a national and Arab history to 1948.

Russia is the awkward one, and the awkwardness is ours. The volume here is the grade-10 book, whose declared range is 1914–1945; the grade-11 companion that covers 1945 onward is not in this corpus. So the absence of “Cold War” from these 241 pages is a fact about this edition, not a demonstration about Russian teaching. What can be said is narrower and still worth saying: the book does carry its postwar chapter to 1955 without using the phrase. The grade-11 poster aid we hold as a stopgap does use it — and there the Berlin blockade appears not as a narrative but as a row in an answer key: «Первый Берлинский кризис (блокада Западного Берлина)», 1948, in a table whose left-hand column is headed «Советские лидеры» and whose entry for that row is Stalin. The Fulton “iron curtain” sentence appears in the same aid as a caption. A crisis with no verb, filed under a name.

Page numbers are not uniform in quality. Ukraine, Belarus, Russia and the United States are printed-page numbers read off the page. Italy’s extraction carries running heads only intermittently and the offset drifts, so Italian pages are given as extract pages with an approximate printed equivalent. Lowe’s file is a reflowed edition with no page numbers at all; its citations are by section, with the anchors taken from the book’s own printed index.

Kept verbatim because errors are data, and because this set contains a rare head-on collision. OpenStax says that at Yalta the Allies confirmed “the division of Germany and Berlin into three occupied zones (later expanded to four).” Sabbatucci–Vidotto says that at Yalta it was established “that Germany would be divided into four zones of occupation (one of which reserved for France).” Two market-published books, no ministry between them and the page, same conference, different count.

A second keep: NCERT dates the Potsdam Proclamation to 1949 in its Taiwan box. The Potsdam Proclamation is July 1945. A third, smaller: the money. Ukraine, Italy and Lowe all say the Marshall Plan delivered about $13 billion; OpenStax says $12 billion. The books then convert to modern money and disagree again — Ukraine’s $13 billion becomes $125 billion, OpenStax’s $12 billion becomes about $147 billion. The editions are three years apart, which explains some of it and not all of it. The tonnage does the same: Ukraine reports over 2.3 million tons airlifted, Lowe 2 million; Ukraine has an American transport landing every five minutes, OpenStax has one taking off every forty-five seconds at peak. Neither pair is a contradiction once you read the qualifiers. Both pairs will look like one to a student holding two books.

The Sudanese line quoted in the receipts is the weakest evidence in this chapter. That PDF’s Arabic extraction is ligature-mangled — letters decompose and reorder, so the string cannot be searched or copied — and the sentence has been reconstructed to standard orthography from the mangled page with a machine translation used only for discovery. It must be read against the printed page before it is called anything. The counts behind it (two occurrences of ترومان, zero of يالطا, بوتسدام, مارشال, الستار الحديدي and حصار برلين) survive the mangling and are reliable.

Finally, none of these six books is a history of the Cold War’s origins. Five of them give the whole 1945–49 question between four and twelve pages. If you want the archives — the Long Telegram in full, Novikov’s reply, the Soviet files opened after 1991 — every column here will leave you unsatisfied, including the university manual.
