### Pitch 1: The word still in the diplomat’s mouth *(linguistic — seed from non-intervention split)*

- **Seed (from the ladder / tell).** Covering books do not share the foreign label. RU prints scare-quoted «политику невмешательства» and names who proclaimed it (France, Great Britain, the USA) next to who armed the rebels (RU, pp. 102–103). IT stages the agreement: subscribed by Italy and Germany, “respected only by France and Great Britain” (IT, §18.10). UK: all the powers undertook neutrality, “but only Britain and France kept the agreement” (UK, p. 345). US softens to “nonintervention” as prudence (US, p. 496). BY never needs the word — only “factual refusal” then the Axis name. DE names broken neutrality and the secret Condor dispatch, then renames the war *Generalprobe*.
- **Loop.** Democracies sign a Non-Intervention paper → fascist powers and the USSR keep arming clients under or beside that paper → schoolbooks either keep the policy name (and show who broke it), drop the name (help checklist only), or replace the whole foreign story with a rehearsal vocabulary → later speakers still reach for “non-intervention” whenever a civil war has foreign sponsors and great-power caution.
- **Lander.** The phrase **non-intervention** (and its cousins nonintervention / non-interference) in news and argument about someone else’s war. Readers who have never opened a Spain chapter still hear the word when capitals debate arming, embargoes, and “not getting involved.”

- **Receipts-lite.** Non-Intervention Committee (London, 1936); Lowe p. 345 and IT §18.10 as paired “only France and Britain kept it” lines; any recent headline using “non-intervention.” <!-- CHECK: committee membership and chronology -->

### Pitch 2: Guernica still on the wall *(material — seed from DE/UK Condor naming)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Only two covering columns name the Condor Legion and Guernica as one operation: UK (hundred bombers, “killing 1600,” p. 345) and DE (Legion Condor, 26 April 1937, “several hundred civilians,” *Generalprobe*, pp. 7–8). US has Guernica as civilian bombing and a Picasso mural caption without Condor. RU has Picasso’s *Герника* as a culture-plate caption, not a war-narrative raid. IT describes bombardments of inhabited centers as a technical prelude without the place-name. BY: silence.
- **Loop.** German air units train and bomb under live conditions in Spain → one Basque market town becomes the emblem → Picasso paints *Guernica* for the 1937 Paris fair → the image outlives the war as a portable symbol of air war on civilians (DE notes a UN building copy) → classrooms that need German responsibility print Legion + town; classrooms that need only “horror of warfare” can keep the painting and drop the unit.

- **Receipts-lite.** Raid 26 April 1937; Condor Legion; Picasso 1937; Reina Sofía / UN copy as DE’s own receipts trail. <!-- CHECK: death-toll dispute (UK 1600 vs DE several hundred vs later historical ranges) — keep as range, not one number -->

### Pitch 3: The Axis name earned in Spain *(material/diplomatic — seed from BY exclusive)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Belarus alone wires Spain to the birth certificate of the Axis: “In 1936 Hitler and Mussolini, seeing the factual refusal of democratic states to help the Spanish government, decided to form an alliance that received the name ‘Berlin—Rome Axis’” (BY, ~pp. 149–150). Other columns put Italian and German help in Spain and put the Axis elsewhere; BY makes democratic refusal in Spain the hinge sentence for the alliance name.
- **Loop.** Democracies refuse effective aid to the Spanish Republic → Hitler and Mussolini read that refusal as permission and affinity → they formalize a Berlin–Rome alignment while their forces already cooperate in Spain → later “Axis” talk in Munich and world-war chapters inherits a partnership practiced on Spanish soil → the alliance label students meet in 1939–45 had a 1936 Spanish rehearsal of diplomacy, not only of bombing.
- **Lander.** The word **Axis** in every later chapter and in ordinary speech about the Second World War’s fascist coalition. Readers who never study Spain still live with the team name; few are told which civil war the Belarusian classroom uses to mint it. <!-- CHECK: Ciano/Mussolini “Axis” language Oct 1936; Spanish war as concurrent cooperation -->

- **Receipts-lite.** BY ~pp. 149–150; October 1936 Mussolini “axis” speech / German–Italian protocols; feeds ch. 11’s alliance grammar. <!-- CHECK: exact first public “Asse” / “Achse” uses -->

### Pitch 4: Scare-quote volunteers *(linguistic — my candidate, non-obvious)*

- **Seed (from the ladder / pull quote).** Italy alone teaches the student to open a word: Mussolini sent 50,000 “volontari” “but they were in reality regular units” (IT, §18.10). The same section makes the *other* volunteers — International Brigades, antifascist emigration, “Oggi in Spagna, domani in Italia” — morally heavier than their military weight, and stages Italians defeating Italians at Guadalajara. UK prints 70,000 Italian *troops* without the scare-quote lesson; RU prints a 125-thousand “expeditionary corps” and separate “brigades of volunteers” for the Republic; DE never needs Italian volunteer vocabulary; US has no headcount.
- **Loop.** A sending state labels regulars “volunteers” to preserve non-intervention fictions → a domestic textbook a generation later puts the label in quotes and corrects it → a second volunteer stream (International Brigades) keeps the word in earnest for men who crossed borders without a state order → later arguments about “foreign fighters,” “volunteers,” and deniable force still run on the same split: who owns the uniform under the nickname.
- **Lander.** The word **volunteer** in news about foreign fighters and deniable deployments — and in ordinary life (volunteer work, volunteer armies) where the word still promises unpaid willingness. Spain is where schoolbooks first show the same word doing honest and dishonest work on one page. <!-- CHECK: CTV / Corpo Truppe Volontarie as official Italian designation; Brigades recruitment -->
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 unmasks “volunteers” as regulars in the same sentence (IT); 2 print Republican International Brigades as volunteers with organization credit (RU, UK); 0 of 11 join scare-quote state volunteers → earnest Brigade volunteers → modern “foreign fighter” talk as one loop.
- **Receipts-lite.** IT “50.000 ‘volontari’” line; UK 70,000 troops; RU 125-thousand corps; CTV designation in standard military histories. <!-- CHECK: force-size discrepancy RU 125k / IT 50k / UK 70k — errors/ranges are data -->
- **Why it fits.** Non-obvious own candidate; pure vocabulary finding already centered by the chapter question; Italian self-marking is the fairness engine’s friend. Shared lander via a common English/European word, not via a Spanish-only memory. Causal precision: the scare quotes did not lose Franco the war — they are what one classroom does when its own state’s euphemism survives into the textbook.
