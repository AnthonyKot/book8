China, 1911–1949

On the night of 10 October 1911, in Wuchang on the Yangtze, soldiers of an engineering battalion of the New Army turn their rifles on their own officers. Thirty-eight years later, on the afternoon of 1 October 1949, a man stands on a gate tower in Beijing and announces that a central people’s government has been established today. In December the other government’s aircraft land in Taipei. What the classrooms call the years in between is the subject of this chapter.

The question · vocabulary

What do you call the war you won?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 10 October 1911: mutiny at Wuchang. Province after province declares against the Qing. On 1 January 1912 a republic is proclaimed at Nanjing with Sun Yat-sen as provisional president; on 12 February the last emperor abdicates and the presidency passes to Yuan Shikai.
  • Yuan dies in 1916. For the next twelve years no government in Beijing controls more than a fraction of the country; regional commanders do. 4 May 1919: students demonstrate in Beijing against the Shandong clauses of the Paris settlement. The Chinese Communist Party is founded in 1921; from 1924 it works inside Sun’s Nationalist Party.
  • 1926–28: the Northern Expedition. On 12 April 1927 Nationalist forces and allied groups in Shanghai turn on the Communists and the labour unions; a nationwide purge follows. A Nationalist government is established at Nanjing.
  • 1930–34: five encirclement campaigns against the Communist base areas in the south. In October 1934 the main Communist force leaves Jiangxi. In January 1935 a Party conference at Zunyi changes the leadership. The main columns reach northern Shaanxi in October 1935; three Red Army forces join at Huining in October 1936.
  • December 1936: Chiang Kai-shek is seized at Xi’an by his own commanders and released on terms. 7 July 1937: full-scale war with Japan begins at the Marco Polo Bridge; it runs until Japan’s surrender in the summer of 1945. Historians commonly put Chinese deaths in that war in the range of fifteen to twenty million.
  • August–October 1945: Mao Zedong and Chiang Kai-shek negotiate at Chongqing. The Double Tenth Agreement is signed on 10 October 1945. General fighting resumes in 1946.
  • Autumn 1948 to January 1949: three large campaigns destroy the Nationalist armies in the north. Beiping falls in January 1949, Nanjing in April. On 1 October 1949 the People’s Republic is proclaimed in Beijing. The Republic of China moves to Taipei and holds China’s seat at the United Nations until 1971.
  • All eight in-scope books in this corpus cover the period. That is a curriculum norm, not a finding.

2 The ladder

Eight narrators who cover the period, ordered from the classroom where nobody starts the war and nobody founds the republic, out to the classroom where a named party launches one war and a named party leads another — in the same sentence. Germany’s volume is a National Socialism booklet and Sudan’s is a regional syllabus; both are in the receipts with their search stems. Kazakhstan’s book is a national history and is never a world-event column here.

One month, two names

3 The tell

Nothing on this ladder is in dispute about the outcome. Eight books, one date: on 1 October 1949 the Communists have the mainland and the Nationalists have an island. Seven of the eight also agree on what the fighting was — a civil war, guerra civile, гражданская война, громадянська війна, Civil Wars in China. The eighth book uses the same words, and then uses different ones, and the difference is the chapter.

Look at the shortest sentence in the corpus on this topic. It is a line in a chronological table at the back of the Chinese volume, twenty-two characters long, with a comma in the middle: 国民党发动全面内战，人民解放战争开始 — “the Nationalist Party launched an all-out civil war; the People’s War of Liberation began.” One month, June 1946. One war. Two names. Which name a reader gets depends entirely on which army is the subject of the clause. On the Nationalist side of the comma there is a transitive verb, an agent and the word 内战, civil war. On the Communist side there is an intransitive verb, no agent at all, and the word 解放, liberation. The book’s Unit 8 opening does it again at greater length, and adds the motive: the Nationalists launched the civil war “in order to maintain dictatorial rule,” and the Party “led the People’s War of Liberation.”

The obvious reading — that this classroom has no word for civil war — is wrong, and the correct reading is more interesting. 内战 appears twenty times in the volume. Some of those uses are its own: the peaceful resolution of the Xi’an Incident of December 1936 “basically ended the ten years’ civil war,” 十年内战, which is 1927 to 1937 and is precisely the fighting the Party spent those years doing. Others are the demands of people the book approves of — students marching in 1947 under the slogan 反内战, “oppose the civil war.” The word is not unavailable. It is allocated. A civil war is what you are in before you are winning, and what the other side starts.

1950 年 6 月 25 日，朝鲜内战爆发。

“On 25 June 1950, the Korean civil war broke out.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 (2019) · 第26课, printed p. 176 — three pages after the founding of the People’s Republic

Three pages after the gate tower, the same volume reaches a different peninsula and reaches for a different grammar. The Korean war does not get a launcher. It 爆发 — bursts out, the verb this book uses for earthquakes of history it does not intend to assign. The book that will not allow its own war of 1946 to break out allows Korea’s to. This is the distance rule of the whole project, visible inside a single volume and a span of three pages: the further the actor from the narrator’s own state, the looser the grammar. It is also the seam that Chapter 15 opens along, so it is worth marking here, in the book where the seam runs shortest.

Now watch the same word travel without its war. Exactly one book on this ladder puts the winner’s own name for the winning army on the page: OpenStax, p. 582 — “the CCP’s People’s Liberation Army decisively defeated the forces of the GMD.” Britain, Italy, Russia, Belarus, Ukraine and India never name it; the force that takes the mainland is “the communists,” “Mao,” «l’esercito di Mao», the CCP. And twenty lines below its own unquoted Liberation Army, on the same page, OpenStax reaches Tibet in 1950 and puts the identical root in quotation marks: the Seventeen Point Agreement was “an event described by the CCP as the ‘liberation of Tibet.’” Same page, same book, same word. Used as a proper noun it passes without comment; used as a description it is handed back to the people who said it. That is not inconsistency. It is a survey deciding, twice on one page, where a name stops being a name and starts being a claim.

The numbers sort the books along a different axis, and the sorting is the reverse of what a reader might expect. Take the Long March, the one episode every covering book except Belarus and Ukraine narrates. Britain: “almost 100 000 communists set out,” “about 6000 miles in 368 days,” and “the 20 000 survivors found refuge at Yenan in Shensi province.” Italy: about 100,000 left Hunan and “fewer than 10,000 arrived at their destination, after a march lasting a year and 10,000 kilometres long.” India: 6,000 miles, no headcount. The United States: no headcount and no distance. And the Chinese volume, which devotes a full lesson and a route map to the march, gives the sharpest loss figure of all — after breaking four Nationalist blockade lines the Central Red Army “sharply decreased from over 80,000 people to over 30,000 people” — and then gives no arrival figure whatever. The two books written furthest from the event count both ends of the march and disagree with each other by a factor of two. The book written closest counts the beginning of the loss, in the paragraph where the loss is attributed to “‘Left’ errors,” and lets the arithmetic stop there. What it supplies instead is a distance: 二万五千里长征, the twenty-five-thousand-li Long March, a number that is part of the name.

The 1927 killings work the same way and faster. The Chinese lesson is exact about the date and the deed: on 12 April 1927 Chiang Kai-shek “launched a counter-revolutionary coup in Shanghai, wantonly arresting and killing Communists and the revolutionary masses.” It gives no total. Lowe, in a British survey with no stake in the question, calls the same event a “terrible ‘purification movement’” and adds the sentence nobody else in the corpus writes: “some estimates put the total murdered as high as 250 000.” The classroom that owns the dead names the crime to the day; the classroom nine thousand kilometres away supplies the arithmetic. This is the shape the whole corpus keeps producing, and it is worth saying plainly, because it is not a moral ranking: proximity buys detail, and distance buys the count.

Russia’s column is the strangest object here and most of the strangeness is a calendar. Chubaryan’s volume stops in 1945, so the war that ends the story falls outside it — and yet the ending is in the book, twice, in two of the least emphatic positions a textbook offers. The 1946–49 war and the loss of the mainland appear inside the boxed biography of the man who lost: “After defeat in the civil war (1946–1949), together with 2 million supporters he fled to Taiwan.” The People’s Republic itself appears eighty-eight pages later, in a paragraph about the American plan for a separate peace treaty with Japan, as a subordinate clause attached to a list of excluded parties — “and a number of other interested countries, including the People’s Republic of China, which was formed in October 1949.” One relative clause. The narrator that would sign a treaty of friendship and alliance with that state four months later gives its founding a dependent clause about somebody else’s conference.

Belarus does something related with less material. China gets a corner of the interwar chapter; the split between the Communists and the Kuomintang “led to a long and brutal civil war” with no dates, no march, no Mao in the running text, and no ending. The ending arrives seventy-five pages later, in the chapter on decolonisation, in a sentence with no human being in it at all: “In 1949 the People’s Republic of China was formed, which had enormous significance for the strengthening of national-liberation forces in Asia.” The republic forms itself. Note the phrase that does the evaluating — “national-liberation forces.” The adjective the Chinese book attaches to its war, this book attaches to a category of world history, and attaches it from a distance great enough that no Chinese person has to be named for it to work.

One last word behaves oddly across the columns, and it is the word for the men who ran China between 1916 and 1928. Lowe gives them a period with capital letters, the Warlord Era, and uses the term straight, twenty-seven times. NCERT keeps it at arm’s length in single quotation marks: Chiang “launched a military campaign to control the ‘warlords’.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto translates and then apologises for the translation — the military governors, «i cosiddetti signori della guerra», “the so-called lords of war.” Russia and Belarus decline the metaphor entirely and use a class label instead, «военно-феодальные клики», “military-feudal cliques.” The Chinese volume uses the word the others are all rendering, 军阀, thirty-eight times, and it is the source: a compound of “army” and the old word for a powerful family, which reached English through the newspapers of the 1910s and 1920s and is now the standard English noun for an armed man who holds territory anywhere on earth. Four classrooms, four different degrees of confidence in the same borrowed noun, and two of them printing it inside quotation marks to keep it at a distance.

4 The thread

5 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the credit line, and earns it three times over on a topic where Britain has almost nothing at stake. It is the only book in the corpus that puts a number on the 1927 killings. It is the only one that tells a student the ending was contingent — §19.4(b) is headed “Victory for the communists was still not inevitable,” and the section then explains, in order, what the Nationalist government did to lose. And it is the only one that lets a student see the story being argued about: it quotes the American journalist Edgar Snow by name on the Long March, and then reports that Jung Chang and Jon Halliday, in a book of 2005, “suggested that Mao’s ‘breakout’ in October 1934 was actually permitted by Chiang Kai-shek.” No other column shows the reader that the sources disagree.

OpenStax (US) takes the second nod for explaining a defeat instead of asserting a victory. Its account of 1945–49 is almost entirely mechanism: the Japanese-seized factories that the Nationalists kept and did not reopen, the urban unemployment that followed, the hyperinflation nobody could control, the enlisted men who were “often underfed and scorned by their officers,” the wartime taxes the peasants remembered. It also handles the winner’s vocabulary with more care than any other column — printing the People’s Liberation Army as a name and quarantining “liberation of Tibet” as a claim, on the same page.

纲要 (CN) is the depth lead, and depth is a different prize from distance. Fifty-odd pages, an engineering battalion named, a route map, a chronology to the month — no other book here can afford any of it. It should also be credited for something a self-narrating state is not obliged to do: it prints its own side’s failures in its own voice. The fifth encirclement campaign is lost because “the Central Committee committed ‘Left’ errors”; the retreat opens with “the error of flightism”; the force is cut from over eighty thousand to over thirty thousand before Zunyi. The book is candid about being wrong. It is the naming that is not up for discussion.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight pinned editions, not eight countries and not eleven narrators. Every in-scope book covers the period — eight of eight — so there is no avoidance signal here and no signature topic. Germany’s booklet is a National Socialism primer and Sudan’s is a regional syllabus; those two zeros are syllabus boundaries and their search stems are in the receipts. Kazakhstan’s volume is a national history and is never a peer column in a world-event ladder in this book.

Two columns are truncated by their own volumes and the truncation is not a choice. Ukraine’s book begins in 1945, so its column covers the last four years only: no 1911, no May Fourth, no Long March. Russia’s ends in 1945, so its treatment of the ending survives in a biography box and one relative clause. Neither is avoidance. Both are the edges of a syllabus, and both are why the ladder above is ordered by what the war is called rather than by how much of it each book tells.

Machine-translated files were used for discovery only. The Chinese, Russian, Belarusian, Ukrainian and Italian lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English here for reading; every one was checked character by character against the original file before it was quoted. One thing that check caught belongs in this section rather than a footnote: the Russian book does not use the phrase “Long March.” It writes «Великий, или Северо-Западный поход 1934—1936 гг.» — “the Great, or North-Western, Campaign of 1934–1936.” The machine translation of that page returned “the Long, or North-Western March,” quietly restoring the international name over the Russian one. That is exactly the smoothing this project exists to avoid, and it happened to us, on this page, at the discovery stage.

Kept verbatim, because errors are data. OpenStax dates the Long March to the wrong year in its running text — “in 1935, Mao was able to lead them on the Long March” — where its own peers, and the Chinese volume, put the departure in October 1934. Belarus writes that Japan “occupied Manchuria in 1932”; the invasion began on 18 September 1931 and the puppet state was proclaimed in March 1932, so the sentence has compressed an eighteen-month campaign onto its endpoint. NCERT writes that “a republic” was “established in 1911 under Sun Yat-sen,” which folds a six-week provisional presidency that began on 1 January 1912 into the year before it. And two books send the Long March to the wrong province in the same way: Italy has the marchers transfer to «la regione settentrionale dello Shanxi» and NCERT has them go “6,000 gruelling and difficult miles to Shanxi,” then locate their new base at Yanan — which is in Shaanxi, a different province with a nearly identical romanisation. Britain’s older spelling, “Shensi,” gets it right by avoiding the collision.

One attribution needs care. The Chinese chronology line that this chapter leans on is a table entry, not running prose, and table entries compress. The reading offered above — that the name changes with the subject — is supported by the Unit 8 opening and the Lesson 25 narrative in the same volume, which is why both are quoted. A reader who wants to test it should read those two passages, not the table alone.

Page conventions differ by book and are cited as found. Chinese printed pages are four less than the PDF’s; Russian, Belarusian and Ukrainian printed pages are one less. Italian and OpenStax numbers are the ones carried in the extraction. The Lowe extraction has no printed page numbers in the body, so pages here come from that book’s own index and are given with the section number, which is the durable reference.

Finally, none of these books is a history of China. Six of the eight are histories of Europe or of the world with a Chinese chapter; one is a survey of paths to modernisation; one is the national story told by the state that won. If you want the thirty-eight years as they were lived in a Henan village, or in a Shanghai mill, or by a conscript on either side, every column on this ladder will leave you unsatisfied — including the one that runs fifty pages.
