### Pitch 1: The bitter drink that opened the interior *(my candidate — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** NCERT states the timing and refuses the reason: “In Africa, Europeans traded on the coast, except in South Africa, and only in the late nineteenth century did they venture into the interior” (IN, p. 136). Every other column starts the story *after* that sentence — Lowe’s “after 1880,” Belarus’s “Africa became,” Russia’s “was divided.” One book, OpenStax, has the missing half a chapter earlier: “by the 1870s the Dutch were successfully growing them [cinchona trees] in their colony of Indonesia and producing additional quinine, a development that assisted Europeans in their exploitation of the African interior” (US, p. 350).
- **Loop.** Europeans had traded on West African coasts for four centuries and died inland of malaria at rates that made conquest arithmetic impossible → in the 1840s the British establish that quinine taken *before* exposure prevents the disease, and by the 1870s Dutch cinchona plantations in Java make it cheap and plentiful → shallow-draught steamboats let the same men go up the Niger and the Congo instead of stopping at the bar → the interior stops being lethal in the same decade the Berlin Conference makes “effective occupation” the test of a claim, and the race inland begins.
- **Lander.** The bitterness in tonic water is quinine — it was mixed into gin because officers had to swallow the dose daily. The drink in the reader’s hand is the prophylactic that made the map on their wall possible.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms names quinine at all (US), and it names it in the *technology* section, sixty pages from the partition; 0 of 11 join it to the timing NCERT flatly states.
- **Receipts-lite.** William Baikie’s 1854 Niger expedition — quinine prophylaxis, no European deaths; Java cinchona plantations, Dutch East Indies, 1850s–70s; Schweppes has sold “Indian Tonic Water” under that name since the nineteenth century. <!-- CHECK: Baikie 1854 Pleiad expedition, zero European deaths; Dutch Java cinchona monopoly by 1880s -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** It answers the one question all seven books leave open — *why the 1880s?* — with an object, not a motive. It also inherits cleanly from ch. 1: cinchona was stolen out of the Andes and replanted in Asian colonies the same way tea was stolen out of China. Same century, same botanists’ trick, second time.

### Pitch 2: The tyre and the tally of hands *(my candidate)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** OpenStax spends its most graphic paragraph on the Congo Free State — men required to “harvest rubber from wild rubber tree vines,” quotas so large the latex was scraped off their own skin, and a Force Publique that “cut a hand from each victim to maintain a tally of the dead” (US, pp. 368–369) — and never says what the rubber was *for*. Sabbatucci–Vidotto say what it was for, twenty pages earlier and in the Second Industrial Revolution chapter: “In 1888 the invention of the pneumatic tyre by the Scot … Dunlop opened new horizons for the rubber industry” (IT, p. 122). Neither book joins the two sentences.
- **Loop.** The pneumatic tyre (1888) turns the bicycle into mass transport and then puts the motor car on the road → world demand for wild rubber goes vertical in exactly the years Leopold II holds the Congo basin as personal property, recognised at Berlin → the Free State meets demand with a quota system enforced by a militia paid in cartridges, which are audited by severed hands → the boom funds the Belgian public works Leopold is remembered for, and collapses only when plantation rubber from Southeast Asia undercuts it after 1910.
- **Lander.** Every bicycle and every car the reader has ever ridden rolls on the invention that created the quota. The tyre is the demand curve; the tally of hands is the supply.
- **Who tells it.** 0 of 11 classrooms connect them. 1 of 11 (US) describes the quota and the hands; 1 of 11 (IT) dates the tyre; 1 of 11 (IT) calls the Free State “a paradoxical euphemism.”
- **Receipts-lite.** Roger Casement’s consular report to the Foreign Office, 1904; E. D. Morel’s *Red Rubber* (1906); Alice Seeley Harris’s photographs, credited by name in OpenStax; Belgian annexation of the Free State, 1908. <!-- CHECK: Casement report published Feb 1904; Morel, Red Rubber, 1906 -->
- **Bonus for Limits if this wins.** Sabbatucci–Vidotto calls him “Robert Dunlop”; the pneumatic-tyre patent is John Boyd Dunlop’s. Errors are data, and this one is on the same page as the seed. <!-- CHECK: John Boyd Dunlop, pneumatic tyre patent 1888 -->

### Pitch 3: The straight lines *(the known seed, developed)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Sabbatucci–Vidotto close the partition with borders “traced on the geographic map (in some cases along meridians and parallels) without taking any account of tribal divisions and of the pre-existing ethno-linguistic realities” — and add the sentence that makes it a live problem: those borders “were nevertheless destined to be preserved at the moment when those composite and artificial entities became independent states” (IT, p. 144). OpenStax supplies the room: “without input from Africans, European nations simply allotted different parts of the continent to one another” (US, p. 352).
- **Loop.** Berlin’s “effective occupation” rule rewards whoever notifies a claim first, so claims are filed faster than they can be surveyed → boundaries get written as coordinates in European capitals because coordinates are the only thing the negotiators can agree on without a survey → independence in the 1950s–60s arrives with the Organisation of African Unity resolving to keep the colonial lines rather than reopen every one of them → the coordinates outlive the empires that wrote them.
- **Lander.** Open the map app. The border between Egypt and Sudan runs along 22°N; Egypt–Libya sits on a meridian; the corners of Namibia, Botswana, Zambia and Zimbabwe meet at a point. Those are not rivers or ridges — they are ruler marks from a room the chapter opens in.
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 classrooms say the borders were drawn without regard to who lived inside them (IT, US); 1 of 11 (IT) says they follow meridians and parallels; 1 of 11 (IT) says they survived independence.
- **Receipts-lite.** General Act of the Berlin Conference, 26 February 1885; Anglo-German agreement of 1890 (the Caprivi Strip, still on the map as a panhandle); OAU Cairo resolution on the inviolability of inherited frontiers, 1964. <!-- CHECK: OAU AHG/Res.16(I), Cairo, July 1964 -->

### Pitch 4: A canal, a debt, and the alliance that fought 1914 *(my candidate — braids to ch. 4)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Belarus states the motive in one clause — Britain in 1882 “occupied Egypt and thereby established control over the most important global artery, the Suez Canal” (BY, p. 77) — and Sabbatucci–Vidotto state the consequence nobody else states: after Fashoda, France withdrew, and the episode “opened the road to a closer understanding between the two powers” (IT, p. 144). Russia records the result without the cause: “Anglo-French agreements were concluded on the partition of territories and spheres of influence in Africa” (RU, p. 24).
- **Loop.** Egypt borrows in Europe to dig the canal and modernise, defaults, and is put under European debt administration → an Egyptian nationalist movement threatens the bondholders and the canal, so Britain occupies Egypt in 1882 “temporarily” → Egypt drags Britain into Sudan, where the Mahdist state has taken Khartoum, and the reconquest of 1898 marches a British army straight into a French column at Fashoda → France backs down, and the two empires convert their African quarrel into the colonial swap of 1904 (Britain gets Egypt, France gets Morocco) that is the Entente Cordiale → the alliance that faces Germany in 1914 is assembled out of an African argument about a ditch.
- **Lander.** The Suez Canal is still the argument: roughly a tenth of world seaborne trade, and six days of it stopped in March 2021 when one ship turned sideways and supermarket shelves in eleven countries noticed. <!-- CHECK: Ever Given grounded 23–29 March 2021; Suez carries roughly 10–12% of global trade -->
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 11 classrooms hold pieces (BY: canal and the 1904 swap; RU: the agreements and the Entente; IT: Egypt → Sudan → Fashoda → *intesa*); 1 of 11 connects Fashoda to the Entente; 0 of 11 run the chain from the debt to the alliance.
- **Receipts-lite.** Caisse de la Dette Publique, 1876; Anglo-French Entente Cordiale declarations, 8 April 1904; the canal is still there, still counted daily by the Suez Canal Authority.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (quinine)** first: it is the only one that answers the *why then* the ladder leaves
hanging, the lander is an object on a bar shelf everywhere in the corpus, and it braids to ch. 1’s
stolen tea without repeating it. **Pitch 4** second, and the strongest if the braid to ch. 4 matters
more than the object. **Pitch 2** is the most vivid and the most likely to tip into the “look what
they hid from you” register that CONTEXT.md bans — writable, but it needs a cold hand.
**Pitch 3** has the best lander per word and the least new history in it; it may work better as a
two-sentence echo inside whichever thread wins than as the thread itself.
