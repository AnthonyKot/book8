### Pitch 1: The Shinkolobwe Mine & the Smartphone Battery
- **Seed**: OpenStax (US) names the Manhattan Project, but 10 of 11 textbooks are silent on where the raw fissile material for the first atomic bomb actually came from.
- **The Loop**: In 1940, Edgar Sengier, director of the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga, secretly shipped 1,200 tons of high-grade uranium pitchblende from the Shinkolobwe mine in the Belgian Congo to a warehouse in Staten Island. That Congolese ore supplied over 80% of the uranium-235 enriched at Oak Ridge for the Hiroshima bomb (*Little Boy*). Post-1945 covert operations to secure Katanga’s mineral wealth destabilized the Congo during independence, creating a mining infrastructure that shifted from uranium to cobalt and lithium.
- **The Lander**: The lithium-ion battery powering the reader’s smartphone or laptop today comes from the exact same Katanga mining belt in Congo that yielded the uranium dropped on Hiroshima.
- **Classroom Count**: 1 of 11 classrooms names the Manhattan Project (US); 0 of 11 name the Congolese mine that fed it.

### Pitch 2: The 72-Hour Map: The 38th Parallel and the Kurils
- **Seed**: Russian and Belarusian textbooks pair the atomic bombs with the Soviet invasion of Manchuria as co-forces of surrender; US and UK books frame Soviet entry as a post-Hiroshima spoiler.
- **The Loop**: The Potsdam agreement bound the USSR to enter the Pacific war three months after Germany's defeat. Sensing Japan's imminent collapse after Hiroshima, Stalin accelerated the Soviet invasion of Manchuria, Sakhalin, and the Kurils on August 8. In panic that Red Army troops would occupy the entire Korean peninsula before US forces arrived, two American officers (Dean Rusk and Charles Bonesteel) were given 30 minutes on the night of August 10 to draw a line across a National Geographic map—selecting the 38th parallel.
- **The Lander**: The 38th parallel dividing Korea and the disputed Kuril Islands on today's news map were physically fixed in the 72 hours between the Hiroshima and Nagasaki blasts.
- **Classroom Count**: 0 of 11 classrooms connect the timing of the atomic bombs to the hasty drafting of East Asia's Cold War borders.

### Pitch 3: "Thousands of Hiroshimas": From Bombing to Fallout Language
- **Seed**: Kazakhstan’s national history textbook embeds Hiroshima and Nagasaki inside its account of the Semipalatinsk nuclear testing ground, quoting President Nazarbayev that Soviet testing detonated "more than 2.5 thousand bombs dropped on Hiroshima."
- **The Loop**: The shock of Hiroshima forced a global vocabulary shift: nuclear fission transformed overnight from a theoretical physics paper into the ultimate measure of destruction. In 1953, Eisenhower launched "Atoms for Peace" to reframe nuclear technology into civilian electricity, while both superpowers conducted over 2,000 atmospheric and underground tests in Kazakhstan, Nevada, and the Pacific. 
- **The Lander**: The word "atomic" used in modern energy debates, radiation therapy, and nuclear power grids entered public language specifically to quantify the blast at Hiroshima.
- **Classroom Count**: 1 of 11 classrooms (KZ) links Hiroshima to civilian nuclear testing fallout; 10 treat Hiroshima strictly as a WWII military climax.

### Pitch 4: Kokura's Luck: How a Morning Cloud Selected Nagasaki
- **Seed**: All textbooks present Nagasaki as the pre-planned target of August 9, 1945; none explain why Fat Man was detonated over Nagasaki rather than its intended target.
- **The Loop**: The US Target Committee selected four un-bombed urban centers to measure exact blast radius. On the morning of August 9, the bomber *Bockscar* flew three passes over its primary target, the industrial city of Kokura, but heavy cloud cover and smoke from a nearby conventional raid on Yahata obscured visual sighting. Low on fuel, the plane diverted to its secondary target, Nagasaki, dropping the bomb through a break in the clouds.
- **The Lander**: The Japanese phrase "Kokura's luck" (*Kokura no tsuki*) remains in common use today to describe escaping an catastrophic disaster by sheer chance.
- **Classroom Count**: 0 of 11 classrooms mention Kokura or explain why Nagasaki became the second target.
