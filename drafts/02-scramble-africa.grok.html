Scramble for Africa

In January 1885, Mahdist forces enter Khartoum and the Sudanese syllabus will later call it liberation. In 1898 a British–Egyptian army takes the Nile cities back. Between those two dates, and for a decade after, European cabinets draw lines across a continent most of their students will only ever meet as colored blocks on a wall map. Seven classrooms that cover the drawing cannot agree who holds the pen — or how many pages the pen is worth.

The question · agency

Who takes Africa — and who, in each classroom’s grammar, is allowed to act?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • From the 1870s into the early 1900s, European powers accelerate formal colonial claims across Africa — a process later nicknamed the “Scramble for Africa.”
  • Berlin Conference, 1884–1885: convened largely over the Congo question; European states set rules of claim (notably “effective occupation”) and recognize spheres. No African delegates.
  • Britain occupies Egypt 1882 (Suez). France takes Tunisia 1881; Italy later Libya 1911; Germany builds colonies in SW and E Africa, Togo, Cameroon.
  • In Sudan, Muhammad Ahmad (the Mahdi) builds a state after taking Khartoum in January 1885; Anglo-Egyptian reconquest culminates 1898 (Omdurman / Karari).
  • Battle of Adwa, 1896: Ethiopia under Menelik II defeats Italy; Ethiopia remains independent. Liberia also remains independent into the early 20th century.
  • King Leopold II of the Belgians rules the Congo Free State as a personal possession until Belgium annexes it in 1908 after international scandal over forced labor and atrocities.
  • By the eve of World War I, nearly the entire continent is under European rule or claim; redivision of colonies sits among the war’s stated and unstated stakes.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators who cover the partition, ordered from thinnest agency (Africa “taken over,” no named hand) to thickest (named liberators, named invaders, named conference without Africans). Ukraine’s volume starts in 1945, China’s is national history for this span, Germany’s booklet is National Socialism only — out of scope here, not avoidance. Kazakhstan is never a world-history peer column.

3 The tell

Nobody in this set denies that Europe partitioned Africa. The disagreement is about the subject of the verb — and about how many pages that verb is allowed to occupy.

Start with space, because space is the loudest silence. Norman Lowe’s United Kingdom volume — the book of the power that held the largest African empire — gives the Scramble one paragraph inside the causes of World War I. No Berlin Conference. No Congo. No Khartoum, no Mahdi, no Adwa, no Fashoda. Africa “was taken over.” Markets and raw materials explain the idea. The double article in the printed line — “the ‘the Scramble for Africa’” — is a compositor’s slip that survives into the fifth edition; errors are data too. The classroom effect of the paragraph is cleaner than the typo: the empire that ran the Scramble treats the Scramble as furniture for 1914.

India’s NCERT timeline marks “Beginning of the European ‘Scramble for Africa’” for 1880–90, then a box where “some of the European countries reached an agreement to divide up Africa as colonies for themselves.” Agreement is the verb. No state is named as drawer of a line. Russia’s Чубарьян folds Africa into “New Imperialism” as a path toward world war: by 1914 the continent “was divided among” a named list of powers. The list is agentive for Europe; Africa remains the object of a passive. Belarus upgrades the label — “continent of classical colonialism” — and keeps the same passive division, then, elsewhere, lets England “occupy” Egypt and France “seize” Tunisia. The continent becomes a category; the powers still hold the verbs.

«…confini spesso arbitrari, tracciati sulla carta geografica… senza tenere alcun conto delle divisioni tribali e delle preesistenti realtà etnicolinguistiche.»

“…borders often arbitrary, traced on the geographic map… without taking any account of tribal divisions and of the pre-existing ethno-linguistic realities.”

IT · Sabbatucci & Vidotto, Il mondo contemporaneo (2004) · p. 144

Italy alone stages the full sequence the dossier maps: Leopold’s Congo, Berlin 1884–85 as “first sanction to the partition,” the Free State as a “paradoxical euphemism,” the Mahdi’s Khartoum, Fashoda 1898, then partition “virtually complete.” Fashoda is an Italian signature topic in this corpus — a near-war on the Nile between Britain and France that most other books never stage. OpenStax (US) adds the number that makes the scale legible — Europeans from roughly 10 percent of the continent to 90 — and the sentence that names the empty chair: Berlin allotted Africa “without input from Africans.” Leopold’s Congo Free State is not a euphemism here; it is a private colony with a Force Publique that cut hands to tally the dead.

Then the pole that only one narrator can occupy. Sudan’s ministry book does not open with Berlin. It opens with the Mahdist Revolution — lessons from roughly pages 14 to 41, liberation of Khartoum as the peak of success, the Mahdi leading “from victory to victory until he liberated the country.” Condominium rule after 1898 is the sequel: “invaders” extend influence; Britain wants exclusive power under a dual name. When the same book reaches the European map on page 196, Britain “occupied” Egypt and Sudan; the grammar is active and colonial. The African classroom’s long story is resistance and state-building. The short European list is the reconquest that ends it. Put that beside Lowe’s one paragraph and the finding is structural: the empire’s self-pole is thin; the colonized pole is thick. Space is agency’s twin.

Numbers travel the same gradient. Belarus counts about 11 thousand Italians lost at Adwa and 28 thousand English dead in the Boer War. OpenStax counts 6,000 Italians killed at the same Adwa — the two books disagree, and the disagreement is data. Sudan counts its own: ten thousand Ansar killed in one attack on El Obeid in September 1882. European dead get European school numbers. African dead, across most of this ladder, do not — except where the African narrator is counting its own army.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns the credit lead for sequence. It alone among the European volumes walks Leopold, Berlin, Free State euphemism, Mahdi, Fashoda, and the arbitrary borders in one continuous Africa section. The partition is not a single passive clause; it is a process with a timetable and a map problem that outlives the empires.

OpenStax (US) deserves the quantification and empty-chair lines: 10 percent to 90 percent, Berlin “without input from Africans,” and the Force Publique hand-tally. That is still a Western survey voice. It is clearer about African absence at the conference table than books that list only European sharers.

MoE Sudan (SD) is the depth and agency lead, not the distance lead. Twenty-eight pages of Mahdist revolution and state make the Scramble legible as something done to and in a country that had already built a capital at Omdurman. Depth is not neutrality. The chapter needs SD for the liberation grammar and the page count; it needs IT and US for the European machinery SD compresses into a later list.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is seven books that put the late-nineteenth-century partition of Africa on the syllabus. It is not “Africa” as lived history, and it is not every textbook in these countries — only the pinned editions in the corpus.

Ukraine (1945–2018), China’s national-history volume for this span, and Germany’s National Socialism booklet are out of chronological or topical scope. Their zeros are other syllabuses, not a verdict that the Scramble did not matter. Kazakhstan is national history and is never a peer world-history column.

Findings kept verbatim because errors are data: Sudan’s page 196 lists “Berlin Conference 1878 AD” alongside 1884 — almost certainly the Congress of Berlin (1878, Balkans/Ottoman) conflated with the West Africa conference of 1884–85. The UK edition’s doubled “the ‘the Scramble’” is retained as printed. Adwa casualties: BY ~11 thousand Italians lost; US 6,000 killed and 3,000 captured — both quoted, neither reconciled here.

All Sudanese Arabic lines above ride a ligature-mangled extraction. Machine reading cannot verify them; the printed PDF page must be eye-checked before any SD sentence ships as a hard claim. Machine-translated discovery files led to the pages; Russian, Belarusian, and Italian quotes are taken from original extractions and translated for the reader.

None of these classrooms is a full military history of the Congo basin, the desert campaigns, or the Rhodesian and German East African wars. If you want campaign maps and total African dead, you will leave every column short.
