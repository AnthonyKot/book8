### Pitch 1: The booklet in the drawer *(my candidate — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Two columns describe the same crossing without noticing it is
  a crossing. OpenStax: “a small band of would-be Bosnian Serb … assassins armed with
  weapons from the Serbian military crossed the border from Serbia into Bosnia-Herzegovina”
  (US, p. 436). Sabbatucci–Vidotto: the assassin belonged to an organisation “that had its
  operational base in Serbia and enjoyed a certain tolerance on the part of the government
  of that country” (IT, p. 269). A group of young men moves pistols and bombs across the
  frontier of a great power, and no book on the ladder mentions a document, a check or a
  guard — because in June 1914 there was not one to mention.
- **Loop.** Pre-1914 Europe travelled largely without passports; the frontier the killers
  crossed was a line on a map with a customs post on it → within days of the outbreak every
  belligerent imposes emergency controls on movement and on foreigners, and Britain’s
  wartime document adds the two things a modern passport has and a nineteenth-century one
  did not: a photograph and a physical description → the war ends and the controls do not
  → in October 1920 a League of Nations conference in Paris standardises the booklet —
  size, thirty-two pages, the photo page, the visa pages — and in 1922 issues the Nansen
  passport for people whose state no longer exists → the emergency of July 1914 is now the
  most-owned document on earth.
- **Lander.** The booklet in the drawer — the photograph, the description, the visa pages
  — is a temporary wartime measure from the crisis in this chapter, and the queue at the
  airport is the measure still being enforced.
- **Who tells it.** 0 of 11 classrooms connect the modern passport to the war. 2 of 11
  describe the unchecked crossing that starts it (US, IT); 0 of 11 remark on it.
- **Receipts-lite.** UK Aliens Restriction Act, 5 August 1914 (passed the day Britain
  entered the war); British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914; League of Nations
  Conference on Passports, Customs Formalities and Through Tickets, Paris, October 1920 —
  the proceedings are online; Nansen passport, 1922. <!-- CHECK: Aliens Restriction Act received royal assent 5 Aug 1914; the 1920 Paris conference set the 15.5×10.5 cm, 32-page booklet with photograph; ICAO 9303 descends from it -->
- **Why it fits.** It answers a question the ladder raises and drops — *how did they get
  the guns in?* — with an object every reader in every corpus country owns or wants. It
  feeds ch. 5 directly (the League’s first practical act is standardising the document),
  and it keeps the register at “look what connects,” not “look what they hid.”

### Pitch 2: The timetable that could not be stopped *(my candidate — the braid to ch. 3)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Three of the five columns explain the German war plan by
  Russian slowness and none says what made Russia slow: Chubaryan’s Schlieffen Plan
  “proceeded from the fact that Russia was weak” (RU, p. 32); OpenStax’s “rested on the
  belief that Russia would be slow to mobilize” (US, p. 437); Belarus’s Germany striking
  west “while slow-moving Russia had not deployed its armed forces in the east” (BY, p. 89).
  Lowe prints the mechanism and leaves it as a theory: A. J. P. Taylor’s plans “based on
  precise railway timetables … reduced almost to nil the time available for negotiation,”
  and Moltke refusing to switch fronts because there was “no time to change all the railway
  timetables to send the troop trains to Russia instead of to Belgium” (UK, pp. 16–17).
- **Loop.** After 1905 French capital pays for Russian strategic railways aimed at the
  German frontier, because a faster Russia is France’s insurance → every year those lines
  open, the German staff’s two-front plan gets less plausible, which is what “the sooner
  the better” means in Moltke’s 1912 minute → in July 1914 the blank cheque is a bet on a
  closing window → and mobilisation, being a timetable rather than a threat, cannot be
  paused halfway, which is why the corpus’s one disputed date is the Russian mobilisation.
- **Lander.** The brake is still on the ground. Russian-gauge track is 1,520 mm and
  European track is 1,435 mm, so freight still stops at the Polish–Belarusian and
  Polish–Ukrainian border to change bogies or transship — the reason grain and aid trains
  queue at Rava-Ruska and Małaszewicze today is a nineteenth-century decision about how
  fast an army could arrive.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 names the railway timetable as a cause (UK, quoting Taylor);
  3 of 11 rest the German plan on Russian slowness (RU, US, BY); 0 of 11 say what set the
  speed.
- **Receipts-lite.** Franco-Russian loans and the Grand Programme of 1913–14; the Russian
  1,520 mm gauge and the break-of-gauge at Brest; Moltke’s “war the sooner the better,”
  quoted in Lowe from Admiral von Müller’s diary of the 8 December 1912 meeting.
  <!-- CHECK: Russian 5-foot (1,524→1,520 mm) gauge standard from the 1840s; French loans financing Russian strategic railway construction 1890s–1914; Müller diary, "Kriegsrat" 8 Dec 1912 -->
- **Caveat.** The lander is strong for readers in RU/BY/UA/IN/ES and weaker in
  UK/US/CN/IT/SD. If it wins, the lander has to be the timetable itself, not the gauge.

### Pitch 3: Two spellings of one cheque *(linguistic; the corpus’s own shared word)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** The only phrase two independent classrooms quote verbatim
  about July 1914 is a banking instrument, and each keeps it in scare quotes in its own
  orthography. Lowe: “This was like giving the Austrians a blank cheque to do whatever they
  wanted” (UK, p. 14). OpenStax: “Germany then extended a ‘blank check’ to Austria-Hungary,
  asking for no details about what Austria-Hungary was planning but essentially agreed to
  back it no matter what” (US, p. 437). Both books then use the phrase as a cause: Lowe’s
  Fischer blames Germany “because they risked a major war by sending the ‘blank cheque’.”
- **Loop.** A cheque with the amount left blank is an ordinary commercial object and an
  extraordinary risk → the metaphor attaches to one telegram of 6 July 1914 and never
  detaches → the historiography then argues about the war by arguing about the metaphor,
  which is what Lowe’s §1.4 is doing on the page → and the phrase leaves the archive for
  general political speech, where “giving X a blank cheque” is now the standard charge
  against any unconditional backing, in every corpus language.
- **Lander.** The word is in the reader’s mouth. It is also, for most readers under forty,
  the only cheque they will ever hold — the instrument is nearly dead and the metaphor is
  in this week’s newspaper.
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 print the phrase (UK, US), in two spellings; 0 of 11 print the
  document behind it.
- **Receipts-lite.** Wilhelm II’s assurance to Count Hoyos, 5 July 1914; Bethmann-Hollweg’s
  telegram to Ambassador Tschirschky, 6 July 1914 — both in the published German documents
  of 1919 (Kautsky’s *Deutsche Dokumente zum Kriegsausbruch*). <!-- CHECK: Hoyos mission 5 July; Bethmann telegram 6 July 1914; Kautsky documents published 1919 -->
- **Honest caveat, must be in the text if this wins.** “Blank cheque” as a metaphor for
  unconditional licence is older than 1914. The claim is narrower and still true: 1914 is
  what fixed it as the name of a specific diplomatic act, and it is the one piece of
  vocabulary a British and an American classroom hand a student in identical words.

### Pitch 4: Bread from the shell factory *(bank if it doesn’t win here)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** OpenStax states the expectation and never explains why it
  failed: “Other countries also believed in 1914 that even if a war broke out that summer,
  the troops would be home by Christmas” (US, p. 438). Sabbatucci–Vidotto call the whole
  July decision-making a “sottovalutazione della guerra.” No column on the ladder says what
  a long war needed that a short one did not.
- **Loop.** Shells need nitrates, and in 1914 the world’s nitrate came out of the Atacama
  desert by ship → the British blockade cuts Germany off from Chile within months, and on
  the pre-war arithmetic Germany runs out of explosives in 1915 → Haber’s ammonia process,
  scaled by Bosch at Oppau from 1913, is redirected to nitric acid, and Germany fights for
  three more years on nitrogen taken out of the air → after 1918 the same plants make
  fertiliser, and world food production is rebuilt on it.
- **Lander.** A large share of the nitrogen in a living human body today has passed through
  that reaction. The bread on the table is the war’s longest-running product.
- **Who tells it.** 0 of 11 classrooms join the blockade, the process and the length of
  the war. <!-- CHECK: count against the corpus before this line ships -->
- **Receipts-lite.** Haber patent 1908–10; BASF Oppau ammonia plant on stream September
  1913; the Saltpetre Works Programme (Salpeterprogramm) of 1915; Haber’s Nobel Prize,
  awarded 1918. <!-- CHECK: Oppau opened 9 Sept 1913; Haber awarded the 1918 chemistry Nobel in 1919 -->
- **Why it is fourth.** It answers *why so long*, and this chapter’s question is *which
  move made it start*. Strong material hinge, wrong beat — it probably belongs to ch. 5 or
  to a Great War interlude.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (the passport)** — it grows out of a sentence already on the ladder, the lander is
in a drawer in every corpus country, and it hands ch. 5 the League of Nations doing its
first useful thing. **Pitch 2 (the timetable)** second, and first if the braid back to ch. 3
matters more than the object: it is the only candidate that makes the corpus’s one
disagreement about a date — 29 vs 30 July — into the point rather than a footnote.
**Pitch 3** is the cheapest to write and the easiest to overclaim; it survives only with the
caveat printed. **Pitch 4** should be banked whatever happens here.
