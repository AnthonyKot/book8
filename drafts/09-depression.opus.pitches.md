### Pitch 1: The coffee in the firebox *(my candidate — recommended, non-obvious)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Two columns name the commodity and neither follows it. Russia lists what was already unsellable before the crash: «Мировой рынок сельскохозяйственных продуктов: пшеницы, сахара, кофе, каучука, шерсти, жиров — был переполнен уже в 1928 г.» — "The world market in agricultural products: wheat, sugar, coffee, rubber, wool, fats — was already overstocked in 1928" (RU, p. 77). Italy prints the measurement that sorts the victims: manufactures down 30 per cent, raw materials down 26, and farm prices down "more than 50 per cent" (IT, p. 372) — and, in its Latin America section, says the crisis hit a whole continent by "making the prices of raw materials and foodstuffs collapse" (IT, p. 460). Belarus gets one sentence further than anyone: the crisis caused "a sharp reduction in demand for agricultural and raw-material production… In Brazil and in Cuba revolutions occurred" (BY, p. 139). Nobody says what was done with the crop.
- **Loop.** A decade of over-planting plus a Brazilian price-support scheme leaves the world holding more coffee than it can drink → American demand collapses and American banks call in foreign loans, so Brazil can neither sell the coffee nor borrow against it → to hold the price up the state buys the crop and destroys it: roughly 78 million 60-kilo bags burned, dumped at sea, or shovelled into locomotive fireboxes between 1931 and 1944, while men queue at soup kitchens in Chicago → the coffee economy that had run Brazilian politics for forty years breaks, and in October 1930 Vargas takes power → the "we grow one thing and sell it to them" model is the thing the 1930s discredits, from Brazil to the Gezira.
- **Lander.** Coffee. It is on the table in all eleven corpus countries, and the price a grower gets is still set on a futures exchange rather than by the grower. The most famous photograph of the Depression is a queue for free food; the same years were burning food to keep its price up, on another continent, for the same reason.
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 11 classrooms name a farm commodity at all in the crisis (RU's overstock list, SD's cotton, BY's "agricultural and raw-material production"); 1 of 11 (IT) prints the figures showing farm prices fell furthest; 1 of 11 (BY) mentions the Brazilian revolution; 0 of 11 mention what happened to the crop, and 0 of 11 note that the Depression's deepest price falls were outside every country on the ladder.
- **Receipts-lite.** Brazil's Departamento Nacional do Café, created 1933; the destruction figures are in the Brazilian coffee institute's own records and in any standard history of the trade; Vargas takes power 3 November 1930. <!-- CHECK: ~78.2 million bags of coffee destroyed by Brazil 1931–44, burned, dumped at sea and used as locomotive fuel; coffee ~70% of Brazilian export earnings in 1929; DNC founded 1933; Vargas assumed power 3 Nov 1930 -->
- **Why it fits.** It answers "who does the crash happen to?" — which is this chapter's real question — with a place none of the eleven look, and it does it with an object rather than a lesson. It also pays off the ladder's shape: the further you get from Wall Street, the more concrete the story becomes, and Brazil is off the end of the ladder entirely.

### Pitch 2: The exam question Belarus never answers *(my candidate)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Belarus ends its Depression section with homework: «Почему он не затронул экономику Советского государства?» — "Why did it not affect the economy of the Soviet state?" (BY, p. 124). The book does not answer it. Italy states the same fact as a parenthesis — the recession spread everywhere "with the significant exception of the USSR" (IT, p. 372) — and also does not explain it. Russia devotes two sections to the crisis and never asks.
- **Loop.** The USSR was outside the world capital market, so there was nothing in Moscow to crash → but the First Five-Year Plan needed machinery and engineers the USSR could not build, and from 1929 the firms that made them were desperate for orders → Ford contracts to build the Nizhny Novgorod car plant; Albert Kahn's Detroit office opens a Moscow bureau and designs hundreds of Soviet factories; the Magnitogorsk works is laid out by a Cleveland engineering firm on the model of Gary, Indiana → the USSR pays in grain and timber sold into precisely the collapsed world prices this chapter is about, during the years of the famine → by 1933 the plants are running and the salesmen have gone home.
- **Precise punchline (causal care).** The Depression did not industrialise the Soviet Union — the Plan was written before the crash and would have been carried out anyway. What the Depression changed was the price. It put the West's industrial capacity on sale to the one customer with no stock market.
- **Lander.** The GAZ badge. The first car mass-produced in the Soviet Union, the GAZ-A, is a Ford Model A built under a 1929 licence in a plant Ford helped design — a Detroit car with Cyrillic on the paperwork, and the ancestor of every Volga a reader in Minsk, Moscow, Kyiv or Almaty has ridden in.
- **Who tells it.** 3 of 11 raise the Soviet exception (RU, BY, IT); 1 of 11 makes it a graded question (BY); 0 of 11 answer it. Note the asymmetry worth naming out loud: the two books that most insist the crisis was a crisis of capitalism as a system are also the two that decline to say what their own side was buying from it.
- **Receipts-lite.** Ford–VSNKh agreement, 31 May 1929; the Nizhny Novgorod plant opens January 1932; Albert Kahn Associates' Moscow bureau, 1930–32. <!-- CHECK: Ford agreement 31 May 1929; GAZ plant opened 1 January 1932; Albert Kahn Inc. contracted 1929/1930 to design 500+ Soviet industrial plants, Moscow office under Moritz Kahn; Magnitogorsk blast furnaces designed by Arthur G. McKee & Co. of Cleveland on the Gary, Indiana model -->
- **Risk.** Needs a cold hand near the famine sentence, and the lander is strong for four corpus countries and weaker for the rest.

### Pitch 3: The tariff word *(the obvious seed, developed)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** OpenStax hands the world consequence to a named American statute: "Smoot-Hawley was the highest tariff the U.S. government has ever enacted. It helped stifle world trade, which decreased 30 percent by the early 1930s" (US, p. 492). Italy says the same thing about the state rather than the law — the USA, "rather than taking on the responsibilities attached to the role of hegemonic power… sought above all to defend their own production by hardening protectionism" (IT, p. 372). Lowe supplies the mechanics from the other end and dates the first move to 1922 (UK, pp. 490–491, the Fordney–McCumber tariff).
- **Loop.** A tariff passed to protect American farmers invites retaliation → every trading state defends its own balance the same way, and the value of world trade falls by roughly two-thirds in three years → the depression exports itself through the customs house rather than the stock exchange → the men who design the postwar order in 1944–47 treat that spiral as the thing to be engineered against, and write GATT: twenty-three countries, thirty October 1947, a treaty whose entire purpose is to stop 1930 happening again → GATT becomes the WTO, and the argument is running again today in every country in this corpus.
- **Lander.** The word "tariff," which was a dead technical term for two generations and is now back on the news in all eleven countries, and the price tag on anything imported. The 1930 Act is the reason there is an international body to argue in front of at all.
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 name Smoot-Hawley (US, UK); 1 of 11 blames American protectionism as a choice by a hegemonic power (IT); 0 of 11 connect the tariff spiral to the postwar trade order they teach in a later unit.
- **Receipts-lite.** Smoot–Hawley signed 17 June 1930 over a petition by more than a thousand economists; GATT signed 30 October 1947; WTO from 1 January 1995. <!-- CHECK: 1,028 economists petitioned Hoover to veto, May 1930; GATT signed 30 Oct 1947 by 23 contracting parties, provisional application 1 Jan 1948 -->
- **Note.** This braids forward to ch. 14 (the 1945–49 order) more cleanly than it braids into ch. 9. If ch. 14 wants it, bank it there.

### Pitch 4: Two payrolls in the same office *(my candidate — the ladder's own bottom rung)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Sudan's ministry textbook tells the Depression with no crash in it, and then prints a comparison no other column has any use for: "the direct cause for the strike was the reduction of graduates' salaries, due to the global economic crisis. But the government did not reduce the salaries of the British and the foreigners" (SD, p. 99; reconstructed Arabic — read against the printed page). The cause is on p. 80: Gezira cotton made 40 per cent of government income and two-thirds of exports, demand fell, "and the government resorted to strict economic measures, so it reduced employment and salaries."
- **Loop.** The Sennar dam and the Gezira scheme turn the land between the Blue and White Niles into one crop grown for one market → the colonial budget becomes a bet on the Liverpool cotton price → the price collapses with everything else in 1929–31 → the administration balances the books by cutting Sudanese graduates from £8 to £5½ and leaving British and foreign salaries alone → the Gordon College students strike in 1931 under Makki al-Manna, and the government has to send two Sudanese notables to negotiate → that graduate cohort becomes the Graduates' General Congress in 1938 and the political class of independence in 1956.
- **Precise punchline (causal care).** The Depression did not create the two payrolls. It made the difference between them a published number, and put it in front of the only people in Sudan trained to read a budget.
- **Lander.** Cotton. The Gezira scheme is still there, still one of the largest irrigation systems in the world under a single management, still growing cotton; the difference between what the crop earns and what the grower gets is still the argument. And a salary scale with two rates in one office is the most portable object in this chapter — every corpus country has a version of it.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms tells the Depression as a decision about whose pay to cut (SD). 8 of 11 tell it as a stock exchange. 0 of 11 outside Khartoum mention Sudan, cotton, or any colonial budget in connection with 1929.
- **Receipts-lite.** Sennar dam completed 1925; the Gezira scheme run by the Sudan Plantations Syndicate; Gordon Memorial College strike 1931; Graduates' General Congress founded 1938; independence 1 January 1956. <!-- CHECK: Sennar Dam completed 1925, Gezira Scheme irrigation from 1925; Sudan Plantations Syndicate; Graduates' General Congress founded 1938; Sudan independent 1 January 1956; the SD book dates nationalisation of the scheme to 1951 -->
- **Risk.** It is the chapter's most distant column carrying the thread, which is right for the argument but means the reader meets Sudan twice in one chapter. Would need the tell trimmed if this wins.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (coffee)** first. It is the only candidate whose lander is on the reader's table in
every corpus country, it answers the chapter's actual question — who does the crash happen
to — with a place none of the eleven look, and it lands the "burning food while people queue
for food" recognition without any sneer available to it. It also has three independent seeds
in three different books, which is what the missing-piece rule wants.

**Pitch 2 (the Soviet exception)** is the strongest analytically and the best fit for the
comparison itself: a graded exam question in one corpus book that the corpus cannot answer.
Take it if the user reacts more to the argument than to the object.

**Pitch 4** is the cleanest causal loop of the four and the most humane; take it if the
reaction to Pitch 1 is lukewarm. **Pitch 3** is the most obvious and should probably be
banked for ch. 14 rather than spent here.
