The 1945–1949 order

On 5 March 1946, in the gymnasium of Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, Winston Churchill steps to the microphone under American flags. Two years later, red wooden barriers turn back every truck and train on the Autobahn leading into West Berlin. What six classrooms do with the four years between Yalta and the founding of NATO is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who froze Europe into two camps — and whose grammar makes the other side move first?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • February 4–11, 1945: Allied leaders (Stalin, Roosevelt, Churchill) meet at Yalta in the Crimea to outline postwar European occupation zones, Polish borders, and the voting structure of the United Nations.
  • April 25 – June 26, 1945: Delegates from 51 nations meet in San Francisco to draft the United Nations Charter, which enters into force on October 24, 1945.
  • July 17 – August 2, 1945: Allied leaders (Stalin, Truman, Churchill, later Attlee) meet at Potsdam to establish principles for occupied Germany: demilitarization, denazification, democratization, and decartelization.
  • March 5, 1946: Winston Churchill delivers his "Sinews of Peace" address at Fulton, Missouri, declaring that "an iron curtain has descended across the Continent."
  • March 12, 1947: US President Harry S. Truman requests $400 million in aid for Greece and Turkey, establishing the "Truman Doctrine" of containment against communist expansion.
  • June 5, 1947: US Secretary of State George C. Marshall proposes the European Recovery Program (Marshall Plan); between 1948 and 1952, over $12–13 billion in US assistance is distributed to Western European states.
  • June 24, 1948 – May 12, 1949: The Soviet Union halts ground, rail, and canal traffic into West Berlin; Western allies supply the city via the Berlin Airlift, transporting over 2 million tons of cargo.
  • April 4, 1949: Twelve nations sign the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington, establishing NATO. In May and October 1949, the Federal Republic of Germany (FRG) and the German Democratic Republic (GDR) are proclaimed.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who cover the 1945–1949 European settlement, ordered from thinnest Western agency (a system taking shape as American leverage and British speeches) to thickest Soviet agency (interfering, expanding, and starving a city). China and India focus on national civil war and decolonization; Germany's booklet covers National Socialism; Sudan covers regional Arab history. Those omissions are syllabus structure, not avoidance. Kazakhstan's volume is national history and is never a world-event column here.

3 The tell

Every textbook covering these four years agrees on the map of 1949: two German states, a divided Europe, and a military alliance anchored in Washington. The disagreement is about whose grammar turns a wartime alliance into a frozen frontier.

Belarus opens with a passive noun: the Yalta-Potsdam system took shape. Nobody built it; it crystallized. When agency enters the sentence, it belongs to the West. Churchill's Fulton address is a "warlike speech" that "laid the beginning of the Cold War." The Marshall Plan is not economic reconstruction; it is a mechanism carrying "political demands" that handed the United States "powerful levers of influence." NATO is an active military-political block formed in 1949; the Warsaw Pact is a defensive response formed six years later. The Berlin blockade—the actual military standoff of 1948—is erased entirely. The division of Europe happens through American leverage and British speeches.

Russia takes a different path to the same destination: it stops the clock. The 10th-grade volume records Yalta, Potsdam, and the 51 founding states of the United Nations in San Francisco as cooperative achievements of the anti-Hitler coalition. Ideological disagreements are acknowledged as "predetermined," but the narrative ends in 1945. The Fulton speech, the Marshall Plan, the Berlin blockade, and NATO do not appear. By ending the book at the peace settlement, the textbook preserves Yalta and Potsdam as monument rather than breakdown.

«Попередньою умовою надання допомоги було усунення комуністів зі складу урядів. До 1948 р. комуністів у жодному уряді країн Західної Європи не стало.»

“A preliminary condition for providing aid was the removal of communists from the composition of governments. By 1948 there were no communists left in any government of Western Europe.”

UA · Гісем/Мартинюк, Всесвітня історія (2019) · p. 17

Ukraine's volume is the only textbook in the set that constructs a dual grammar. The USA and the USSR "divided" the world between the two of them. It quotes Stalin directly on territorial control—that whoever occupies a territory forces his social system upon it—while detailing the economic conditions of the Marshall Plan. Marshall Aid ($13 billion, or $125 billion in modern prices) required the expulsion of communist ministers from Western European cabinets. The Berlin blockade is a Soviet action ("having received a refusal, the Soviet occupation administration began a blockade"), and the airlift is a Western response (2.3 million tons transported). Neither side is passive, and neither side is exempt.

Italy, the United States, and the United Kingdom move progressively deeper into active Soviet culpability. Sabbatucci–Vidotto locate the split at Potsdam, where a "Stalinist design of subjection" emerged in Eastern Europe, framing the Berlin blockade as a Soviet "trial of strength." OpenStax presents the Truman Doctrine as a pledge to help "free peoples" resist communist expansion, while explaining the Berlin blockade as an emotional reaction: "Infuriated, the Soviet Union cut off all ground routes." Lowe writes the thickest grammar of intent: the Russians "systematically interfered" in Eastern Europe, and their goal in blocking West Berlin was "reducing it to starvation point." In the British classroom, NATO is not an offensive lever; it is the natural consequence of a West "frightened into making definite preparations."

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Гісем/Мартинюк (UA) earns the credit line for this chapter. It is the only volume in the set that narrates both mechanisms of the division with equal structural clarity: it quotes Stalin on occupation forces imposing social systems, while explicitly laying out the political prerequisite of the Marshall Plan—that Western European cabinets remove communist ministers before receiving American aid. It measures the physical outcome on both sides: 13 billion dollars poured in, 2.3 million tons airlifted into Berlin, and zero communists left in Western cabinets by 1948.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns a secondary nod for conceptual precision. It names Walter Lippmann's coinage of "cold war" in real time, acknowledges the economic mechanics of the European Recovery Program, and frames the Berlin blockade as a calculated "trial of strength" without erasing Western policy choices in currency reform.

Lowe (UK) and OpenStax (US) provide essential narrative depth for Western diplomatic reasoning, but both lean heavily on defensive grammar—treating NATO as a spontaneous reaction to Soviet fright rather than part of an ongoing contest over European alignment.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder reflects six textbooks that structure the 1945–1949 postwar settlement as a discrete international relations unit. It is not the entire globe. China's state textbook devotes these years entirely to the Chinese Civil War and the founding of the People's Republic of China; India's NCERT moves directly from Indian independence and partition to mid-century decolonization; Germany's federal booklet is a specialized primer on National Socialism and the Holocaust; Sudan's secondary curriculum focuses on Arab national history and the 1948 Palestine conflict. Those absences are curriculum boundaries, not national avoidance.

Kazakhstan's secondary history volume (Койгелдиев / Кабульдинов 2021) covers 1945–1949 as lived national history within the USSR—documenting postwar industrial reconstruction, the expansion of the Gulag system, and the first Soviet atomic bomb test at Semipalatinsk in August 1949. Per this project's rules, national history volumes are kept off world-event ladders; Kazakhstan's republic-level experience belongs to signature topics in republic memory.

Russia's 10th-grade survey ends chronologically in 1945 with the Yalta, Potsdam, and San Francisco agreements. The subsequent breakdown into NATO and the Berlin blockade is carried in the 11th-grade volume (Мединский/Чубарьян 2023), which was unavailable in the core extraction set. The finding here is structural: the 10th-grade book presents the 1945 settlement as a clean victory coda before the textbook closes.
