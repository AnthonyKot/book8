Munich → Pact → September 1939

On September 30, 1938, Neville Chamberlain steps off a Lockheed Electra at Heston Aerodrome, waving a piece of paper bearing Hitler's signature to cheering crowds. Eleven months later, on August 23, 1939, Joachim von Ribbentrop and Vyacheslav Molotov shake hands under a portrait of Lenin in Moscow. Eight days after that, Luftwaffe bombers appear over Wieluń. What six national classrooms do with those three dates is the subject of this chapter.

The question · causality

What caused the war — and what does your textbook need Munich and the Pact to be?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • September 29–30, 1938: The Munich Agreement is signed by Britain (Chamberlain), France (Daladier), Germany (Hitler), and Italy (Mussolini), forcing Czechoslovakia to cede the Sudetenland to Germany by October 10 without Czechoslovak representatives present.
  • March 15, 1939: German Wehrmacht troops occupy Prague, Bohemia, and Moravia, violating the Munich Agreement and destroying the rump Czechoslovak state.
  • March 31, 1939: Great Britain and France issue formal military guarantees to defend Polish independence in the event of aggression.
  • August 23, 1939: The Soviet-German Treaty of Non-Aggression (Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact) is signed in Moscow, accompanied by a secret protocol partitioning Eastern Europe into spheres of influence.
  • September 1, 1939: German forces launch a full-scale invasion of Poland (Operation Weiss); Britain and France declare war on Germany on September 3, 1939.
  • September 17, 1939: Red Army forces cross the eastern border of Poland.
  • September 28, 1939: Germany and the Soviet Union sign the Treaty of Friendship and Border, establishing a joint frontier along the Curzon Line.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who cover the outbreak of World War II, ordered from thinnest agency (an unnamed order received by marching troops) to thickest (explicit state directives to march in and annex under a secret pact, dual-sided invasion, and military destruction). Books outside the period or syllabus scope—Ukraine 1945–2018, Sudan's national syllabus, China's state-compiled history volume, and Kazakhstan's national history edition—are absent from the ladder rows. India's NCERT textbook covers the 1933–1945 timeline but omits Munich, the Pact, and the 1939 invasion entirely, an avoidance signal against the corpus baseline.

3 The tell

Every national textbook in this set agrees on three chronological anchors: a four-power conference in Munich in September 1938, a Soviet-German non-aggression pact signed in Moscow in August 1939, and military operations in Poland in September 1939. What they dispute—violently, in sentence structure and cause-and-effect arrows—is what caused what.

Act I: Munich 1938 — Appeasement versus Collusion

In British, American, and Italian classrooms, Munich is the climax of appeasement (*appeasement*, *politica di appeasement*). Norman Lowe's UK text defines it as the policy of "avoiding war with aggressive powers... by giving way to their demands, provided they were not too unreasonable." OpenStax records that Britain and France granted territorial concessions in the hope that Hitler "would be satisfied and cease to be aggressive." Sabbatucci–Vidotto in Italy describe a strategy of attempting to "tame" Hitler by satisfying his "reasonable" demands to compensate for Versailles. Both Western accounts highlight domestic relief: Chamberlain returning to London declaring "peace for our time," greeted by cheering crowds, alongside Winston Churchill's sharp dissent: "They could choose between dishonor and war, they have chosen dishonor and will have war."

«Мюнхенский сговор стал синонимом предательства и позора, венцом беспринципных уступок со стороны Англии и Франции.»

“The Munich collusion became synonymous with betrayal and disgrace, the crown of unprincipled concessions on the part of England and France.”

RU · Чубарьян (2023) · p. 109

In Moscow and Minsk, Munich is given a different noun: «Мюнхенский сговор»—the *Munich collusion*. Russia's state textbook frames Munich as "the crown of unprincipled concessions" that gave German aggression international legal cover. Both Russian and Belarusian volumes stress what Western books leave out: that the Soviet Union offered military assistance to defend Czechoslovakia in 1938, but was turned down. Koshelev's Belarusian textbook asserts that Soviet offers were rejected "because of fear of the 'red danger'," and charges that London and Paris "secretly hoped to direct the point of German aggression to the east, against the Soviet Union." In this framing, Munich was not an honest blunder by tired peacemakers; it was a deliberate Western effort to steer Nazi expansion toward Moscow.

Germany's federal agency booklet (bpb) takes a position distinct from both. It shows Hitler instructing Sudeten leader Konrad Henlein in March 1938 to make "unacceptable demands" to ensure the crisis could not be defused through compromise. It acknowledges that Chamberlain was greeted in London as a politician of peace, but delivers a stark verdict: "Sacrificed in the process is Czechoslovakia."

Act II: The Non-Aggression Pact — Forced Step versus Joint Division

When the story moves to August 23, 1939, the causal arrows flip. In Western and German textbooks, the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact is the trigger that made war inevitable. Germany's bpb booklet marks August 23 as a critical date representing "the joint division, exploitation, and oppression of Europe by National Socialist Germany and the Stalinist Soviet Union." OpenStax and Lowe detail the secret protocols that divided Poland and Eastern Europe into spheres of influence, enabling Hitler to invade Poland without fearing a two-front war.

Russia's textbook presents the exact inverse logic: Munich *caused* the Pact. Having watched Britain and France abandon Czechoslovakia and undermine collective security, the Soviet Union faced isolation. The non-aggression pact is characterized as a "forced step on the part of the USSR," with the division of spheres initiated by Germany. Belarus reinforces this defense, noting that the pact bought the Soviet state "almost two years of respite" to prepare for war, while Western democracies became "victims of their own policy of 'appeasement'."

Italy's manual captures the global shock of August 23, calling the alliance between ideologically opposed regimes "one of the greatest theatrical plot twists in the history of diplomacy," born of "unprincipled realism" (*spregiudicato realismo*) that offered immediate territorial advantages to both dictatorships.

Act III: September 1939 — Liberation versus Invasion

The final act reveals the deepest grammatical division in the corpus: the events of September 17, 1939. On September 1, Germany's Wehrmacht attacked Poland, preceded by Luftwaffe bombing of Wieluń—an assault detailed in Germany's bpb volume with dive-bombers and siren screams. Sixteen days later, Soviet forces entered eastern Poland.

In British, American, and Italian textbooks, this entry is described using active invasion verbs: "the Russians invaded eastern Poland" (UK), "Soviet forces invaded Poland from the east" (US), and "seized the eastern regions of the country" (IT). OpenStax summarizes the outcome: "Crushed from two sides, Poland essentially ceased to exist." Germany's bpb booklet names the institutional actor explicitly: "the Soviet leadership gave the order to march into Eastern Poland according to the pact concluded with Hitler and to annex this territory."

In Russia's textbook, the order-giver vanishes into passive grammar: Soviet troops crossed the border "having received the order to proceed to the liberation of Western Ukraine and Western Belorussia." The entry is framed as recovering territories lost under the 1921 Treaty of Riga after the Polish state had ceased functioning. Belarus goes further: the Red Army's entry is titled a "liberation campaign" that fulfilled the historic "reunification of the Belarusian people." In 2021, Minsk codified this narrative into law, designating September 17 as an official public holiday—National Unity Day.

In the occupied territories, German forces established brutal administrative zones while Soviet authorities interned captured Polish officers and state officials.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

bpb (DE) earns the credit lead for rigorous self-incrimination. It does not soften German responsibility, detailing how Hitler manufactured the Sudeten crisis, opened the war with terror bombing at Wieluń, and signed a secret additional protocol with Moscow for the explicit division and oppression of Europe.

Lowe (UK) deserves recognition for material precision regarding Munich. While many surveys treat Munich as an abstract diplomatic failure, Lowe quantifies the concrete cost forced onto Czechoslovakia: the loss of 70 percent of its heavy industry, one-third of its population, one-third of its territory, and its entire border fortification system.

Koshelev (BY) and Чубарьян (RU) merit credit for preserving diplomatic sequence. They maintain visibility on the 1938–1939 tripartite negotiations, reminding students that Western governments repeatedly turned down Soviet collective security proposals prior to Munich and August 1939 out of anti-communist suspicion.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns a nod for diplomatic candor, acknowledging that the Italian peace proposal presented by Mussolini at Munich was in fact written verbatim by the German Foreign Office in Berlin.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder compares six national history textbooks in our corpus that include World War II's diplomatic origin within their standard curriculum. It reflects approved state or market-dominant editions in these jurisdictions, not every textbook published.

Four volumes in our corpus are out of scope for this episode: Ukraine's 11th-grade volume covers 1945–2018; Sudan's secondary history focuses on 1821–1948 national history; China's state volume (*纲要 上*) covers domestic Chinese history from antiquity to 2017; and Kazakhstan's 11th-grade textbook is a national-history edition tracking Kazakh history rather than European world history.

India's NCERT 11th-grade textbook (*Themes in World History*) is in-scope and covers the 1933–1945 timeline, but contains zero mentions of the Munich Agreement, the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, or the September 1939 invasion of Poland. Under our corpus rules, this shared omission across 544 pages represents a clear avoidance signal.

Two findings retained because errors are data: Belarus's textbook compresses colonial expansion by listing Hong Kong and Singapore as seized together during the Opium War, and OpenStax compresses the complex multi-stage Polish campaign into a brief two-sentence summary that minimizes the operational details of the eastern front.
