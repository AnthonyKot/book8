Korea — the finale

Before dawn on June 25, 1950, artillery opens fire across a line drawn five years earlier with a razor blade and a National Geographic map. By nightfall, armored columns are heading south toward Seoul. What seven national classrooms do with that morning—and the three years of destruction that followed—is the full thesis of this book in a single war.

The question · agency

Who started it — and what word does your textbook need for the side that moved first?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • August 10–11, 1945: The United States and USSR agree to divide joint occupation of the Korean Peninsula at the 38th parallel following Japanese surrender.
  • 1948: Two separate states are proclaimed: the Republic of Korea (ROK) in Seoul under Syngman Rhee, and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK) in Pyongyang under Kim Il-sung.
  • June 25, 1950: North Korean forces (Korean People's Army) cross the 38th parallel into South Korea; Seoul falls within three days.
  • June 27, 1950: UN Security Council passes Resolution 83 authorizing military assistance to South Korea (USSR boycotting Security Council over PRC representation).
  • September 15, 1950: US-led UN forces under General Douglas MacArthur execute the Incheon amphibious landing, recapturing Seoul and advancing north across the 38th parallel toward the Yalu River.
  • October 19, 1950: Chinese People's Volunteers (CPV) cross the Yalu River into Korea, forcing UN troops into a long retreat south of the 38th parallel.
  • July 27, 1953: The Korean Armistice Agreement is signed at Panmunjom, establishing the Korean Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) near the 38th parallel without a formal peace treaty.
  • Casualties: Military and civilian deaths total an estimated 3 million people, mostly Korean civilians, leaving urban and industrial infrastructure devastated across the peninsula.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators who cover the Korean War, ordered from thinnest agency (passive global backdrop) to thickest (named commanders, explicit warnings, and strategic initiatives). Books outside the 1950–53 period—Russia's core 1914–45 textbook, Germany's NS volume, Sudan's 1821–1948 syllabus, and Kazakhstan's national history edition—are absent from the ladder rows. That absence marks syllabus scope, not a historical verdict.

3 The tell

Every classroom in this set agrees that fighting raged across the Korean Peninsula between June 1950 and July 1953, leaving millions dead and cities shattered. The divide is entirely grammatical: who acts in the opening sentence, and what name is given to the army that crossed the line.

Belarus and India occupy the agentless pole of the ladder. In Minsk's state text, the Korean War is stripped of all campaign narrative and reduced to a line item in a list of third-world local conflicts that "flared up" («вспыхивали») while superpowers fought "indirectly rather than directly." For New Delhi's NCERT textbook, the war "broke out" in June 1950 and evolved into a "vintage proxy war" whose primary significance was disrupting South Korea's economic development and delaying democratization.

China's state textbook constructs the war as a two-act drama. Act One opens not with an invasion, but with internal conflict: "On June 25, 1950, the Korean civil war broke out" («朝鲜内战爆发»). Act Two introduces foreign agency, but exclusively from Washington: the United States "manipulated" the UN Security Council, crossed the 38th parallel, and "invaded Korea" («侵略朝鲜»). Chinese military action is framed as an unavoidable defensive reaction: the Chinese People's Volunteers enter "upon the request of the Korean government" to "resist US aggression, aid Korea, protect the home, and defend the country" («抗美援朝，保家卫国»). The official war title—War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea—serves as the verdict. Margin notes quote Commander Peng Dehuai declaring that the victory proved the era of Western imperialists installing cannons on Eastern shores was "gone forever."

（抗美援朝战争的胜利）雄辩地证明：西方侵略者几百年来只要在东方一个海岸上架起几尊大炮就可霸占一个国家的时代是一去不复返了。

“(The victory of the War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea) eloquently proves that the era when Western aggressors could occupy a country just by setting up a few cannons on a coast in the East is gone forever.”

CN · Commander Peng Dehuai · quoted in 纲要 上 p. 175 sidebar

Ukraine provides a crucial structural hinge. It acknowledges the underlying symmetry of ambition—noting that "both regimes sought to unite the Korean Peninsula under their power"—before explicitly identifying who launched the offensive: "North Korean troops crossed the 38th parallel and began a war." Ukraine also records the stark reality of the 1953 resolution: "the parties are still in a state of war" («сторони досі перебувають у стані війни»).

«Хоча його вдалося зупинити, сторони досі перебувають у стані війни.»

“Although it was possible to stop it, the parties are still in a state of war.”

UA · Hisem & Martyniuk, Всесвітня історія, 11 кл. (2019) · p. 172

Italy and the Anglo-American textbooks move to explicit military agency. Sabbatucci–Vidotto emphasize that North Korean forces were "armed by the Soviets" when they "invaded the South," while noting that US forces acted "under the flag of the UN." Norman Lowe's UK text opens with "Without warning, North Korean troops invaded South Korea" and proceeds to lay out four distinct historical interpretations of the war's origin (Kim Il-sung's initiative, Dean Acheson's perimeter speech, Soviet approval, and communist claims of South Korean provocation).

The US volume (OpenStax) reaches the peak of agentive detail. It names the specific attacking force ("Korean People's Army"), notes their mistaken assumption of being "confident of welcome," and offers remarkable historiographical candor regarding Moscow: while Washington suspected a Soviet masterplan, "Stalin had in fact warned Kim against it" and "the Soviet Union sent nothing," forcing Kim to turn to Mao instead. Where Beijing sees US imperial aggression and London sees a multi-causal crisis, OpenStax details a local Korean gamble that drew hesitant superpowers into a three-year slaughter.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Hisem & Martyniuk (UA) earns the credit lead for analytical balance. It opens its Korean section by stating that both Kim Il-sung's northern regime and Syngman Rhee's southern regime sought to unite the peninsula under their own authority, establishing the dual domestic drive for unification before identifying that North Korea crossed the 38th parallel first.

OpenStax (US) deserves high credit for historiographical candor regarding Soviet reluctance. Rather than asserting a monolithic Cold War conspiracy directed from Moscow, OpenStax explicitly informs students that Stalin warned Kim Il-sung against invading the South and refused to send Soviet ground forces, dismantling a durable Western myth.

Lowe (UK) merits credit for historiographical breadth. It presents four competing hypotheses for how the war began—including Kim's initiative, Acheson's speech omission, Stalin's material backing, and communist counter-claims—giving students a masterclass in evaluating historical causation.

纲要 (CN) receives credit for detailed military perspective and primary source integration. By printing Commander Peng Dehuai's report alongside campaign maps, it gives students the exact anti-imperialist lens through which Beijing viewed its intervention in 1950.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This comparison covers seven national textbooks that include the 1950–53 Korean War in their world-history curriculum. Four volumes in our eleven-country corpus are absent from the ladder due to volume boundaries: Russia's unified 10th-grade state textbook stops at 1945; Germany's BPB volume focuses on National Socialism (1939–1945); Sudan's secondary text covers 1821–1948.

Kazakhstan's 11th-grade textbook (*История Казахстана*) covers national history rather than world history. Under our corpus rules, KZ is never a peer column on world-event ladders. However, KZ contains a signature topic of profound relevance to Korean history: the forced deportation of over 170,000 Far Eastern Koreans (Koryo-saram) to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan under Stalin in 1937. That story lives in Kazakh national memory, not in the Korean War chapter.

While Russia's core 10th-grade history textbook ends in 1945, Russia's official visual-aid companion for 11th grade (*ru-aid.txt*, Poster 21 answer key) provides the bare state label «Корейская война» (Korean War) under Stalin's leadership span (1950–1953). That entry is recorded below as a visual-aid receipt, not a textbook narrative.
