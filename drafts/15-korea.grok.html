Korea

Before dawn on 25 June 1950, artillery opens along a line drawn on a map five years earlier — the 38th parallel, a temporary occupation boundary that had become a frontier between two Korean states. Within days Seoul falls. Within months Chinese troops cross the Yalu. Three years later the front is nearly where it began. What seven classrooms do with the sentence that makes that war start is the subject of this chapter — and the book’s full agency ladder in one event.

The question · agency

Who started it — and what word does your textbook need for the side that moved first?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • August 1945: after Japan’s surrender, Korea is divided for occupation along the 38th parallel — Soviet forces north, US forces south.
  • 1948: separate governments form — Republic of Korea (South, Syngman Rhee) and Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (North, Kim Il-sung). Occupation troops later withdraw.
  • 25 June 1950: war begins on the peninsula. Seoul falls within days; UN Security Council resolutions authorize a multinational force under US command (Soviet delegate absent).
  • September 1950: UN/US landing at Incheon (Inchon) reverses the northern advance; UN forces cross the 38th parallel northward.
  • October 1950: Chinese People’s Volunteers enter the war; fighting pushes south again, then stabilizes near the parallel.
  • 27 July 1953: Korean Armistice Agreement signed (often associated with Panmunjom). No final peace treaty follows; a Demilitarized Zone roughly follows the front.
  • Scholarly casualty ranges for the war run in the millions (soldiers and civilians); national textbooks disagree on the figure and on whose dead count.

2 The ladder

Seven narrators who cover the 1950–53 war, ordered from most agentless (war as a list entry or a thing that “broke out”) to most agentive (named army, named invasion, named external armers and restrainers). Russia’s grade volume in this corpus ends in 1945, so it has no war chapter; its visual-aid companion posts the bare label «Корейская война» and is logged in the receipts, not as a peer row. Kazakhstan is a national-history book, never a world-event ladder column. Germany’s NS booklet and Sudan’s syllabus are out of chronological scope.

3 The tell

Nobody in this set denies that a war was fought on the Korean peninsula from 1950 to 1953, or that the front ended near the 38th parallel. The disagreement is about the sentence that makes the war start — who holds the verb.

Belarus is the agentless pole. The Korean War appears as one item in a list of “hot” wars in Asia, Africa, and Latin America, then again as a timeline label paired with Vietnam (“Wars in Korea / 1950—1953, 1964—1973”). No parallel is crossed. No capital falls. The USA and USSR “supported the opposing sides” and fought “indirectly” — superpower framing without a first mover on the ground. India keeps a short outbreak clause with the same grammar of weather: the war “broke out.” Support arrives for both sides; the war becomes a “vintage proxy war”; the armistice leaves Korea divided. The next sentences shift to South Korean inflation, destroyed industry, US aid, and Syngman Rhee’s illegal extensions of power. The war is a delay of free-market development and democratisation, not a campaign narrative.

China’s lesson reassigns the verb. First the “Korean civil war broke out” — no northern army, no southern army. Then the United States “manipulated” the Security Council, formed a “United Nations Force” in scare quotes, crossed the “38th Parallel” in scare quotes, and “invaded Korea.” Chinese entry is request, duty, and title: the People’s Volunteers go “to resist US aggression, aid Korea, protect the home, and defend the country” — 抗美援朝. The armistice is not a mutual ceasefire; the United States was “forced to sign.” The victory coda is national: the Chinese people won the War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea; the war “forged national prestige and military prestige.” War-title-as-verdict is the Chinese classroom’s load-bearing device. The same page that softens Korean agency hardens American agency until the labels do the moral work.

Ukraine opens with both Korean governments seeking unification — a rare admission that ambition was shared — then names who moved: North Korean troops “crossed the 38th parallel and began a war.” South fights under the UN flag with US and allied forces; North has Chinese troops and Soviet military advisors. Elsewhere the same book records that PRC troops entered on 25 October 1950 and that Kim Il-sung’s government was saved “at the cost of lives of 1 million Chinese soldiers,” and later that although the fighting could be stopped, “the parties are still in a state of war.” Italy compresses the agentive Western consensus into one clause: North Korean forces, “armed by the Soviets,” “invaded the South.” The UN authorization (Soviet seat empty) and Mao’s “volunteers” in scare quotes complete the Italian frame.

The UK and US books sit at the agentive end, but they do different jobs with the surplus. Lowe leads with “Without warning” — surprise as moral coloring — then stages an open origin debate (Kim’s idea; Acheson’s Pacific perimeter omission; Chinese encouragement; Stalin; a communist claim that the South started it) and settles on Kim pressing unification with Soviet and Chinese material approval but no desire for direct involvement. He alone among these seven stages Inchon, Pusan, the Yalu, Panmunjom, MacArthur’s atomic-bomb argument and removal, and a Korean death toll of “about four million” with five million homeless. OpenStax names the Korean People’s Army, dates the fall of Seoul within two days, and adds a claim the others do not: Stalin had “warned Kim against it,” redirected him toward Mao, and “the Soviet Union sent nothing.” US agency is the air-and-naval dispatch; Soviet agency is absence. The war is later called “futile,” with “approximately three million” dead, “most of them Korean civilians.” Same invasion sentence; different auxiliary clauses about Moscow.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Гісем–Мартинюк (UA) earns the credit lead for this event. Before the invasion sentence, it states that both governments wanted the peninsula under their power. That two-beat structure — shared ambition, then who crossed the line — is the cleanest agency setup in the set. The later note that the parties remain “in a state of war” also keeps the armistice from pretending to be a peace.

Lowe (UK) deserves the depth credit. It alone among these classroom voices stages competing origin hypotheses, names the operational geography (Inchon, Pusan, Chinese “volunteers,” Panmunjom), and puts Korean dead and homeless on the page as a disaster for Korea itself, not only as a Cold War case study.

OpenStax (US) earns a nod for an awkward candor the others skip: Stalin’s warning against the attack and the claim that Moscow “sent nothing” while Washington moved air and naval forces. Whether that reading of Soviet policy holds is a historian’s fight; the classroom move is to complicate the “armed by the Soviets” shorthand without erasing who invaded.

纲要 (CN) is the vocabulary lead, not the fairness lead. No other book in this ladder builds a full national war-name (抗美援朝), a scare-quoted UN force, a forced signature, and a victory coda into one continuous lesson. Depth of national framing is not the same as distance from the actor.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is seven books that place the 1950–53 war inside a general world-history (or university survey) frame. It is not every textbook published in these countries — only the pinned editions in the corpus.

Russia’s Чубарьян volume covers 1914–1945; the 1950–53 war falls outside its covers. The Russian visual-aid companion (answer-key / poster set) carries the bare label «Корейская война» on a Cold War events table — a naming receipt, not a narrative peer. Germany’s BPB booklet is a National Socialism and Holocaust primer; Sudan’s secondary history ends before the Cold War’s hot wars. Those zeros are syllabus boundaries, not avoidance of Korea measured against books that never reach 1950.

Kazakhstan’s 11th-grade volume is national history of Kazakhstan, not a world-history column. It does not narrate the 1950–53 war. What it does carry — and what no other book in this corpus treats as a signature topic — is the 1937 forced relocation of Far Eastern Koreans to Kazakhstan (and the opening of a Korean theater at Kzyl-Orda the same year). Under corpus rules that story belongs in limits and receipts, never as a peer ladder cell for a world event.

Machine-translated discovery files pointed to pages; the Chinese, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Italian, and Russian-aid lines above are taken from original extractions and rendered in English for the reader. Before hard claims ship, the printed PDF pages should be opened again — especially CN page composition (sidebar Peng Dehuai quote vs main narrative) and BY’s timeline pairing of Korea with 1964–1973.

None of these classrooms is a full military history of the campaigns. If you want hills, logistics, and day-by-day orders of battle, every column will leave you unsatisfied.
