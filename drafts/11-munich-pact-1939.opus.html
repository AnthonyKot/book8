Same War, Different Classroom
Chapters Method

Part IV · Deals & War · Chapter 11

Inherits: Versailles (ch. 5) · Feeds: Holocaust (ch. 12)

Munich → Pact → September 1939

On 30 September 1938, at an airfield outside London, a sixty-nine-year-old prime minister steps off a plane holding a sheet of paper above his head. Photographers get the shot. Eleven months later, two armies enter Poland from opposite directions, three weeks apart, under an agreement whose second half is not published. Every textbook in this chapter has the same dates. This chapter is about what they do with the arrows between them.

The question · causality

What caused the war — and what does your textbook need Munich and the Pact to be for that answer to hold?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement. Nothing here is disputed by any book in the corpus.

  • 12 March 1938: German troops enter Austria; the Anschluss is announced the next day.
  • 29–30 September 1938: Chamberlain (UK), Daladier (France), Hitler (Germany) and Mussolini (Italy) meet at Munich and sign an agreement transferring the Sudetenland to Germany. Czechoslovakia is not at the table; the USSR is not invited. Transfer runs 1–10 October.
  • 15 March 1939: German troops occupy Bohemia and Moravia; Slovakia becomes a separate state under German protection.
  • 31 March 1939: Britain publicly guarantees Poland; France follows. Anglo-French–Soviet military talks in Moscow run through August without agreement.
  • 23 August 1939: Ribbentrop and Molotov sign a non-aggression treaty in Moscow with a secret additional protocol dividing spheres of interest in eastern Europe.
  • 1 September 1939: Germany invades Poland. 3 September: Britain and France declare war on Germany, and not on the USSR.
  • 17 September 1939: the Red Army crosses into eastern Poland. The Polish government leaves for Romania the same day.
  • 28 September 1939: a German–Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty fixes the line between them. Organised Polish resistance ends in early October.

2 Act I · Munich, 1938

Six classrooms cover Munich. They agree on the room, the four signatures and the missing Czechs. They disagree about what the room was for.

Norman Lowe’s British textbook is the only one in the corpus that stops the narrative to define the vocabulary. Appeasement gets its own numbered section and a dictionary entry: “Appeasement was the policy followed by the British, and later by the French, of avoiding war with aggressive powers such as Japan, Italy and Germany, by giving way to their demands, provided they were not too unreasonable.” The Munich narrative that follows is unsparing about the mechanics — the four-power plan “produced by Mussolini (but actually written by the German Foreign Office),” and then: “Neither the Czechs nor the Russians were invited to the conference. The Czechs were told that if they resisted the Munich decision, they would receive no help from Britain or France.” The bill arrives with a figure attached: Czechoslovakia lost “70 per cent of her heavy industry, a third of her population, roughly a third of her territory and almost all her carefully prepared fortifications.” No other book in the corpus prices Munich.

OpenStax is shorter and flatter, and one verb does a great deal of work: “On September 30, they produced the Munich Pact, in which Czechoslovakia granted territorial concessions to Germany, Poland, and Hungary in what has since been called appeasement.” Granted is the grammar of a country that was not in the building. The American book’s real interest arrives two pages later, when Munich stops being an event and becomes a tool: using it “to warn against the perils of allowing one nation to invade another without opposition, whether it be Hitler’s Germany or Putin’s Russia, is known as invoking the Munich Analogy.” This is a 2022 textbook teaching 1938 as a rhetorical move students will meet in the news.

The Italian manual has the same events with a different centre of gravity, because one of the four men at the table is theirs. Sabbatucci and Vidotto record that Hitler accepted a meeting of the European heads of government “(Russia excluded), launched in extremis by Mussolini on the suggestion of Chamberlain himself,” and that at Munich, Chamberlain and Daladier «accettarono un progetto presentato dall’Italia che in realtà accoglieva quasi alla lettera le richieste tedesche» — “accepted a project presented by Italy which in reality took up the German demands almost to the letter.” The Czechoslovaks “had not been admitted to the conference and not even consulted”; the Soviets, “also kept outside the negotiating table,” drew the conclusion that they could not count on the Western powers and abandoned the policy of alliance with the democracies. Munich, in this telling, is where the road to August 1939 is paved. Churchill supplies the epitaph, in Italian: «Potevano scegliere fra il disonore e la guerra, hanno scelto il disonore e avranno la guerra» — “They could choose between dishonour and war; they have chosen dishonour and will have war.”

The German civic-education booklet does something no other book here attempts: it declines the easy verdict on the British and then hands out a German one. It records that the Munich Agreement and the appeasement policy «sind im Nachhinein als der Ausdruck einer verantwortungslosen und allzu nachgiebigen Politik gegenüber den Nationalsozialisten bewertet worden» — “have been evaluated in hindsight as the expression of an irresponsible and overly lenient policy toward the National Socialists” — and immediately notes that neither French nor British majorities would have backed a war, and that «Chamberlain wurde bei seiner Rückkehr in London mit großem Beifall als europäischer Friedenspolitiker begrüßt», “on his return to London Chamberlain was greeted with great applause as a European politician of peace.” Then the booklet turns the responsibility inward: at least as much blame for the missed chance to stop Nazi expansion, it says, belonged to the officers around Ludwig Beck, who had planned a coup for the event of war over the Sudetenland and cancelled their preparations once Munich removed the pretext. «Eine erfolgversprechende Möglichkeit, Hitler zu stürzen, war damit vertan.» — “A promising opportunity to overthrow Hitler was thereby squandered.”

«Мюнхенский сговор стал синонимом предательства и позора, венцом беспринципных уступок со стороны Англии и Франции.»

“The Munich collusion became a synonym for betrayal and disgrace, the crown of unprincipled concessions on the part of England and France.”

RU · Чубарьян, Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл. (Просвещение 2023) · extract p. 108

Russia’s state textbook is the only book in the corpus that does not call the event an agreement or a conference in its heading. Section 4 is titled «Чехословацкий кризис. Мюнхенский сговор 1938 г.» — the Czechoslovak crisis, the Munich collusion. The word is stronger than “deal” and weaker than “conspiracy”; it means an arrangement reached behind someone’s back. The account is precise about who signed, notes that Poland and Hungary “also took part in the division,” and grants Britain and France a motive: they “took this step in an attempt to avoid war with Germany.” It ends the section elsewhere, in the failure of collective security: in 1938 the USSR “was ready to render assistance to Czechoslovakia,” but France, “following in the wake of British policy, did not fulfil the conditions of the Franco-Czechoslovak mutual assistance treaty.”

Belarus arrives at the same place by a different road and says the quiet part in plain type. The ruling circles of England and France, Koshelev’s textbook explains, took a course of “appeasement” of Hitler, and «Кроме того, они втайне надеялись направить острие немецкой агрессии на восток, против Советского Союза» — “in addition, they secretly hoped to direct the point of German aggression to the east, against the Soviet Union.” A Soviet offer of help to Czechoslovakia “was rejected because of fear of the ‘red danger’.” The verdict is one line: «Так Англия и Франция предали страну, которую были обязаны защищать.» — “Thus England and France betrayed a country they were obliged to defend.”

3 Act II · The Pact, 23 August 1939

Now the same six books have to explain a document signed by two states that had spent six years calling each other the main enemy. Here the chapter’s question becomes visible: a book’s Munich determines what its Pact is allowed to be.

«Der 23. August 1939 markiert daher ein besonderes Datum: die gemeinsame Aufteilung, Ausbeutung und Unterdrückung Europas durch das nationalsozialistische Deutschland und die stalinistische Sowjetunion.»

“23 August 1939 therefore marks a special date: the joint division, exploitation and oppression of Europe by National Socialist Germany and the Stalinist Soviet Union.”

DE · bpb, Informationen zur politischen Bildung Nr. 316, „Nationalsozialismus: Krieg und Holocaust“ (2012) · p. 13

The German booklet prints the treaty text and the secret protocol side by side on the page, and states what the protocol provided for: «die Zerschlagung Polens und die Aufteilung des Landes zwischen Deutschland und der Sowjetunion» — “the smashing of Poland and the division of the country between Germany and the Soviet Union.” It then places 1939 in a series: after the partitions of the late eighteenth century, Poland, which had finally won its independence in 1919, «wurde … nun erneut von seinen Nachbarländern einverleibt» — “was now once again incorporated by its neighbouring countries.” One sentence, two centuries, no comfort for anybody.

Russia’s account of the same signature is the most carefully engineered passage in this chapter. The sequence is: British and French guarantees, deadlocked Moscow talks in which, the book says, the participants felt deep mistrust and suspicion toward one another, a German offer at the height of those talks, and a Soviet acceptance. Then two flat declarative sentences do the load-bearing: «Пакт о ненападении был вынужденным шагом со стороны СССР» — “the non-aggression pact was a forced step on the part of the USSR” — and, after the list of territories placed in the Soviet sphere, «Разграничение сфер интересов было инициировано Германией», “the delimitation of spheres of interest was initiated by Germany.” The secret protocol is not hidden and not defended; its authorship is assigned. And the textbook then does something that deserves noting: it prints a box telling students that historians disagree, that many “approve” the treaty and the protocol given the concrete historical situation, and that «Некоторые осуждают подписание этих документов, считая, что они нанесли политический и моральный ущерб СССР» — “some condemn the signing of these documents, considering that they inflicted political and moral damage on the USSR.”

Belarus goes further into the argument and further into the sources. Koshelev’s text explains that German diplomacy threatened to deal with London against Moscow if refused, that «политика „умиротворения“ подталкивала Сталина к подписанию договора с Германией» — “the policy of ‘appeasement’ pushed Stalin toward signing a treaty with Germany” — and that Hitler “proposed to divide Poland and the rest of Eastern Europe between Germany and the USSR.” The pact’s value is stated as a calculation: the Soviet leadership, in no doubt about a coming clash, «получило почти два года передышки», “received almost two years of breathing space,” to prepare. Two pages later the book prints Stalin’s radio address of 3 July 1941 — «Мы обеспечили нашей стране мир в течение полутора годов», “we secured our country peace for a year and a half” — next to a passage from Churchill’s war memoirs, in the textbook’s own Russian rendering, conceding that if Soviet policy in 1939 «и была холодно расчетливой, то она была также в тот момент в высокой степени реалистичной» — “was coldly calculating, it was also at that moment realistic in the highest degree” — and asks the student to compare the two assessments and explain why they differ.

The British book, at the far end of Europe, reports that same defence as a live historiographical position: “Russian historians justify the pact on the grounds that it gave the USSR time to prepare its defences against a possible German attack.” Lowe’s section 5.6 is titled “Why did war break out? Were Hitler or the appeasers to blame?” and answers it four times, with named historians on each side — Bullock, Charmley, Roberts, Self, Gilbert, Trevor-Roper, Taylor — including Richard Overy’s reminder that “war in 1939 was declared by Britain and France on Germany, and not the other way round.” The Italian manual, for its part, calls the announcement of an accord between two ideologically opposed states «uno dei più grandi colpi di scena nella storia della diplomazia di ogni tempo» — “one of the greatest coups de théâtre in the history of diplomacy of all time” — received across the world «con un misto di stupore e di indignazione», “with a mixture of astonishment and indignation,” and reads it as «un gesto di spregiudicato realismo», an act of unscrupulous realism, profitable to both signatories.

One detail separates the books that read the protocol from the books that summarise it. Belarus lists the German sphere first and correctly — «„Сферой интересов“ Германии были признаны Западная и Центральная Польша, Литва», “Western and Central Poland and Lithuania were recognised as Germany’s ‘sphere of interest’.” OpenStax writes that “Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, and parts of eastern Poland were allocated to the USSR,” and Russia’s textbook writes «Прибалтика», the Baltic region, undivided. In the document signed on 23 August, Lithuania was on the German side of the line; it moved into the Soviet sphere only in the treaty of 28 September. The state textbook of Minsk is the precise one here, and the American and Russian books make the same compression in opposite political directions.

4 Act III · The ladder — one moment, 17 September 1939

Every book above gives 1 September the same subject: Germany. The disagreement is sixteen days later. On 17 September 1939 the Red Army crossed into eastern Poland. Six narrators describe that single morning, ordered from the most agentless telling (nobody crosses anything) to the most agentive (a named leadership issues an order with a stated purpose). Books whose syllabus does not reach the event — Ukraine 1945–2018, China’s national survey, Sudan’s regional history, Kazakhstan’s national volume — are absent by scope, not by verdict.

5 The tell

Read the six rows for their verbs and the ladder resolves into a single question: who is the subject of the sentence, and what is its object?

In the Belarusian row nothing crosses anything. The grammatical subject is «освободительный поход» — a liberation campaign — and its main verb is «начавшийся», “which began.” Campaigns that begin, rather than armies that are sent, do not require a sender. Poland is present only as a time-marker in the previous clause (“in the period of the German campaign in Poland”), never as a place entered. The two objects in the passage are abstract nouns of gain: reunification, and a state holiday. This is the corpus’s agentless pole, and it is not clumsiness; it is a complete, self-consistent sentence about an event with no one in it.

Russia’s row restores an actor and a border — troops cross — and then removes the one participant a reader would ask about. «Получив приказ» is an adverbial participle: having received the order. Orders are received in this sentence; nobody gives one. Compare the same book on 1 September, two pages earlier, where agency is dense and specific: Hitler’s desire, the “Weiss” plan under development since April, the raid at 4:30 a.m., the frontal advance at 4:45. The grammar knows perfectly well how to name a decision-maker. On 17 September it declines to. The clause that follows performs the other half of the work: the territories are ones “which had passed to it under the Riga Treaty of 1921,” which converts a border crossing into a reversal of an earlier one.

Then the three market books, in ascending order of exposure. Lowe puts the invasion in a subordinate clause — “When the Russians invaded eastern Poland, resistance collapsed” — so that the sentence’s grammatical business is Polish resistance, and Soviet action is the circumstance in which it fails. Sabbatucci and Vidotto make the Russians the subject of a main clause and attach the legal instrument: they seized the eastern regions “on the basis of the secret clauses.” OpenStax names the act and its result in two sentences and adds, a paragraph earlier, the asymmetry that most books leave for the reader to notice: Britain and France declared war on Germany “but not on the Soviet Union.”

The German booklet is the most agentive row, and its extra content is a purpose. It names the order-giver by office — «die sowjetische Führung» — the instrument (“in accordance with the pact concluded with Hitler”), and the intent: «dieses Gebiet zu annektieren», to annex this territory. It is the only row in which the invasion has a stated goal rather than a stated beneficiary.

«Начало военных действий против Польши было представлено как оборона страны. Само слово „война“ не упоминалось, в выступлении Гитлера 1 сентября говорилось об „обеспечении безопасности Третьего рейха и его прав“.»

“The beginning of military operations against Poland was presented as a defence of the country. The word ‘war’ itself was not mentioned; Hitler’s speech of 1 September spoke of ‘securing the safety of the Third Reich and its rights’.”

RU · Чубарьян (2023) · Gleiwitz box · extract p. 158

That box is the sharpest passage about language in this entire chapter, and it belongs to the Russian textbook. It walks students through Operation “Canned Goods” in full: the provocateurs who “seized” a German radio station ten kilometres from the Polish border, the broadcast in Polish, the body of a murdered prisoner left in Polish uniform, twelve more bodies in the woods nearby. Then it names the mechanism — an attack presented as a defence, with the word war withheld. Sixteen days and one page later, the same book will describe a border crossing as the receipt of an order to begin a liberation. Both things are true of the same textbook, and the second is not evidence that the first was insincere. It is evidence that the analysis of naming is easiest to teach about somebody else.

The wiring diagrams. Same nodes — Versailles, Munich, collective security, the Pact, 1 September, 17 September. Only the arrows move.

  • RU — Versailles crisis → Munich collusion → collective security fails (France defaults on Czechoslovakia; Moscow talks deadlock) → the Pact as a forced step, spheres initiated by Germany → German war on 1 September → 17 September as a consequence of the war already begun.
  • BY — Versailles injustice → “appeasement” aimed at turning German aggression east → betrayal of Czechoslovakia → appeasement pushes Stalin toward Berlin → the Pact buys almost two years → the western democracies become “victims of their own policy.”
  • UK — Hitler’s aims and/or the appeasers’ concessions, argued four ways with named historians → Munich as the supreme act of appeasement → the guarantee to Poland → the Pact removes the last restraint → Britain and France declare war.
  • US — Hitler gathers “the German people” → Munich Pact → its violation teaches the lesson → guarantees to Poland → Nonaggression Pact with secret protocols → invasion, war, and a permanent analogy.
  • IT — appeasement as an attempt to “tame” Hitler and compensate Versailles → Munich destroys collective security and Western credibility → Moscow draws the conclusion → the Pact as unscrupulous realism → war as a replay of 1914’s stakes.
  • DE — Hitler’s instruction to Henlein and the 30 May order to “smash” Czechoslovakia → Munich as a war postponed and a German coup abandoned → 23 August as joint division → the Wehrmacht marches on 1 September → the Soviet order to annex on 17 September.

Two books have the arrow from the Pact pointing backwards, into Munich. Two have it pointing forward, into Poland. All six draw it.

One book in the corpus draws no arrow here at all and is the one that kept the word: on the same NCERT page that gave chapter 1 its “triangular trade,” Indian students read that the eighteenth-century partition of Poland so preoccupied Chinese reformers that “by the late 1890s it came to be used as a verb: ‘to Poland us’ (bolan wo)” — four decades before September 1939 partitioned Poland a fourth time.

One count belongs inside this chapter rather than on its ladder: what happened to the Polish officers who ended up on the Soviet side of the line drawn on 28 September.

6 The thread · Munich, still in service

Five of the six books close Munich when the tanks move: an agreement, a violation, a verdict, and on to September. The American one keeps walking, two pages past the event, and the sentence it walks into is why this chapter is an argument and not a date.

“The lessons learned from Hitler’s violation of the Munich Pact spurred Britain and France to take action to protect Poland. They have also been invoked by world leaders ever since, whenever the aggression of one nation threatens the sovereignty or the territorial integrity of another. Using the example of Munich to warn against the perils of allowing one nation to invade another without opposition, whether it be Hitler’s Germany or Putin’s Russia, is known as invoking the Munich Analogy.” US · OpenStax, World History, Volume 2: from 1400 (2022) · pp. 531–532 (the sentence runs across the page break)

That is a textbook handing over a piece of equipment. Munich stops being a room in Bavaria and becomes a move available in an argument about a war happening this year — and the equipment has a service history. In June 1950, on the flight back to Washington after the first reports from Korea, Harry Truman was thinking about the 1930s: Manchuria, Ethiopia, Austria, and what had followed from letting each one pass. He wrote it down six years later in the second volume of his memoirs, Years of Trial and Hope. From there “no more Munichs” kept turning up in the mouths of people who wanted opposite things — over Suez in 1956, over Vietnam through the 1960s, and in every decade since.

Set the current editions side by side and the loop closes. Three days in 1938; three lessons; all of them in print now. In the American classroom Munich is a transferable warning, and the 2022 edition aims it at Moscow. In the Russian classroom the same three days are «сговор» — the arrangement made behind Prague’s back and over Moscow’s head, “a synonym for betrayal and disgrace,” which is also the grievance the same book will draw on eleven months later when the Pact becomes “a forced step.” In the Belarusian classroom that betrayal does heavier work still: appeasement “pushed Stalin toward signing a treaty with Germany,” so Munich is the thing the Pact is an answer to. One node, and the arrows leaving it run in opposite directions — a warning against making deals with an aggressor, and an explanation of why a deal with an aggressor had to be made. Both are being taught to sixteen-year-olds in the same school year.

This is the rare thread that reaches the reader without a map or a museum, because the word got out of the classroom. Anyone who has followed an argument about a current war has heard someone called an appeaser, and heard someone else answer that Munich is a bad analogy for this. The word walks in already loaded. Which way it is loaded — a warning about the aggressor, or a charge against the West — was settled years earlier, in whichever classroom the speaker sat in, and neither speaker is quoting anything they were not taught.

Who teaches the analogy. Search all eleven books for Munich: six carry the conference; five have no trace of it. Of the six, two of eleven teach Munich as something transferable rather than as an event with a verdict attached. OpenStax (US) names the instrument — “the Munich Analogy” — and points it at the present. Lowe (UK) never names it and shows it working twice, decades downstream: on Truman’s decision over Korea, “Some Americans saw the invasion as similar to Hitler’s policies during the 1930s. Appeasement of the aggressors had failed then, and therefore it was essential not to make the same mistake again”; and on Suez, where Hugh Gaitskell “agreed that Nasser must not be appeased in the way that Hitler and Mussolini had been appeased in the 1930s.” The other four books — Russia, Belarus, Germany, Italy — argue hard about what Munich caused, and end it in 1939.

7 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) takes the credit lead for this chapter, and takes it on method. Section 5.6 does not answer “who caused the war”; it stages the answer four times — the appeasers, the USSR, Hitler, and A. J. P. Taylor’s dissent that Hitler expected at most a short war with Poland — attaches historians’ names and publication dates to each, and prints Richard Overy’s inconvenient line that “war in 1939 was declared by Britain and France on Germany, and not the other way round.” It is the only book in the corpus that hands a schoolchild the argument rather than the verdict, and the only one that reports its rivals’ case in their own terms, including the Russian defence of the Pact.

bpb (DE) earns the self-incrimination credit twice over. It refuses the comfortable reading of appeasement — recording the applause for Chamberlain and the state of British rearmament — and then relocates the blame for the missed chance onto German officers who called off their coup once Munich removed its pretext. On 23 August it uses the word gemeinsame, joint, for the division and oppression of Europe, and on 17 September it names a Soviet order and the word annex. A booklet published by a German federal agency is under no obligation to be exact about Soviet agency; it is exact anyway.

Koshelev (BY) deserves the pedagogy credit and a factual one. Its “causes of the policy of appeasement” section gives three of them — the memory of 1914–18 among electorates, conservative fear of communism, and the novelty of a diplomacy that ignored the convention that signatures bind — which is a better account of 1938 than most one-line verdicts. It then prints Stalin’s 1941 radio defence of the Pact beside Churchill’s memoir passage and asks students to compare them and to explain why the assessments differ. And it is the only book here that reproduces the 23 August spheres correctly, Lithuania included.

Чубарьян (RU) is credited for the Gleiwitz box. It is the most detailed account in the corpus of how a state renames an attack, down to the withheld word, and it is the only place a student in any of these six classrooms is taught that vocabulary is a weapon system. The book also declines to hide the secret protocol and prints the historians’ disagreement about the Pact, including those who hold it damaged the USSR politically and morally.

8 Where this stops being true

Limits

Six books cover this chapter’s events. Four do not, and their zeros are syllabus, not avoidance: Ukraine’s 11th-grade volume begins in 1945 (its only two hits for «Мюнхен» are a university in Munich and the 1972 Olympics), China’s state survey is a Chinese-history course with no 慕尼黑 or 波兰 at all, Sudan’s secondary history stays with the Nile valley and Palestine, and Kazakhstan’s volume is national history and is never a peer column in this book.

India is the exception that counts. NCERT’s Themes in World History is a world-history course covering the period, and it returns zero hits for Munich, Sudetenland, appeasement, Chamberlain, Molotov and Ribbentrop; the Second World War appears in the timeline as “Second World War (1939–45).” Under the corpus rule — six of seven in-scope narrators cover a topic — that is an avoidance signal rather than a curriculum norm. What it avoids is Europe, not war.

Three findings are kept verbatim because errors are data. Lowe dates the secret agreement to divide Poland to 24 August, where the other five books and the document itself say 23 August — defensible, since the signing ran past midnight in Moscow, and worth a student’s attention either way. OpenStax places Lithuania in the Soviet sphere as of 23 August, which the protocol of that date does not. And in the Belarusian extraction the sentence “this day in Belarus became a state holiday” sits immediately after a sentence about the Baltic republics rather than after the 17 September sentence it refers to; that may be a column-layout artifact of the extraction rather than the printed page, and the printed page should be checked before the point is pressed.

Machine translation was used for discovery only. Every non-English line above was pulled from the original-language extraction, joined across line breaks and hyphenation, and hand-translated here. Page numbers follow the extractions and the dossiers; before any of these quotes hardens into a claim, the printed page should be reopened. The German booklet is set in three columns and its Wieluń pages are a reprinted newspaper article by Joachim Trenkner (Die Zeit, 1 September 2009), not the agency’s own prose — quoted here only for the invasion timeline, never as the booklet’s voice.

None of these classrooms is a military history of the September campaign, and none of them is a legal history of the protocol. If you want the order of battle, or the text of the 28 September maps, you will leave all six unsatisfied.
