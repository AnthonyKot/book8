### Pitch (a): The half-hour border — staged candidate, refined

- **Seed (a silence, found by the ladder).** All seven books draw the line and
  none says who drew it. Every one uses a passive or an agreement: UK — Korea
  "had been under Japanese occupation… the USA and the USSR agreed to divide the
  country into two zones along the 38th parallel" and, later, "the artificial
  division forced on their country by outsiders" (Lowe 2013, p. 142). US — Korea
  "was divided in half at the thirty-eighth parallel of latitude" (OpenStax
  2022, p. 583). IT — «In base agli accordi interalleati, quel paese… era stato
  diviso in due zone, delimitate dal 38° parallelo» (Laterza 2004, §22.8).
  UA — "Korea was divided along the 38th parallel into Soviet and American
  occupation zones" (2019, p. 18). IN — "temporarily divided… with the Soviets
  managing the North and the U.N. managing the South" (NCERT, p. 175).
  Grep confirms: **zero of eleven books name Dean Rusk or Charles Bonesteel.**
  The line has no author in any classroom on earth — which is exactly the same
  grammatical move the chapter has spent five pages catching in the *war*.
- **Loop.** Japan surrenders faster than Washington expected → on the night of
  10–11 August 1945, two American colonels are given thirty minutes in an
  adjoining room to find a division of Korea that keeps Seoul on the American
  side and that the Soviets might accept → working from a small-scale
  *National Geographic* wall map, with no Korea specialist in the room and no
  Korean consulted, they pick the 38th parallel because it is a round number
  above Seoul → the line goes into General Order No. 1 → Moscow accepts it
  without argument → the administrative convenience becomes two states in 1948,
  a war in 1950, and a permanent frontier in 1953. The line the textbooks
  present as a fact of geography was a thirty-minute answer to an administrative
  question. <!-- CHECK: Rusk & Bonesteel, night of 10–11 Aug 1945, State-War-Navy Coordinating Committee; Rusk's own memoir account of the National Geographic map and the ~30 minutes; General Order No. 1 -->
- **Lander (the cup of tea).** The line is still there, now as the Demilitarized
  Zone — routinely described as the most heavily fortified border in the world
  <!-- CHECK: "most heavily militarised/fortified border" characterisation of the DMZ; ~250 km long, 4 km wide -->. And the reader is holding the
  south side of it: the semiconductor and display plants that supply a large
  share of the world's phone memory and screens sit in Gyeonggi province, inside
  roughly a hundred kilometres of that 1945 pencil line
  <!-- CHECK: Samsung Giheung/Hwaseong and SK hynix Icheon fabs, distance from the DMZ; safest formulation is "under ~100 km" not "~50 km" — verify before printing a number -->. Whichever
  phone is in the reader's pocket, part of it was made within an afternoon's
  drive of a border two men improvised in half an hour.
- **Shared test — passes.** The 38th parallel is in every corpus country's
  supply chain, not its memory. Bonus receipt already in the chapter: the
  Kazakh textbook's only living Korea is Samsung taking over Kazakhmys.
- **Risk.** The "thirty minutes" and "National Geographic map" details rest
  largely on Rusk's own later account; write it as what Rusk said he did, with
  the date and the order it produced as the hard facts.

### Pitch (d): The empty chair — writer's own, added

- **Seed (a mechanism four books name and none joins up).** US — "The Security
  Council's actions could have been prevented by a veto… However, since the
  Nationalists' loss in the Chinese civil war, the United States had insisted
  that China's seat on the council belonged to Taiwan, not to the People's
  Republic of China, and the Soviet Union had boycotted the council's meetings
  in protest. It was thus unable to stop the resolution from passing"
  (OpenStax 2022, p. 583). IT — the Council acted «in assenza del delegato
  sovietico» (Laterza 2004). CN — the US <span>操纵</span> "manipulated the
  United Nations Security Council into passing a resolution" (纲要 上, p. 175).
  UA, in its China chapter, supplies the far end: the PRC's "place in the UN
  until 1971 was occupied by representatives of the Kuomintang" (2019, p. 125).
  UK supplies the verdict: China "was now clearly a world power. The fact that
  she was still not allowed a seat in the UN seemed even more unreasonable"
  (Lowe 2013, p. 146). Five books hold five pieces of one loop; no book draws it.
- **Loop.** The PRC is founded in October 1949, but China's Security Council
  seat stays with Taipei → Moscow walks out of the Council in January 1950 in
  protest and stays out → June 1950: the one veto that would have stopped
  Resolution 83 is not cast, because the chair is empty → an American-led force
  deploys legally under the UN flag → that force reaches the Yalu, China enters
  the war, and the UN's own General Assembly names the PRC an aggressor in 1951
  → the boycott staged to win China's seat is the direct reason China does not
  get it for another twenty-one years, until 1971
  <!-- CHECK: Soviet UNSC boycott Jan–Aug 1950 over PRC representation; UNSC Res. 82/83/84 June–July 1950; UNGA Res. 498 (Feb 1951) naming the PRC an aggressor; UNGA Res. 2758 (Oct 1971) seating the PRC -->. A protest about an
  empty chair produced the war it was protesting the exclusion from.
- **Lander.** The Security Council veto — the thing on the news every time a
  resolution "fails" — and the reason the rules now say an absence is not a veto.
  June 1950 is the only time in the Council's history the absent-member question
  decided a war, and it is why no permanent member has walked out since
  <!-- CHECK: no P5 member has repeated a prolonged UNSC boycott since 1950; abstention/absence practice under Art. 27(3) -->.
- **Shared test — passes.** The UN veto is a live argument in every corpus
  country, and this loop is the one where the veto's absence, not its use,
  killed the people.
- **Why it might win.** It is the best *loop* of the four — five textbooks each
  holding one link and none holding the chain is precisely the book's thesis
  about causality being visible only between chapters.

### Pitch (b): The war that never ended — staged candidate, verified

- **Seed (verified verbatim; it is in the dossier and in the original).**
  UA 2019, p. 172: «Одним із найбільш небезпечних був конфлікт у Кореї
  (1950—1953 рр.). Хоча його вдалося зупинити, **сторони досі перебувають у
  стані війни**.» — "Although it was possible to stop it, the parties are still
  in a state of war." Present tense, in a schoolbook. **One of seven.** Nobody
  else tells a student the war is unfinished: UK ends at Panmunjom with "an
  agreement that the frontier should be roughly along the 38th parallel"; US
  ends at "the Korean Armistice Agreement was signed" and a DMZ; IN at "the war
  ended in an armistice agreement"; CN at the United States "had no choice but
  to sign"; BY at a date range on a timeline.
- **Loop.** 27 July 1953 produces an *armistice* — a ceasefire between military
  commands, signed by the UN Command, the Korean People's Army and the Chinese
  People's Volunteers → South Korea's president refuses to sign it at all → the
  document's own text calls for a political conference within three months to
  settle the question; the 1954 Geneva conference on Korea ends without
  agreement → no peace treaty is ever concluded → the ceasefire line becomes the
  DMZ, and the legal state of war outlives everyone who signed
  <!-- CHECK: armistice signatories 27 July 1953; ROK/Syngman Rhee declined to sign; Art. IV para 60 (political conference within three months); Geneva Conference on Korea, Apr–Jun 1954, no settlement -->.
- **Lander.** Every conscript in South Korea today serves because of an unsigned
  peace <!-- CHECK: ROK universal male conscription, ~18 months, justified by the armistice-not-peace status -->, and every "Korea talks" headline the reader has ever
  scrolled past is an attempt to finish a document from 1953.
- **Shared test — passes, thinly.** The headline is genuinely global; the
  object is a news item rather than a thing in the reader's hand.
- **Weakness.** It is a *state* rather than a *chain* — the causal loop is short
  and mostly legal. Strong candidate for one paragraph inside another thread
  (it would sit naturally at the end of (a)) rather than as the thread itself.

### Pitch (c): Koryo-saram — staged candidate, flagged as the writer said it would be

- **Seed.** KZ 2015: «Одной из первых жертв массовой депортации народов в СССР
  стали дальневосточные корейцы» — "one of the first victims of the mass
  deportation of peoples in the USSR were the Far Eastern Koreans," deported in
  two stages from autumn 1937 to spring 1938, settled across eight Kazakh
  regions, alongside a Korean theatre opening in Kzyl-Orda in 1937. In the same
  book, the only other Korea is Samsung managing Kazakhmys in the 1990s. The war
  itself: zero mentions. This is a signature topic by the book's own rule.
- **Loop.** 1937: about 172,000 Soviet Koreans are deported from the Far East to
  Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan as suspected "Japanese spies" — thirteen years
  before the war, and for being Korean <!-- CHECK: Politburo/Sovnarkom decree of 21 Aug 1937; ~172,000 deported; "Japanese espionage" pretext --> → their descendants, the
  Koryo-saram, become a Central Asian minority with its own theatre and press →
  one of them, born in Leningrad to a Koryo-saram father, is Viktor Tsoi, whose
  band Kino wrote the song that became the anthem of the last Soviet years
  <!-- CHECK: Viktor Tsoi b. 1962 Leningrad, father Robert Tsoi of Koryo-saram descent, family deported/relocated; Kino, "Хочу перемен!" 1986–88 --> → a peninsula partitioned in
  1945 had already scattered its people across a continent by 1938.
- **Lander.** A song a very large number of post-Soviet readers know by heart,
  written by a man whose family was moved to Kazakhstan for being Korean.
- **Shared test — FAILS, and the writer flags it.** "Перемен!" lands for BY, RU,
  UA and KZ readers and is close to meaningless for UK, US, IN, IT, CN, SD, DE
  readers. AGENT.md is explicit: a hook only some nationalities feel fails the
  test. It is also *thirteen years off-topic* — the chapter's question is who
  started the 1950 war, and this thread answers a different question.
- **Disposition.** Recommend **banking it**, not writing it. It already earns
  its place in the chapter as a Limits paragraph and a receipts badge, which is
  exactly where CONTEXT.md says a KZ signature topic belongs. If a later
  chapter or the epilogue takes up deportation memory, this is its thread.
