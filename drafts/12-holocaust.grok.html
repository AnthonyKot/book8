The Holocaust

In late September 1941, in a ravine outside occupied Kyiv that the maps still call Babi Yar, German units shoot thousands of people over two days. One classroom will write that “about 70 thousand Jews were shot.” Another will write that “more than 33,000 people” were shot by SS and police. The disagreement is not only about the number. What each classroom does with the hands, the names, and the word for the dead is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who killed them — and how many hands does your textbook put on the trigger?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • From 1933, Nazi Germany stripped Jews of civil rights; the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 defined Jewish status in law.
  • 9–10 November 1938: the nationwide pogrom later called Kristallnacht / Night of Broken Glass — synagogues burned, Jewish businesses smashed, tens of thousands of Jewish men arrested and sent to concentration camps.
  • September 1939: German invasion of Poland; ghettos established; occupation regimes including the General Government under Hans Frank.
  • June 1941: German invasion of the Soviet Union (Operation Barbarossa). Mobile killing units of the SS and police (Einsatzgruppen), often with Wehrmacht support and local auxiliaries, carry out mass shootings of Jews and others behind the front.
  • 20 January 1942: Wannsee Conference in Berlin — senior officials coordinate the deportation and murder of European Jews under the bureaucratic label “Final Solution of the Jewish Question.”
  • 1941–45: killing centres and camps in occupied Poland and elsewhere — among them Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, Majdanek, Auschwitz-Birkenau — combine gas chambers, forced labour, and starvation.
  • Scholarly consensus places Jewish deaths in the Holocaust at approximately six million. Other groups murdered under Nazi racial and political policy include Roma and Sinti, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, and political opponents; those tallies are separate and also large.
  • German defeat in 1945 ends the killing system. Postwar trials, memorials, and school curricula make the event a permanent subject in some countries and a thin or missing one in others — which is what the ladder measures.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who actually cover the murder of the European Jews, ordered from thinnest agency (a body count with almost no hands) to thickest (named German institutions, multi-agency coordination, and the refusal to stop the sentence at Hitler alone). Belarus and India are in period range and silent — avoidance signals, tracked in the receipts, not as peer rows. Kazakhstan’s national volume and China’s national survey never enter this event as a world-history lesson; Sudan’s syllabus ends in 1948 without the European murder narrative. Kazakhstan’s wartime phrasing appears only in the tell as a signature national line, never as a ladder column.

3 The tell

Nobody on this ladder denies that millions of Jews were murdered. The disagreement is about the sentence that holds the hands — and, one step down, about which dead the paragraph is allowed to name when the shooting is not only Jewish.

Ukraine’s volume covers 1945–2018. The Holocaust enters once, as a number that explains something else: six million Jews “at the hands of the Nazis,” then the Zionist slogan, then the UN partition and the proclamation of Israel. The agent is a plural noun. There is no camp, no ravine, no Wannsee, no named unit. The murder is real and the murder is furniture. A second passage, on Orbán’s 2012 speech in the Knesset, condemns “the crimes of the Holocaust” and “the destruction of Jewry by the Nazis and their accomplices” — again as present-day memory politics, not as wartime sequence.

Russia’s state textbook does more work and still leaves the trigger empty. At Babi Yar the grammar is passive: about 70 thousand Jews were shot. No SS, no police battalion, no Einsatzgruppe. Two pages of “new order” later, the same book can write a “targeted policy of destruction of Jews” called the Holocaust, about six million dead, and list Auschwitz, Treblinka, Majdanek as “death factories” in which about eleven million people were destroyed — “Jews, Russians, Ukrainians, Poles, and all those whom the Nazis considered inferior.” Jews open the sentence; a multi-national camp total closes it. Elsewhere the same volume totals “Soviet citizens destroyed on occupied territory” at 13.7 million. Three ledgers share the page. They are not the same count, and the book does not say they are. The classroom effect is a stack of large numbers in which Jewish specificity is both stated and absorbed.

«Фашисты приступили к планомерному истреблению советского народа.»

“The fascists proceeded to the systematic extermination of the Soviet people.”

KZ · История Казахстана, 11 кл., 2015 · p. 201 — national-history volume, not a ladder peer; the only wartime victim category in the passage is “the Soviet people”

That Kazakhstan line is not a world-history column here. It is the cleanest version of a move the Soviet-era tradition still makes available: name the dead as a single patriotic collective. Belarus’s world-history volume, by contrast, never reaches the Jewish murder at all. Its fascism chapter records that the Nazi party programme “put forward anti-Semitic and patriotic slogans,” and then moves on to living space, race hierarchy, and partisan war. Jews appear as a slogan the Nazis used, not as a people the Nazis killed. That is not a thin account. It is a zero. Under this book’s rule — six of eight in-scope books cover the topic — the Belarusian silence is an avoidance signal, and so is India’s. NCERT’s thematic world history marks Hitler’s capture of power and the Second World War on its timeline and never opens a Jewish genocide file.

Italy builds a full sequence: ghettos, yellow star, camps, gas chambers. The agent at the top of the chain is singular. Hitler “projected and set in motion” the final solution from 1941; the SS received principal care of it; between five and six million “Israelites” “disappeared.” The verb is soft. The structure is clear: one mind, one organisation, a continent of victims. Lowe’s British survey does something the Italian manual does not: it stages a historians’ fight. Intentionalists put the plan on Hitler early; functionalists put the Final Solution on the circumstances of the war; Kershaw is quoted that Hitler’s role was “decisive and indispensable.” Under that debate the book still puts live hands on the ground — “SS units and the regular army” at Babi Yar, “some 34 000 Jews” in two days — and it keeps the Jewish noun in every key sentence. OpenStax goes further into the machine: it names the Einsatzgruppen, prints Ohlendorf’s Nuremberg testimony that Jews and commissars were to be “liquidated,” sets Wannsee as state policy, and refuses a lone-gunman picture by counting “the collaboration of tens of thousands of people from across Europe.”

Then the German booklet does the job no other column on this ladder is built to do. It is a federal civic-education text titled War and Holocaust. On page 4 it prints nearly six million Jews murdered and, in the same breath, the sentence that organises the whole chapter: the Holocaust “did not occur upon the sole decision of Hitler.” Violence radicalised in the war of annihilation against the Soviet Union; it issued in systematic deportation and murder at named extermination sites. At Babi Yar the same book puts SS and police in the active voice and adds that German soldiers participated in murder against civilians. At Wannsee it stages ministries and Nazi bureaucracy coordinating the “destruction of the Jews.” This is not a foreign survey describing German crimes. It is a German public text teaching German students which German hands were on which German triggers. That is the self-incrimination pole the spine assigned this chapter, and the quotes carry it without help from the guide’s adjectives.

One number still has to be left unsettled. Russia’s “about 70 thousand Jews” at Babi Yar and the German and British “more than 33,000” / “some 34 000” for the same ravine are not the same claim dressed in different clothes. The lower figure is the standard count for the two-day massacre of 29–30 September 1941; the higher figure is the order of magnitude often given for Jewish deaths at the site across the longer occupation. The textbooks do not explain the gap. The chapter does not close it. Errors and compressions are data; so is a spread that two different counting windows can both generate. What the ladder can say with a clean conscience is narrower: three books put a number on the same ravine, two of them put German units in the sentence, and one of them leaves the verb passive and the total nearly doubled.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

bpb (DE) takes the credit line for this event. Distance is usually the fairness engine in this book; here the distance is zero and the candour is maximal. The booklet names Einsatzgruppen, Wannsee as multi-agency coordination, Wehrmacht participation, and the refusal to treat Hitler as a sole decision-maker. No other column on the ladder puts that many German hands on the page. Genre matters: this is civic education about National Socialism, not a survey racing to 1991. Depth and self-implication arrive together, and the chapter needs both on the record.

OpenStax (US) earns the second nod for machinery without self-defence. Einsatzgruppen, Wannsee as state policy, camp names, and “tens of thousands” of collaborators across Europe are a survey’s honest maximum. It is written far from the perpetrators’ state, and it uses that distance to keep the system visible rather than the Führer alone.

Lowe (UK) deserves a third line for the only classroom that teaches the historiographical fight as part of the event: intentionalists, functionalists, and a quoted verdict on Hitler’s role. A British market textbook has no national need to clear German institutions, and it does not clear them; what it adds is the argument students will meet if they read further.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is six books that open a Jewish-genocide file. It is not “the world,” and it is not six countries speaking with one voice each — it is six pinned editions. Ukraine’s volume is a postwar syllabus that uses the Holocaust as a hinge to 1948; that is coverage, but it is not a wartime chapter. Germany’s text is a federal booklet on National Socialism, not a commercial school survey of the twentieth century. Those genre facts cut both ways: DE has room to be thorough because the booklet’s job is this subject; UA has little room because its job is the world after 1945.

Kazakhstan’s “systematic extermination of the Soviet people” is a signature national-history line, not a peer world-history column. China’s volume is Chinese history; Sudan’s secondary course is regional and ends in 1948 without the European murder narrative. Those zeros are syllabus boundaries. Belarus and India are different: both are in scope for a mid-century world-history treatment, six of eight in-scope books cover the topic, and both return zero on Holocaust stems. That is the avoidance grade, not the curriculum-norm grade.

The Babi Yar numbers are left as a spread, not reconciled into a single “truth figure” for the chapter. If a later rewrite collapses 33–34 thousand and “about 70 thousand” into one number without naming the counting window, that rewrite will be false. Machine-translated discovery files led to pages; the Russian, German, Italian, and Ukrainian lines above are taken from the original extractions. Before any of them becomes a hard claim in a published edition, the printed PDF page should be opened again — especially RU p. 171 (70 thousand) and DE p. 29 (more than 33,000).

Finally, none of these classrooms is a history of Jewish life, of Jewish resistance, or of the murder as it was experienced from inside a ghetto or a transport. If you want that, every column here will leave you unsatisfied — including the German one that runs longest.
