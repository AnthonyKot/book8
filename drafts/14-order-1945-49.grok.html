The 1945–1949 order

In June 1948 the road, rail and canal links into the western sectors of Berlin close. For nearly a year food and coal arrive by air. On 4 April 1949 twelve governments sign a mutual-defence treaty in Washington. Between those dates the wartime alliance is already a set of opposite stories about who froze the peace. What each classroom does with that freeze — who closes the roads, who “starts” the Cold War, whose grammar makes whom the aggressor — is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who started the Cold War — and whose grammar makes whom the aggressor?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 4–11 February 1945: the Yalta (Crimea) Conference — Stalin, Roosevelt, Churchill. Occupation zones for Germany, a United Nations conference, and disputed promises about eastern Europe are on the table.
  • 25 April – 26 June 1945: United Nations Conference at San Francisco; the Charter enters into force on 24 October 1945 with 51 founding members.
  • 17 July – 2 August 1945: the Potsdam Conference — Stalin, Truman, Churchill then Attlee. Demilitarization, denazification, democratization (and in some accounts decartelization) for Germany; zones already drawn.
  • 5 March 1946: Churchill speaks at Fulton, Missouri, and popularizes the phrase “iron curtain” for the line across Europe.
  • 12 March 1947: Truman asks Congress for aid to Greece and Turkey — the “Truman Doctrine.” June 1947: Secretary of State George Marshall proposes the European Recovery Program; roughly $12–13 billion flows to western Europe over the following years.
  • June 1948 – May 1949: the Soviet blockade of West Berlin; Western airlift of food and fuel. 1949: Federal Republic of Germany (west) and German Democratic Republic (east).
  • 4 April 1949: North Atlantic Treaty signed in Washington — NATO. The Warsaw Pact answers only in 1955, after West German rearmament enters the story.
  • Who “started” the Cold War is a schoolroom version of a long scholarly fight: traditional accounts stress Soviet expansion; revisionist ones stress American power and markets; later syntheses stress mutual insecurity. The classrooms below already choose a grammar.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who actually build a 1945–49 order, ordered from thinnest agency (contradictions “predetermined” by ideology; no freeze sequence) to thickest (a named power that systematically interferes, then cuts a city off to starve it). China and India are in period for a world order and never build one — avoidance against a 6-of-8 baseline, tracked as badges. Germany’s NS booklet keeps Potsdam as occupation homework; Sudan’s syllabus keeps Truman only for Palestine. Kazakhstan is never a world-event column.

3 The tell

Every covering book on this ladder can name Yalta and Potsdam. Most can name Fulton, Truman, Marshall, Berlin, and NATO. The disagreement is not the calendar. It is the grammar that turns the calendar into a starter — the same July-1914 problem, moved five years and one alliance later.

Russia’s volume is the agentless pole by syllabus as much as by sentence. Its year-range stops at the 1945 settlement: Yalta, Potsdam, the UN in San Francisco, peace treaties “from 1947.” There is no Marshall Plan, no Truman Doctrine, no iron curtain speech, no Berlin blockade, no NATO. What remains is structure. Contradictions between the USSR and the USA “were predetermined by ideological disagreements.” Socialism or capitalism is “posed starkly.” Nobody starts a Cold War because the Cold War, as a sequence of named crises, is not yet a chapter. Predetermination is the softest possible agency: history has already chosen, so no classroom needs a first mover.

Ukraine builds the system the Russian book only implies, and gives it a name almost nobody else in this set uses. The “Yalta-Potsdam system” is completed at Potsdam; inside it the USA and the USSR for the first time “divided” the world — the scare quotes sit on the verb, as if the textbook itself flinches at the claim while still making it. Confrontation is “historically conditioned.” Then the sequence runs both ways: Stalin’s occupation logic, Churchill at Fulton calling English-speaking states to struggle against communism, Truman’s doctrine, Marshall aid with the “preliminary condition” that communists leave governments, Soviet surplus-markets reading of the same plan, the Soviet occupation administration’s blockade of West Berlin, the Western airlift of “over 2.3 million tons,” NATO signed in Washington, and the USSR treating NATO as an “aggressive bloc.” Mutual division is the opening grammar; named actors on both sides fill the pages that follow. It is the dual-agency classroom of the set.

BY · Кошелев 2021 · printed p. 167

Belarus keeps the same system label — “Yalta-Potsdam system of international relations” — and then points the starter finger west. The United States “seriously feared” the growth of Soviet power over a list of eastern European states; the West sought to keep socialism from spreading. Churchill’s Fulton speech is “warlike,” and “as is considered, laid the beginning of the Cold War.” Marshall aid is not a gift: it is “conditioned by political demands,” and “in the end the USA received powerful levers of influence on Europe,” levers that “especially intensified” after NATO in 1949. The Warsaw Pact appears as the response in 1955. The Berlin blockade and the airlift do not appear as a named crisis at all; the German story jumps to two states in 1949 as Western “revision of the Potsdam agreements.” The freeze, in this grammar, is a Western consolidation with a British speech as its opening gun.

Italy, the United Kingdom, and the United States reverse the finger. Sabbatucci–Vidotto put a “Stalinist design of subjection” already “taking body” at Potsdam; Truman’s arrival is a “brusque change of climate” and American “stiffening”; Churchill’s iron curtain quote is printed in full; Stalin “reacted with the trial of strength of the Berlin blockade” when a strong German state in the Western bloc took shape; the Americans organized a “gigantic air bridge.” Lowe is blunter still: after Potsdam “the Russians systematically interfered… to set up pro-communist governments”; they are “bent on ‘indefinite expansion’”; the blockade’s “aim was to force the West to withdraw… by reducing it to starvation point”; NATO is what unreadiness and fright produce. OpenStax loads the same Soviet-side verbs into everyday American survey prose: the iron curtain is “trapping half the continent under Soviet domination”; free peoples must “resist communist expansion”; “Infuriated, the Soviet Union cut off all ground routes”; NATO is mutual defense against that pressure. Three Western books, one aggressor grammar, different temperatures of the same thermometer.

Watch the adjectives travel. Fulton is a “warlike speech” that starts the Cold War in Minsk and a warning about “indefinite expansion” in London and a trapping iron curtain in an American open textbook. Marshall aid is “levers” and “political demands” in Belarus, a “preliminary condition” of removing communists plus US surplus markets in Ukraine, “dollar imperialism” (Molotov’s phrase, printed by Lowe) in the British survey, and reconstruction money that also keeps the US economy from depression in OpenStax. The Berlin crisis is missing in Minsk, a Soviet occupation administration’s blockade answered by an airlift of 2.3 million tons in Kyiv, a Stalin “trial of strength” in Rome, a starvation plan in London, and an “Infuriated” cutoff in Houston. Same three years. Opposite subjects in the sentences that matter.

Two in-scope books never enter this argument. China’s 纲要 fills 1945–49 with the Chinese civil war and the founding of the PRC; Yalta, Potsdam, Marshall, Truman Doctrine, Berlin, and NATO as a European order do not appear as a system (later pages meet the UN as a 1971 seat fight and as the flag over Korea). India’s NCERT lists Cairo, Yalta and Potsdam only as meetings Korean independence activists petitioned, and meets the “Cold War” as a label for the 1950–53 Korea proxy war — not as a 1945–49 European freeze. Against six covering narrators out of eight books that could have taught the order, those two zeros are avoidance of the starter sequence, not a shared curriculum silence about the mid-century world.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Гісем–Мартинюк (UA) earns the credit line for this event. Not because it is neutral — no covering book is — but because it is the only column that forces the student to hold both hands at once: a named bipolar system in which the two superpowers “divided” the world, Soviet occupation of Central-Eastern Europe, American containment and Truman Doctrine, Marshall aid as both recovery money and surplus-markets strategy with a communist-removal condition, a Soviet blockade with airlift tonnage, and NATO treated by Moscow as an “aggressive bloc.” Depth without a single-finger verdict is rare on this topic. The system label it shares with Belarus is also the finding the Western surveys never need.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) earns the sequence nod: Potsdam knots, Stalinist design, Fulton quote in full, Truman containment, Marshall dollars, Berlin as Stalin’s “trial of strength,” Atlantic Pact while the crisis is still open. A free-market Italian manual written far from both superpower ministries stages the freeze as decisions, not weather.

Lowe (UK) deserves the agency-temperature line: “systematically interfered,” “starvation point,” and NATO born of “unreadiness” and being “frightened” are the hardest English verbs in the set. He also alone among the Western three prints Molotov’s “dollar imperialism,” so the student meets the Soviet counter-name for Marshall without leaving the British page. Hard verbs plus the other side’s insult is a useful combination.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is six books that teach a postwar order with agents. It is not “the world.” Germany’s booklet is a National Socialism primer: Potsdam appears as denazify / democratize / demilitarize and occupation zones that “pursued opposing policies,” not as Marshall, Berlin blockade, or NATO. Sudan’s secondary history ends its arc in 1948 with Arab and Sudanese focus; Truman appears for Palestine and the recognition of Israel, not for containment in Europe. Those zeros are syllabus boundaries, not national silence about 1945. Kazakhstan is national history and is never placed as a peer column on world events.

China and India are different. They are in period for a world-history treatment of 1945–49 and they have pages for those years; they do not build the European bipolar freeze as a system. That is the avoidance signal (6/8 in-scope cover the order). It is still not a claim that those classrooms “hide” the mid-century — they use 1945–49 for other cargo (Chinese civil war / PRC founding; Korean independence petitions and later Korea as proxy war).

Russia’s thinness is partly a bound volume: Чубарьян 10 кл. is titled through 1945. The predetermined-ideology sentence is real agency grammar; the missing Marshall-to-NATO sequence is also a year-range fact. A later Russian 11 кл. volume would re-open the column. Until then, RU sits on this ladder as the settlement-without-freeze pole, not as a full Cold War narrator.

Machine-translated discovery files led us to the pages. Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Italian lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English here for the reader. Page numbers follow printed footers and the topic dossier; UK Lowe pages follow the dossier’s print map (interference / Fulton / Truman / Marshall / blockade / NATO across roughly pp. 125–132) and should be re-opened against the PDF before hard citation in a later pass. Dollar totals for Marshall aid disagree inside the corpus on purpose — UA/IT ~$13 billion, US $12 billion with a modern conversion — and that disagreement is data, not a error to “fix.”

Kept as measurement: Belarus alone among the six covering books skips the Berlin blockade as a named crisis while still dating two German states to 1949. That is not a typo to correct in silence; it is a curriculum choice about which freeze moment the student must rehearse. None of these six is a full diplomatic history of 1945–49; if you want the full cable traffic from Kennan to Molotov, every column will leave you unsatisfied — including Ukraine’s dual-agency survey.
