Versailles, 1919

On 28 June 1919, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, German delegates sign a peace treaty. The date is five years to the day after the shots in Sarajevo. Outside that hall, other rooms of the same settlement are still open or still closed: a Chinese delegation that will not sign; a Soviet government not invited; Arab lands reassigned under a new word, “mandate.” What each classroom does with that conference — whose injury it is for — is the subject of this chapter.

The question · space

Whose peace was it — and which grievance does your textbook need the student to stand inside?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • The Paris Peace Conference opens in January 1919. The principal treaty with Germany — the Treaty of Versailles — is signed on 28 June 1919.
  • Among the settlement’s core German terms taught almost everywhere that covers it: territorial losses in Europe and overseas; army limited (commonly to 100,000); Rhineland occupation / demilitarization; reparations; a war-responsibility clause (Article 231).
  • Related treaties and conferences rearrange former Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, and German colonial holdings (Saint-Germain, Trianon, Neuilly, Sèvres / later Lausanne; San Remo mandates, 1920; Washington Naval Conference, 1921–22).
  • China attended as a victorious power; the conference transferred Germany’s former Shandong rights to Japan. Chinese delegates refused to sign. Student protests in Beijing on 4 May 1919 open what Chinese historiography calls the May Fourth Movement.
  • Soviet Russia was not a negotiating party at Paris. The United States Senate did not ratify the Versailles treaty; the U.S. did not join the League of Nations.
  • Who the settlement was for — German punishment, European map-making, colonial redivision, Chinese justice, Arab self-determination — is not a shared classroom fact. The books below share a calendar more than a subject.

2 The ladder

Eight narrators who actually cover the 1919 settlement, ordered by whose grievance the classroom is built to hold — from a thin Chinese one-liner with no treaty name, through the German-humiliation debate that Western and post-Soviet books share, to the full Chinese diplomatic-defeat lesson and the Sudanese reframe that barely needs Germany at all. Ukraine’s volume starts in 1945; Germany’s NS booklet uses Versailles only as a constraint Hitler later broke. Those are syllabus boundaries, not empty ladder rows. Kazakhstan is never a world-event column.

3 The tell

Every covering book on this ladder can place a conference in Paris in 1919. The disagreement is not the calendar. It is which injury the settlement is allowed to be about — and therefore which student is standing in the room while the treaty is signed.

India’s NCERT spends three sentences and never needs the word Versailles. “Decisions of the post-war peace conference” happen; China, “an ally of the victorious side led by Britain,” does not get territories back; a demonstration becomes a movement. No Article 231, no reparations figure, no Big Three, no League. The Chinese grievance is present as a fuse for May Fourth and absent as a map of the European settlement. That is not a soft German story. It is a different subject squeezed into a China chapter.

The United Kingdom is the only column that turns German objections into a multi-point exam problem the student is invited to judge. Germans “were not allowed into the discussions… simply presented with the terms and told to sign.” The word Diktat appears so that Hitler’s later use of it can be anticipated and partly undercut by Brest-Litovsk. Reparations are “the final humiliation,” with a named number fight: £6,600 million announced later, Keynes urging £2,000 million. Lowe’s classroom is still a European peace; the syllabus work is whether the Germans were right to object. OpenStax keeps that German frame and widens the cast in three American directions the UK chapter does not need: “except Russia” in the opening sentence; reparations “over $30 billion in 1919 dollars”; Japan’s rejected racial-equality clause; Shandong kept “without consulting the millions of Chinese people living there.” Same conference. Different guest list in the grammar.

«Для того чтобы уничтожить Германию, договор был слишком мягким, для того чтобы просто наказать ее — слишком унизительным.»

“In order to destroy Germany, the treaty was too soft; in order to simply punish it — too humiliating.”

BY · quoting A. I. Patrushev, printed p. 103 (source box under the humiliating-peace paragraph)

Belarus and Russia package the same settlement as a system — the Versailles-Washington system — and then disagree on how many men set the tone. BY’s “Big Three” (Wilson, Lloyd George, Clemenceau) drop Orlando; RU’s “Big Four” keep him. Italy, watching from the fourth chair, solves the count by counting and demoting: Orlando figures “nominally among the ‘four great ones’” and plays “a marginal role.” Errors and spreads are data. The shared post-Soviet verdict is German humiliation as machinery: BY’s peace “was humiliating” and “contributed to the formation of fascism”; RU’s terms are “predatory and humiliating,” a “time bomb in the very center of Europe,” a “yoke” Germany will strive to throw off. Then RU does a job the German-humiliation belt usually skips: Soviet Russia “was not represented” and became “an object of intervention”; the mandate system is “the division of colonial booty traditional for imperialist predators.” The empty chair and the colonial map sit in the same fragility paragraph as the time bomb.

China never titles the event Versailles. Stem search on the original extract finds zero 凡尔赛. The lesson is “Paris Peace Conference” as China’s diplomatic defeat: “power triumphed over justice”; Britain and the United States “manipulated” the conference; Shandong’s rights go to Japan; that transfer is “the fuse” of May Fourth; Chinese representatives “refused to sign.” The student is not asked whether the Germans were treated fairly. The student is asked what a victorious ally is worth when power writes the table. Italy, later and elsewhere in the same manual, will call the same Chinese outcome a “sacrifice” and an “umiliazione di Versailles” — using the German place-name for a Chinese injury China itself will not put on the lesson title.

Sudan does not need the German army limit. European states “called upon each other to Versailles” to hold a Peace Conference; Wilson brings Fourteen Principles, of which the one that matters is self-determination for colonized peoples — “the principle to which the Arab peoples clung.” San Remo (taught here as 16 April 1920) is where Britain, France, and Italy “clung to their colonial interests” while pretending to reconcile them with Wilson. The mandate is a system “devised by shrewd English politicians.” The punchline is not revanchism in the Rhineland. It is: “the various Arab countries became subject to a new link in the chain of colonialism.” Same year-family as Versailles. Different hinge, different map, different student.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Lowe (UK) earns the credit line for the German settlement as a problem rather than a slogan. Only this classroom runs a multi-point German-objections debate — Diktat, Article 231, the £6,600m / Keynes £2,000m fight — and keeps both the force of the complaint and the counter-arguments (Brest-Litovsk; the treaty “could have been even more harsh”) on the same page. Distance from 1919’s losing capitals becomes syllabus equipment, not soft focus.

纲要 (CN) earns the space lead: it alone makes China’s non-signature and the Shandong transfer the center of what Paris was for, with “power triumphed over justice” as the moral grammar. That is not the full European settlement. It is the finding that a victorious ally can be absent from the Western ladder’s main column and still be the whole lesson somewhere else.

Чубарьян (RU) is the packaging lead. “Versailles-Washington system,” “time bomb,” Soviet non-representation, and “colonial booty” in one fragility section is the multi-grievance map the comparison needs. Credit for range is not credit for calm; the predator vocabulary does the job it is hired to do. OpenStax (US) deserves a nod for three clauses most columns never print together: “except Russia,” the racial-equality rejection, and Shandong “without consulting” the people who lived there.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight books that teach the 1919 settlement as an event with consequences. It is not “the world.” Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945; Germany’s federal booklet is a National Socialism primer that mentions the Versailles treaty only as a set of limits Hitler later revised. Those zeros are syllabus boundaries. Against an 8-of-8 in-scope covering baseline, they are not avoidance of Versailles — they are other books. Kazakhstan is national history and is never a peer column on world events.

Kept as measurement, not as gotcha: “Big Three” (BY, and Lowe’s illustration of three leaders) versus “Big Four” (RU, IT with Orlando “marginal”) is a real spread inside the corpus, not a translation glitch. Reparations appear as £6,600 million (UK), “over $30 billion in 1919 dollars” (US), and as a sum deferred or unnamed in several columns — currency and timing differ because the books are not solving the same arithmetic problem. San Remo’s date is taught in SD as 16 April 1920; standard diplomatic calendars often give a conference window of 19–26 April 1920. None of these eight is a full diplomatic history of every treaty from Versailles to Lausanne; if you want the complete map file, every column will leave you unsatisfied — including RU’s system chapter and Lowe’s objections seminar.
