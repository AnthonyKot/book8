Russo-Japanese War, 1905

In February 1904, in the dark off Port Arthur, Japanese destroyers launch torpedoes into the anchored Russian Pacific Fleet without a declaration of war. Sixteen months later, Russia's Baltic fleet lies destroyed in the Tsushima Strait, and an Asian power has defeated a European empire for the first time in modern history. What seven classrooms do with that defeat is the subject of this chapter.

The question · omission

Whose soil was the war fought on — and whose textbook never names it?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • 1894–1895: First Sino-Japanese War. The Treaty of Shimonoseki cedes the Liaodong Peninsula, Taiwan, and the Penghu Islands to Japan; the Triple Intervention of Russia, Germany, and France forces Japan to relinquish Liaodong.
  • 1897–1898: Russia leases the southern Liaodong Peninsula from China, builds the fortified naval base at Port Arthur (Lüshun), and constructs the South Manchuria Railway linking it to the Trans-Siberian line.
  • 30 January 1902: The Anglo-Japanese Alliance is signed in London, ensuring British neutrality if either signatory enters a war with one power, and military assistance if either faces two powers.
  • 8–9 February 1904: Japan launches a surprise night attack on the Russian fleet at Port Arthur; formal declarations of war follow on 10 February. The siege of Port Arthur ends with Russian surrender in January 1905.
  • February–March 1905: The Battle of Mukden, involving over 600,000 soldiers, ends in a Japanese land victory and Russian retreat northward.
  • 27–28 May 1905: The Battle of Tsushima. Admiral Togo Heihachiro's fleet annihilates Russia's Baltic Fleet after its 18,000-mile voyage from Europe.
  • 5 September 1905: The Treaty of Portsmouth, mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt, ends the war. Russia recognizes Japan's paramount interest in Korea, transfers the Port Arthur lease and South Manchuria Railway rights, and cedes southern Sakhalin.
  • The war's land campaigns were fought entirely on Chinese territory (Manchuria) and Korean soil; the Qing Dynasty declared formal neutrality on 12 February 1904.

Five narrators who actually cover the war, ordered from thinnest agency (war as domestic catastrophe) to thickest (named surprise attack, named battles, and explicit starter syntax). Books outside the period — Russia 1914–45, Ukraine 1945–, Germany’s NS booklet, Sudan’s national syllabus — are absent here on purpose. China's state textbook covers the era but omits the war entirely; Kazakhstan’s book is national history and is never a world-event column here.

3 The tell

The five covering books agree that Japan won, that Russia lost, and that the balance of power in East Asia tilted permanently in 1905. The disagreement begins in the grammar of why the war was fought and whose soil was being fought over.

Belarus grounds the conflict in domestic political calculation. Russia's ruling circles believed that a “small but victorious war” would steady a shaken tsarist government. That calculation failed: the war “turned into a catastrophe,” unleashing Bloody Sunday and the 1905 Revolution. The military campaign itself is compressed into a sentence, but the regime motive is explicit. War was an internal remedy that backfired into revolution.

The British and Italian accounts elevate the defeat to a global civilizational watershed. Norman Lowe presents Japan's victory as its entry ticket to the table of Great Powers, marking the first time in modern history that an Asian country defeated a European power. Sabbatucci and Vidotto go further: the defeat destroyed “in a single blow the myth of the military and technological supremacy of the old continent and that of a presumed superiority of the 'white race'.” In Rome and London, the war's primary outcome was racial and geopolitical: Europe's military invincibility was broken.

«…distruggendo in un sol colpo il mito della supremazia militare e tecnologica del vecchio continente e quello di una presunta superiorità della "razza bianca".»

“…destroying in a single blow the myth of the military and technological supremacy of the old continent and that of a presumed superiority of the ‘white race’.”

IT · Sabbatucci–Vidotto (2004) · p. 236

OpenStax assigns direct active agency to Japan while framing the outbreak through diplomatic cause-and-effect: “When Russia refused to compromise, Japan attacked the Russian fleet at Port Arthur…” The American textbook structures the narrative around bargaining, naval initiative, and legal treaties signed at Portsmouth.

India’s NCERT provides the spatial fact that Western surveys leave implicit: the Russo-Japanese War was “a war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory.” Two foreign empires collided over Manchurian ports and railways while the sovereign owner of the land was reduced to spectator status, abolishing its imperial exam system in 1905 in the war's immediate wake.

That leads to the most significant finding of this chapter: China’s own state textbook (中外历史纲要 上, 2019) never names the 1904–1905 Russo-Japanese War. The volume details late Qing reforms, the 1894 Sino-Japanese War, Russia's 1898 lease of Port Arthur, Sun Yat-sen's 1905 Tongmenghui, and the 1911 Revolution — but the war itself is absent. Because five of six in-scope narrators cover the conflict, China's silence is an avoidance signal rather than a curriculum norm. The nation on whose soil the armies clashed leaves the clash off the page entirely.

Russia’s unified state course (Чубарьян 2023, 10 кл.) begins its narrative in 1914, placing the war chronologically outside its main text. It acknowledges the outcome in a single retrospective phrase on page 26: Russia “strived toward rapprochement” with Britain after “the defeat in the Russo-Japanese War.” In accordance with our scope rules, this is recorded as a structural boundary rather than an avoidance signal.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

NCERT (IN) earns the top credit line for spatial candor. It alone specifies in plain text that this was “a war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory,” restoring the physical ground and victims that Western accounts leave implicit and Chinese state textbooks skip entirely.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) deserves credit for narrative depth and historical synthesis. It stages the entire military progression — Port Arthur, Mukden, Tsushima, Portsmouth — and unhesitatingly names the global shock: the shattering of European military invincibility and the myth of white racial superiority.

OpenStax (US) earns a nod for clear starter syntax, explicitly attributing the tactical opening to Japan’s attack at Port Arthur following Russia’s refusal to negotiate over Korea and Liaodong.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder reflects five textbooks whose published scope covers 1904–1905. The absence of standalone war sections in Ukraine (1945–), Germany (1939–45 NS primer), and Sudan (1821–1948 regional) represents syllabus boundaries rather than topic avoidance.

China’s state-compiled history (纲要 上, 2019) is in scope for late Qing history but omits the 1904–1905 war entirely. This zero has been systematically verified across all relevant search stems in original text (日俄, 俄日, 日俄战争, 对马, 旅顺, 朴茨茅斯, 朴次茅斯, 满洲, 沙俄, 俄国, 1904, 1905). Kazakhstan’s textbook is dedicated to national history and is not evaluated as a world-history column here.

Textbooks provide high-level historical syntheses. Readers seeking operational detail on naval artillery, siege warfare at Port Arthur, or micro-level troop movements at Mukden will need to consult specialized military histories.
