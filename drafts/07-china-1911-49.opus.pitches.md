# Chapter 7 (China, 1911–1949) — Thread Pitches

Four candidates. Each seed is something the ladder actually surfaced — a quote, a number,
or a conspicuous silence — and each loop is general history, never attributed to a textbook.
The chapter's question is **vocabulary**: what do you call the war you won? Three of the four
pitches below are therefore word-shaped; the second is deliberately not.
Pick one; the rest get banked or dropped.

---

### Pitch 1: The word that travels without its war

- **Seed** — one page of the American book, twice. OpenStax p. 582 prints, unquoted, "In 1949,
  the CCP's People's Liberation Army decisively defeated the forces of the GMD." Twenty lines
  further down the same page it reaches Tibet and writes "an event described by the CCP as the
  'liberation of Tibet.'" Same root, same page: a name passes, a claim gets quotation marks.
  Meanwhile seven of the eight columns decline the Chinese name for the war itself
  (人民解放战争, "People's War of Liberation") and print "civil war" — and six of the eight
  never name the Liberation Army at all.
- **Loop** — 解放 *jiěfàng* is an old term for release from bondage that was reworked in
  nineteenth-century Japan (*kaihō*) as the standard East Asian rendering of European
  "emancipation / liberation," and came back into Chinese through students in Tokyo around
  1900 — in the same shipment as 革命 (revolution), 共和 (republic), 民主 (democracy),
  社会主义 (socialism) and 军阀 (warlord). The entire political vocabulary that the years
  1911–49 are argued about in was imported, in the decade before those years began, from the
  country whose invasion occupies the middle of them. The war got its name from Japan and its
  verdict from Beijing.
- **Lander (linguistic, shared)** — the initials PLA. It is the only major army on earth whose
  official English name still contains the verdict of the war that created it, and it is in
  every corpus country's newspapers this month, over stories about Taiwan and the South China
  Sea. A reader who has read that headline has read a 1949 court ruling without noticing.
- **Why it might fail** — it is a thread about words in a chapter already about words; risks
  reading as more of the tell rather than the missing piece. The Japanese-loanword loop also
  needs a careful source, and one term (解放) may have a different borrowing history from the
  others.

### Pitch 2: Nobody's textbook says where the money came from

- **Seed** — four classrooms print the same mechanism and not one prints its cause. China:
  the note issue in the first eight months of 1948 "was already 470,000 times the issue for the
  whole of 1937" (printed p. 165). India: "Prices rose 30 per cent per month between 1945 and
  1949, and utterly destroyed the lives of ordinary people" (p. 171). Britain: "Its policy of
  paying for the wars by printing extra money resulted in galloping inflation, which caused
  hardship for the masses and ruined many of the middle class." The United States: "Ruinous
  hyperinflation, which the GMD was unable to control." Four books agree that the paper money
  lost the Nationalists their cities. None of them says where that paper money came from.
- **Loop** — China was the last large economy running on silver. In June 1934 the United States
  Congress passed the Silver Purchase Act, a farm-and-mining-state measure obliging the
  Treasury to buy silver until it made up a quarter of monetary reserves. The world price rose
  steeply, silver drained out of Shanghai, and China — whose money supply *was* that metal —
  got deflation, bank failures and a credit freeze. In November 1935 the Nationalist government
  gave up silver and issued a managed paper currency, the *fabi*. That is the paper that then
  had to finance eight years of war with Japan and three of civil war, and that is the paper
  measured at 470,000 times in the Chinese textbook. Chapter 1 is the other half of the circuit:
  the silver came *into* China for three centuries in exchange for tea, silk and porcelain, went
  *out* again for opium, and the last of it left in the 1930s because of a bill written for
  Nevada and Montana.
- **Causal precision (the punchline must not overclaim)** — the Silver Purchase Act did not
  cause the hyperinflation. War finance caused the hyperinflation. What the Act did was remove
  the metal floor under Chinese money four years before the war that would be paid for by
  printing. The precise version is the punchline: a country cannot inflate a currency made of
  silver, and in 1935 China stopped having one.
- **Lander (material + linguistic, shared)** — the yuan, the yen and the won are the same word:
  圓/元, "round." All three were named for the round silver dollar — the Spanish and then
  Mexican eight-real coin — that circulated from Manila across East Asia for three hundred
  years. Every reader who has looked at an exchange rate has read the shape of a Spanish silver
  coin. It braids back to ch. 1 (the silver drain) and forward to ch. 15 (the won).
- **Why it might work** — it is the only candidate that answers a question the ladder actually
  leaves open, in a chapter where four narrators name a mechanism and none names its origin;
  and it is wonder rather than accusation.

### Pitch 3: Three lengths for one march, and one notebook behind all of them

- **Seed** — the corpus cannot agree how long the Long March was or how many finished it.
  China: 二万五千里, the twenty-five-thousand-*li* march (about 12,500 km), a number that is
  part of the name. Britain: "about 6000 miles in 368 days," with "almost 100 000" setting out
  and "the 20 000 survivors" arriving. Italy: about 100,000 leaving and "fewer than 10,000"
  arriving "after a march lasting a year and 10,000 kilometres long." Two survivor counts, a
  factor of two apart. And China, which gives the march a full lesson and a route map, prints
  the drop from "over 80,000 people to over 30,000 people" and then no arrival figure at all.
- **Loop** — in June 1936 an American journalist crossed the blockade with a letter of
  introduction written in invisible ink, spent four months in the Communist base, and came out
  with the first extended interviews Mao Zedong had given anybody. *Red Star Over China* (1937)
  was for two decades the only substantial account of these people in any Western language, and
  the itinerary, the days and much of the arithmetic in every Western textbook descend from it.
  Lowe knows this and prints Snow by name for forty-five words. Then he does the thing no other
  column does: he tells the student that two writers challenged the whole story in 2005. One
  set of conversations in a cave in 1936 is still setting the numbers in an Italian university
  manual and a British exam text.
- **Lander (weak)** — a paperback still in print in every corpus language. Honest, but a reader
  who has never read it touches nothing.
- **Why it might fail** — the lander is the problem. This is an analyst's delight and a reader's
  shrug. Bank it for the receipts or for a book-profile column on Lowe.

### Pitch 4: The word China exported

- **Seed** — four classrooms, four degrees of confidence in one borrowed noun. Lowe gives the
  men who ran China from 1916 to 1928 a period with capital letters, the Warlord Era, and uses
  the word twenty-seven times without a flinch. NCERT holds it at arm's length: Chiang "launched
  a military campaign to control the 'warlords'." Sabbatucci–Vidotto translates and then
  apologises for the translation — «i cosiddetti signori della guerra», "the so-called lords of
  war." Russia and Belarus refuse the metaphor and substitute a class label,
  «военно-феодальные клики», "military-feudal cliques." The Chinese volume uses the source word,
  军阀, thirty-eight times.
- **Loop** — 軍閥 is a compound built in Meiji Japan (*gunbatsu*, "army clique"), taken up by
  Chinese writers around 1916 to describe the men holding the provinces, and rendered by
  correspondents in Beijing as "war lord." It entered English in the 1920s attached to that one
  country, then came loose. A century later it is the default English noun for an armed man
  holding territory anywhere — and it travels back into the other corpus languages as a
  translation of the *English*, not the Chinese.
- **Lander (linguistic, shared)** — the reader has used a Chinese political term about Somalia,
  Afghanistan, Libya or Sudan without knowing it was Chinese, and the Sudan of ch. 2 is on the
  list. Every corpus country's press prints it.
- **Why it might fail** — the loop is a dictionary story, not a causal one; the thread beat is
  supposed to be the causal loop the exam units cut, and a word's migration may be too thin to
  carry that weight. Could work as a short coda to whichever thread wins.
