### Pitch 1: The blank cheque *(linguistic — seed from the ladder — recommended)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** OpenStax and Lowe use the same banking metaphor in the same week of the crisis: Germany extends a “blank check” / “blank cheque” to Austria-Hungary — unlimited backing, no conditions. Then the American book spends the next sentence undoing the will in Berlin: Germany “found itself pulled into the fight whether it wanted to be or not” (US, ~p. 437). Lowe keeps the cheque and walks it into Fischer (UK, ~pp. 14–18). Italy never uses the English metaphor and still names the same fact as “unconditional support” from the German government (IT, p. 271).
- **Loop.** A great power promises another great power support without a spending limit → the junior partner writes the July ultimatum and the 28 July declaration knowing the account will clear → when Russia mobilizes, the guarantor must either repudiate the cheque or pay it in declarations of war → schoolbooks still reach for the same two words when they need a name for unlimited commitment.
- **Lander.** “Blank check” / “blank cheque” is still ordinary political English for a leader who hands someone else an open-ended authorization. The reader has used the phrase for a budget vote or a blank permission slip long before anyone mentioned Bethmann-Hollweg.
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 classrooms print the metaphor (UK, US); 1 of 11 pairs it with “pulled into the fight” in the next breath (US); 1 of 11 reaches the same unconditional-support fact without the banking word (IT); 0 of 11 explain why a cheque is the right metaphor for a telegram.
- **Receipts-lite.** The “blank cheque” / Hoyos mission language is standard in any July Crisis survey; Lowe cites the Kaiser’s telegram of support; OpenStax uses the American spelling on p. 437. <!-- CHECK: Hoyos mission to Berlin 5–6 July 1914; German assurance of support commonly dated 5–6 July -->
- **Why it fits this chapter.** It is the ladder’s own hinge — same object, opposite moral finish in US vs UK — and the lander is a phrase still in the mouth in every corpus language’s educated register. Causal precision: the cheque did not fire the first gun; it is what made Austria willing to risk a Russian answer.

### Pitch 2: War guilt as homework that never closed *(linguistic — feeds ch. 5)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Belarus opens the responsibility paragraph with the classroom memory this chapter feeds: “Initially, Germany was considered guilty… under the terms of the Versailles Peace Treaty” before redistributing blame to “all its main participants” (BY, p. 90). Lowe’s whole §1.4 is titled “What caused the war, and who was to blame?” and ends with a majority for Fischer — the long German argument with Versailles’ Article 231 already in the rear-view mirror (UK, ~pp. 11–18). Russia’s “share of guilt” / “unprovoked… bloc” double move is the same homework done in one paragraph (RU, p. 29).
- **Loop.** 1919’s settlement needs a guilty party for reparations and disarmament → Article 231 puts war responsibility on Germany and allies → for a century, schoolbooks either restate that verdict, dilute it into shared guilt, or (in Lowe) restage the historians’ fight over whether Fischer brought the verdict back → the word pair “war guilt” becomes the portable name for “who started 1914.”
- **Lander.** Anyone who has sat through a modern argument about “who started” a later war has borrowed the grammar this settlement fixed in public speech. The phrase outlived the treaty.
- **Who tells it.** 2 of 11 already point at Versailles from inside the 1914 lesson (BY explicitly; UK via the Fischer/Versailles historiography arc); the full Article 231 treatment belongs to ch. 5 — this pitch only plants the hinge the next chapter harvests.
- **Receipts-lite.** Treaty of Versailles, Article 231 (war responsibility clause), 28 June 1919 — signed five years to the day after Sarajevo. <!-- CHECK: Article 231 text; signature date 28 June 1919 -->
- **Why it fits.** Cleanest braid to ch. 5; risk is stacking two chapters on the same noun. Use if the guinea-pig reader cares more about the word that survived than about the banking metaphor.

### Pitch 3: The railway timetable *(my candidate — material / technological)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Lowe alone among the five gives the student the mechanical pressure under the political story: mobilization plans “based on precise railway timetables for the rapid movement of troops… accelerated the tempo of events and reduced almost to nil the time available for negotiation,” with Moltke insisting there was “no time to change all the railway timetables” when the Kaiser asked for a Russia-only option (UK, §1.4 on Schlieffen / Ritter–Taylor). Every other covering book names Schlieffen as a plan; none of them make the trains the constraint.
- **Loop.** Mass armies in 1914 move by rail on pre-written schedules → once general mobilization is ordered, reversing the trains is treated by staffs as harder than going to war → political leaders still talking about localization are overtaken by a logistics script written years earlier → the “who started it” question partly becomes “who ordered mobilization first,” which is why Lowe’s multi-theory paragraph can blame Russia for full mobilization and Germany for the plan that made mobilization mean Belgium.
- **Lander.** Any reader who has watched a modern airport cascade from one delayed bank of flights already knows the shape: a system built for one-way rush has no graceful pause button. The 1914 version was steel rails and printed schedules instead of departure boards.
- **Who tells it.** 1 of 11 classrooms puts railway timetables into the blame section (UK); 4 of 11 name Schlieffen without the trains (RU, BY, IT, US); 0 of 11 join “timetable” to the blank-check week as one mechanism.
- **Receipts-lite.** A. J. P. Taylor’s timetable thesis as Lowe summarizes it; Moltke–Wilhelm exchange of late July 1914 in any standard diplomatic collection; Terence Zuber’s *Inventing the Schlieffen Plan* (2002) as the dissent Lowe himself prints. <!-- CHECK: Taylor, War by Timetable (1969); Zuber 2002 -->
- **Why it fits.** Non-obvious, material, shared (every corpus country runs trains). Causal precision: timetables did not invent the quarrel over Serbia; they compressed the hours in which a quarrel could still be walked back. Risk: can read as exoneration if the blank-check agency is not kept in the same breath.

### Pitch 4: A city that kept the name *(material — Sarajevo on the map)*

- **Seed (from the ladder).** Every covering book that runs the crisis opens on the same six letters: Sarajevo. Lowe: “This tragic event, which took place in Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, on 28 June 1914…” (UK, ~p. 11). Russia: “on one of the streets of Sarajevo…” (RU, p. 28). Italy: Princip kills the archduke “while they were crossing in an open car the streets of Sarajevo” (IT, p. 269). The three zeros never need the city; they only need the war’s years.
- **Loop.** Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia (1908) and stages a royal visit on a Serb national anniversary → a local assassin with cross-border backing kills the heir → the city becomes the portable name of the spark → a century later the same city is still on the map, still the capital of Bosnia and Herzegovina, still carrying tourist plaques for a wrong turn and two shots. <!-- CHECK: visit on Vidovdan (St Vitus Day), 28 June; Latin Bridge / Princip’s Bridge site still marked -->
- **Lander.** Type **Sarajevo** into a map app. The street pattern is not a metaphor. The place where the open car turned is still a place.
- **Who tells it.** 5 of 11 name Sarajevo as the spark (the covering five); 0 of 11 of the zero books do; 0 of 11 tell the student the city is still the capital under that name after 1992–95. <!-- CHECK: Dayton 1995 confirms Sarajevo as capital of BiH -->
- **Receipts-lite.** Museum of Sarajevo 1878–1918 / Latin Bridge site; any contemporary map of Bosnia and Herzegovina.
- **Why it fits.** Most concrete lander; weakest as a *hinge* — the comparison already agrees on the city, so the thread risks becoming tourism unless it stresses the zeros that never need the name. Best as a two-sentence echo inside Pitch 1 or 3 rather than the main thread.

## Recommendation

**Pitch 1 (blank cheque)** first: it is earned entirely by the ladder (US softens it; UK keeps it and hands it to Fischer; IT has the fact without the word), the lander is a living phrase, and causal precision is easy to keep (authorization ≠ first gunshot). **Pitch 3 (railway timetable)** second — the non-obvious material candidate, best if the guinea-pig reader lights up at logistics more than at metaphor. **Pitch 2** if the braid into Versailles is the priority for Part II. **Pitch 4** is the safest sensory lander and the weakest as a standalone finding; bank it as lede reinforcement (already half-used) rather than the thread.
