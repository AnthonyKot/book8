# AGENT.md — instructions for the writing model

Read this BEFORE drafting or revising any chapter. Precedence: CONTEXT.md (rules) →
this file (what the book is for) → TEMPLATE.md (chapter rhythm) → prose.
Nothing here overrides CONTEXT.md's voice rules or the quotes-or-nothing law.

## Who is reading

An adult who finished school 10–30 years ago and remembers almost nothing of it.
They are not a historian, not a student, and owe us no attention. They will stop
reading the moment the book feels like homework. They stay for two things:
watching the narrators contradict each other (the comparison), and the shock of
finding themselves personally inside the story (the hook). Every chapter must
deliver both.

## Priority stack (in order; higher wins on conflict)

1. **Never false.** Every claim checkable; every textbook claim quoted; every
   enrichment claim mainstream-verifiable (dates, names, one reputable source).
   Being boring is a failure; being wrong is a betrayal.
2. **The reader must be IN the chapter** — see the cup-of-tea rule below.
3. **Comparison is the skeleton.** The ladder, the originals, the tells — that
   is the book. Enrichment is muscle on that skeleton, never a replacement.
4. **The missing piece is evidence.** When we add connective history the
   textbooks skip, count who tells it (dossier stats) — the gap is a finding.

## The cup-of-tea rule (the personal hook)

Origin: the Opium War chapter research surfaced that Indian tea exists because
Britain stole Chinese tea stock (Fortune, 1848) after fighting wars to sell
Indian opium for Chinese tea. A reader who has drunk Indian tea all their life
has been holding the Opium War's outcome in their hands — *that* connects them.

Every chapter: at least ONE moment where the history touches something the
reader owns, does, drinks, says, or sees on a map today. It must be true,
concrete, and land in three sentences or fewer. Candidates:
- an object (Darjeeling tea; a Samsung phone and the Korea chapter)
- a word they use ("appeasement" in every modern argument; "propaganda" itself)
- a line on today's map (the 38th parallel; Hong Kong's status until 1997)
- a habit or product with a hidden origin (tea breaks, opium-derived medicine)
If no honest hook exists for a chapter, skip it — a forced hook reads as
marketing and burns trust. Never invent one.

## Vocabulary (shared with the WRITE model)

- **Hinge** — a non-obvious connection the COMPARISON discovered (India in the
  opium triangle; DE alone naming Einsatzgruppen). Found in dossiers/ladders,
  never imported.
- **Lander** — the one concrete bridge from the hinge to ordinary life: a
  commodity, a word still in the mouth, a border still on the news, a product
  in the reader's hand. Analyst win: "IN alone maps Britain–India–China."
  Reader win: "something I still consume was on that map." A chapter needs both.
- Prefer hinges that are **material** (tea, silver, oil, a city still on the
  map) or **linguistic** (a word still used for an enemy) over abstract
  "lessons of history."

## The missing piece (the integrated-history thread)

Textbooks fragment history into exam units. Once per chapter, tell the ONE
causal loop that makes the event make sense as a system — the way the tea
deficit → silver drain → engineered opium market → war → tea theft → collapse
of China's export economy loop makes the Opium Wars legible. Rules:
- **The comparison comes first and the thread grows out of it.** The seed must
  be something the ladder surfaced — a phrase a textbook used ("opium, which
  grew in India"), a number, or a conspicuous silence. Never bolt on a story
  the comparison didn't earn.
- **The thread must be shared.** It has to land for a reader from ANY of the
  corpus countries — an object, word, or consequence they all live with (tea
  is on every table; the 38th parallel is in everyone's phone supply chain).
  A hook only one nationality feels fails the test.
- One thread per chapter, compact (0.5–1 screen). Not a trivia dump.
- It comes from general history, NOT from the corpus — never attribute it to a
  textbook, and keep it visually/structurally distinct from quoted material.
- Close the thread by counting: "N of 11 classrooms tell any of this" (from the
  topic dossier). The thread's absence from the classroom IS the finding.
- **Causal precision in the punchline** (reader-review precedent, ch. 15): state
  exactly what the mechanism produced. The empty chair did not produce the Korean
  War — it produced the flag the war was fought under. A closing line must never
  promote a precondition into a cause; if the punchy version overclaims, the
  precise version IS the punchline.
- Receipts-lite: name + date + where a reader can check (a book title, a
  primary source, a museum, a still-operating factory). No verification
  machinery — but nothing that can't survive two minutes of googling.
- Wonder is allowed and wanted (Fortune in disguise, Wardian cases, Ghazipur
  still operating). Sneering is not. The tone is "look what actually connects"
  — never "look what they hid from you" (that register is banned; see
  CONTEXT.md: the coda depends on the reader trusting textbooks enough to
  check their own).

## Where it goes (beat placement)

TEMPLATE.md beats, with the thread inserted after The tell, before Credit:
kicker → lede → question → boring facts → ladder → the tell → **the thread**
(integrated story + who tells it + the lander) → credit → limits → receipts →
coda. **Integrate, don't stack**: the thread beat is the hinge's home, but the
hinge must also be seeded in the lede (the object on the table) and echoed in
the tell or credit — never a bolted-on "fun fact" box a reader can skip.
Max ONE lander per chapter; a dossier with no honest hinge gets a clean
comparison chapter, said plainly, rather than a forced tea moment.

## Who owns the personal (hard rule)

Reader-generic by default: "the tea in many kitchens," never a fabricated
autobiography and never second-person therapy ("you were lied to in school" is
banned twice over — voice rules and audience respect). Author-personal material
enters ONLY when the human author pastes their own life in; the model must not
invent or extrapolate it. No "you feel X."

## Pitch before writing (the guinea-pig gate)

The thread is never drafted cold. For each chapter, present the user 2–4
candidate threads first — each pitched in ≤4 sentences: the seed quote/silence
from the comparison, the loop, the shared hook. The user is the test reader
(adult, school long ago); the candidate that gets the strongest genuine
reaction gets written, the rest are dropped or banked for other chapters.
No reaction = no thread for that chapter (see cup-of-tea rule: never force).

## Pre-ship test (all four or don't ship)

1. Would a tired adult who hated history class read past the ladder? (If the
   ladder is the last interesting thing, the chapter is homework.)
2. Does the reader touch the history at least once — object, word, map, habit?
3. Is every enrichment claim checkable in two minutes, and marked for human
   verification in the draft (HTML comment `<!-- CHECK: ... -->`)?
4. Did the thread stay under a screen and off the ladder's stage? The chapter's
   longest element must still be the textbooks' own words.
