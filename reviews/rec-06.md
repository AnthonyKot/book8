1. Rewrite the opening as an explicit backward step from Chapter 5. After Versailles has carried the reader through 1919, “In Petrograd on the night of October 25, 1917…” currently feels like the book has slipped two years by accident. Add a hinge such as: before the peacemakers met—and before they excluded Soviet Russia—the event they feared had already happened. This will make the thematic order feel intentional and turn Chapter 6 into the entrance to “Part III · Revolutions.”

2. Keep the operating streetcars in the lede, but make them earn their place. They are the chapter’s best detail: an epochal seizure occurring while an ordinary city keeps moving. Replace the generic finish, “What eight national classrooms do with that night… is the subject of this chapter,” with a sentence that poses the streetcar/Winter Palace contradiction, then return to it at the end of “The tell.” That gives the chapter an image of its own instead of the apparatus’s standard throat-clearing.

3. Simplify “The question · naming.” “Revolution, coup, or external catalyst” mixes two different comparisons: what October was inside Russia and what 1917 later did abroad. Make the main question only “Revolution, insurrection, or coup—who does the verb put in control?” Treat “external catalyst” as a second finding, not a third answer to the same question.

4. Rebuild “The ladder” in two visibly labelled movements without changing any textbook quotation: first “1917 exported” (Sudan, China, India), then “power taken in Petrograd” (Russia, Belarus, Italy, US, UK). The present ordering calls the first group “thinnest agency,” but those books are not offering low-agency accounts of Petrograd; they are answering a different question. A divider will make the absence of local actors an interesting result instead of a taxonomic fudge.

5. Split the Belarus, OpenStax, and Lowe ladder blocks into discrete, page-keyed quotations. Each currently presents excerpts from distant pages as one continuous block joined by ellipses—for Belarus, four pages are collapsed into a single apparent speech. Preserve every quoted word exactly, but show the seams. This chapter argues that grammar and sequence matter; its exhibit cannot conceal its own sequence.

6. Give “The boring facts” one plain calendar rule before the bullets, then apply it consistently. The chapter opens on 25 October, the Italian row opens on 6–7 November, and the first fact introduces Julian/Gregorian dates only in parentheses. Tell the pleasure reader once that Russia’s calendar lag is why the October Revolution occurred in November by the calendar they use; thereafter print paired dates in one stable order. Do not remove any event or qualification.

7. Cut the generic first paragraph of “The tell” (“Every narrator agrees… The debate in the grammar is…”) and open with the live collision: Belarus’s “most important event in world history” beside Lowe’s “almost a bloodless coup,” both describing a night with scarcely any fighting. Then ask what each noun needs the reader to believe about legitimacy and agency. That is the chapter’s argument; reach it before sorting countries into blocs.

8. Rewrite the Russia paragraph’s claim about passivity. “The Provisional Government ‘was overthrown by left-radical socialists’” is grammatically passive but explicitly names the actors, so it does not by itself diffuse agency. Contrast subject position and verb choice precisely—government as grammatical subject, Bolsheviks in a by-phrase, power merely “proclaimed”—and stop short of saying the sentence hides who acted. The sharper close reading will match the standard set by Chapter 5’s bracket and Chapter 7’s comma.

9. Replace “Belarus takes the strongest ideological position in the corpus” with an account of what the page actually does: it puts bread queues, dual power, a nearly unresisted seizure, a world-historical verdict, and Bernard Shaw’s later admiration into one teaching sequence. Also remove the repeated Belarus line as a standalone pull quote, retaining it verbatim in the ladder; the second display adds emphasis but no new evidence. Use the visual break for material not already exhibited.

10. Write the missing “The thread” before doing any fine polish elsewhere. Build it around the chapter’s own best contradiction: the operating streetcars and the “practically bloodless” palace takeover versus the later mental picture of a mass storming. With verified sourcing, trace how a sparse tactical event acquired an epic public image, then connect that process to the reader’s present habit of seeing a few seconds of a government building on a screen and immediately being offered “revolution,” “insurrection,” or “coup” as the caption. End by returning to the streetcar. This would be a human essay; a general meditation on political labels would not be enough.

11. Use the end of that thread to hand the reader to Chapter 7. The current China paragraph already contains the bridge—“The widespread dissemination of Marxism in China began after the October Revolution”—but it is buried in a three-country summary and the next chapter barely inherits 1917. Close on the fact that October becomes an origin story abroad before Chapter 7 asks what China calls its own revolution and civil war. This will prevent the two consecutive revolution chapters from feeling like unrelated dossiers.

12. Rebuild “Credit where due” around features not already praised in “The tell.” “Tactical historiography,” “historiographical balance,” and “analytical tools” read like assessment-report prose, and the Trotsky/coup/dual-power points have just been made. Credit concrete teaching decisions instead: for example, the Italian book’s paired-calendar dating and Constituent Assembly arithmetic, Lowe’s separation of February’s crowd from October’s planners, or Belarus’s clear diagram of dual power—whichever the source pages actually support. Keep the judgments tied to what a pupil can see or understand on the page.

13. Replace the first “Limits” paragraph with the neighbors’ more exact formulation: eight pinned editions, not eight nations, and not all eight are full internal narratives of 1917. “State-approved or dominant market editions” is a vague claim and makes Sudan’s passing influence passage sound equivalent in scope to Belarus’s full section. Also change the literal Markdown around “*История Казахстана*” to HTML italics when the chapter is revised.

14. Expand “Receipts” from a bibliography into an audit trail comparable to Chapters 5 and 7. For each book, name the exact section or claim supported; for out-of-scope volumes, give the relevant search stems or syllabus boundary; for Kazakhstan, record the national-history material that explains why it is tracked but not laddered. The chapter makes unusually fine claims about “coup,” “insurrection,” passive syntax, and Comintern dating, yet its present receipts give the reader only page ranges.

Verdict: This is currently an exhibit, not yet an essay; the single change that would help it most is a fully written thread built around the streetcars, the almost-bloodless takeover, and the epic image that followed.
