# Recommendations — Chapter 2, “Scramble for Africa”

1. Rewrite the lede’s formulaic last movement. “What the classrooms that cover those thirty years do with them is the subject of this chapter” repeats Chapter 1’s closing sentence and Chapter 3 repeats it again. Keep the Berlin room, but land harder: “Fourteen states are in the room. No African state is. By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia remain outside European rule. The comparison begins with who gets to be the subject of that sentence.” Then go straight into the question box.

2. Reorder “The boring facts” into actual chronology: 1869, 1881, 1882, 1884–85, 1896, 1898, 1899–1902, 1910, 1912, 1914. The present 1882-before-1881 shuffle makes the reader do needless sorting at the exact point where the chapter promises a ruler. Preserve every fact, but visually emphasize the three load-bearing turns—Berlin, Adwa, and the Mahdist state—so the list does not make every date look equally central.

3. Break the seven-row ladder into three visibly labeled bands without changing or shortening any quotation: “Africa becomes” (BY/UK/RU), “Europe acts” (IN/US/IT), and “An African acts” (SD). Coming straight from Chapter 1’s six-row table, another uninterrupted wall of quotations feels like the apparatus restarting rather than the book advancing. The bands would let the promised change in agency happen before the reader’s eyes.

4. Open “The tell” with the Sudanese homework question and the paragraph beginning “The Sudanese book is the only one that asks the student to pick the verb.” That is the chapter’s live wire. Compress “Every book on this ladder agrees…” and “The top three rows are a passive belt” into one short paragraph after it. Chapter 1 has already taught the reader how to inspect subjects and passive verbs; Chapter 2 should reward that knowledge with the startling classroom contrast, not reteach the method.

5. Cut the inventory in the Lowe paragraph—“no Berlin Conference in this section, no Leopold, no Congo Free State, no Fashoda, no Omdurman, no Menelik, no Mahdi”—from the main essay and leave it in “Receipts,” where it already appears. Keep the devastating scale comparison and bring it forward: Lowe gives the partition three sentences; the Sudanese book gives the Mahdist state fifty-five pages. That is more memorable than a second zero list and avoids replaying Chapter 1’s treatment of Lowe.

6. Split “Counting the dead sorts the books the same way” into two shorter paragraphs: first Adwa and the European-army counts, then Congo/Sudan and whose dead enter the record. Before publication, resolve the El Obeid chronology: the tell states “ten thousand Ansar killed in a single morning at El Obeid, 8 September 1882,” while the limits section itself notes conflicting lesson dates and a January 1883 surrender. Name the exact battle or episode only after it is verified; the chapter cannot build a numerical argument on a date it later destabilizes.

7. Repair the handoff in the Fashoda paragraph. “The consequence that matters for our next Part” skips over the actual next chapter, the Russo-Japanese War, and makes the thematic order feel accidental. Rewrite this as an explicit pointer to “the 1914 chapter,” then add one brief bridge to Chapter 3: the book now moves from a continent divided as territory to a war fought across Chinese territory while China is grammatically pushed aside.

8. Reorder “The thread” so the two Niger expeditions come before the long OpenStax quinine quotation. Open with the human experiment—about forty European deaths in 1841, none on Baikie’s 1854 voyage—then reveal the textbook passage that contains half the explanation. Keep the quotation whole and verbatim. In the current order, the reader meets another large textbook block immediately after surviving the ladder; in the revised order, the quotation arrives as evidence in a story already in motion.

9. Delete or rewrite “Chapter 1 ends with tea taken out of China and replanted in British India.” The chapter immediately before this one contains no such episode, so the callback makes the reader doubt their memory. Tie the paragraph to what Chapter 1 actually established: “Chapter 1 made India visible as the source of the opium sold into China. Here Asian colonies become the new source of an Andean drug. Empire’s map is also a supply chain.”

10. Rewrite the claim that “the men who acted on it in 1884 wanted what the men of 1584 had wanted.” It is too sweeping for a chapter otherwise careful about causation, and it collapses coastal trade, industrial demand, strategic rivalry, and territorial rule into one timeless motive. Preserve the important distinction by making the narrower claim: quinine did not create imperial ambition; alongside steam navigation and the new rules of effective occupation, it changed the cost and practical reach of inland conquest.

11. Move “Now the count. One classroom in eleven names quinine at all” to precede the tonic-water paragraph, and let “the drink outlived its job” close the thread. The section currently discovers a superb present-day object and then retreats to corpus bookkeeping. The recurring count belongs in the argument; the bottle on the reader’s shelf is the ending they will tell somebody about.

12. Cut “Credit where due” to three sharply differentiated sentences rather than three reprise paragraphs: Sudan for depth and African agency; OpenStax for naming exclusion and measuring the change from roughly 10 to 90 per cent; Sabbatucci–Vidotto for causal sequence. All the supporting evidence has just appeared in “The tell.” Repeating it here slows an already much longer chapter than Chapter 1 and turns an award into a second summary.

13. Do the Arabic page verification before publication, then reduce “Where this stops being true” from five paragraphs to two: one on corpus/syllabus limits, one on the limits of every book’s geographic viewpoint. Move extraction mechanics, PDF-page offsets, and the two textbook date anomalies into receipts or editorial endnotes. “The Sudanese lines above are the weakest evidence in this chapter” is responsible draft-room language, but in a finished pleasure-reading chapter it punctures the authority of the very exhibit on which the chapter depends.

14. Aim to remove roughly 700–900 words, almost all from duplicated analysis in “The tell,” “Credit where due,” and “Where this stops being true”; do not take them from the sacred quotations or the quinine narrative. Chapter 1 is about 1,600 words and this chapter about 3,700, with the still longer Chapter 3 immediately following. A leaner middle chapter would make the expanding scale feel intentional instead of like the house style has suddenly swollen.

Verdict: This is an essay wearing an exhibit’s uniform; moving the Niger–quinine story to the center and cutting the repeated apparatus would help it most.
