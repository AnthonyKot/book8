# Recommendations — Chapter 10, “Spain, 1936–1939”

1. **Give the title the chapter’s actual angle.** “Spain, 1936–1939” is an archive label for a chapter that is really about foreign intervention and its price. Retitle it **“Spain, 1936–1939: The Bombers and the Bill”**, or use that phrase as a deck. It joins the Guernica exhibit to the gold-and-credit thread before the reader has to discover that connection alone.

2. **Do not begin a second consecutive chapter “On [weekday] [date].”** Move the existing Padre Alberto de Onaindía pull quote—“Guernica had no anti-aircraft guns…”—intact to the top as a cold open, then compress the market-day chronology beneath it. Chapter 9 has just opened on Thursday 24 October; Monday 26 April makes the new event feel poured into the same mould. A witness’s plain inventory of what the town lacked is more human and immediately establishes why naming the target matters.

3. **Add the missing handshake from Chapter 9 to the lede.** After the Guernica scene, add one sentence in substance: **“The previous chapter ended with recovery arriving through military spending; Spain is where some of that money became aircraft, credit and practice.”** Chapter 9 explicitly promises Spain as the first place the coming war’s equipment and evasions were tested, but Chapter 10 currently resets the clock without collecting that promise.

4. **Rename “The boring facts” and remove its repeated instruction manual.** Use **“What happened”** and cut “Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.” The exact apparatus has just appeared in Chapter 9 and appears again in Chapter 11. Three long chapters of roughly equal length already risk feeling serialized by template; telling a pleasure reader that the factual entry is boring makes that fatigue worse.

5. **Replace the ladder’s vague single ranking with an explicit naming checklist, while keeping every quotation whole.** The introduction claims an ascent from “the least of the act named to the most,” but the tell later admits that Italy jumps rungs if agency rather than proper nouns is measured. Keep the six rows and sacred blockquotes, but label the comparison components—**aircraft/act, target, unit, date, decision-maker**—and either group the rows into “aid,” “air attack,” and “named raid,” or show those components in the tag. The reader should not have to wait until paragraph eight of the tell to learn that the ladder’s order changes with the criterion.

6. **Rewrite the opening of “The tell,” because “Nothing on this ladder is in dispute” is contradicted by the chapter itself.** The limits later show an order-of-magnitude dispute over Guernica’s dead and three incompatible Italian troop totals; the six books also do not all supply every proposition in “All six books say all of that.” Open more narrowly: **all six agree that foreign support shaped the war and that Franco won; they differ over whether the intervention acquires an aircraft, a target, a unit, a date and an authorizing voice.** That states only what the exhibit demonstrates.

7. **Compress the rung-by-rung reprise in “The tell.”** Merge the Belarus and Russia readings into a short movement from contentless “help” to aircraft without a target; keep Russia’s excellent “museum address” sentence. Pair Italy and OpenStax as the central complication—one names purpose without place, the other place without actor—then let Lowe and the German booklet complete the ascent. The current six consecutive country paragraphs make the reader reread the table before reaching the genuinely interpretive comparison.

8. **Cut the volunteer mini-ladder from the end of “The tell” as a rival exhibit.** Move the paragraph beginning “The same national reflex runs through the word *volunteer*” intact to “Receipts,” except for the Italian parenthesis correcting Mussolini’s “volunteers” to regular units, which can remain as one sentence beside the Italy analysis or credit. Russia’s country list, Lowe’s handler, OpenStax’s absent Americans and the second Guadalajara comparison are individually good, but together they restart the chapter around a new vocabulary test just when the Guernica ladder should be landing.

9. **Bring the Guernica death-toll disagreement forward from “Where this stops being true.”** Place the contrast between Lowe’s “1600 people” and the German booklet’s “several hundred” immediately after those two rows are interpreted, retaining both textbook quotations exactly. The chapter cannot first declare the exhibit undisputed and reveal several sections later that its most vivid number is contested. Keep the lower modern estimate and the 1937 Basque figure in the surrounding prose so the reader can distinguish a textbook’s wording from the book’s factual baseline.

10. **Open “The thread” with “The Republic paid in advance, in metal.”** Move the six-book catalogue beginning “All six covering books reach for the same verb” to the end of the thread or into receipts, with every quoted passage preserved intact. Coming after a six-row ladder and its prose replay, another multilingual quotation inventory feels like the apparatus starting for a third time. The gold leaving Cartagena is the chapter’s second unforgettable scene; begin there, tell the parallel cash-versus-credit story, and only then reveal that every classroom had called both transactions “help.”

11. **Reconcile the thread’s gold arithmetic and debt chronology before polishing its rhetoric.** The prose gives a reserve of about 635 tonnes, roughly 193 tonnes sent or sold through France, and roughly 510 tonnes shipped to Moscow; as presented, those quantities do not fit inside the stated total. The claim that Spain continued paying both German and Italian civil-war debts “into the 1960s” is still marked for confirmation. Verify whether the figures use gross weight, fine-gold weight, separate accounting periods or overlapping totals, then rewrite the paragraph so an adult reader can audit the transaction without consulting the comments. Replace “the shipment that paid for half the war” with the precise, sourced proportion it actually financed.

12. **Remove two flourishes that weaken the thread’s authority.** Rewrite “Say the causation exactly, because the punchy version is a slander” as the direct claim: **“The gold did not lose the Republic the war.”** Chapter 9 has just used “Say the causation exactly, because the punchy version is wrong,” so the echo sounds like house machinery. Also cut “thrown in parliament by people who could not date the shipment” and the unsupported sweep “a live argument in most of the countries in this book right now.” Keep the durable present-day connection: arguments over who controls national reserves held abroad still turn physical custody into political power.

13. **Compress the last three apparatus sections.** Reduce “Credit where due” to two or three one-sentence awards—Germany for the paired perpetrator/witness documents, Italy for correcting its own euphemisms, Russia for exposing the Popular Front’s internal struggle—and move detailed praise already made in the tell to receipts. In “Where this stops being true,” retain only the syllabus boundary, the disputed Guernica toll, and the crucial warning that none of the books supplies a Spanish institutional or village-level history; move spelling errors, Steinbeck verification and extraction workflow to receipts. Chapter 9 and Chapter 11 surround this one with the same credit/limits ceremony, so a full second recap here is where the book becomes most visibly formulaic.

14. **Let the gold thread become the final narrative movement and make it hand the reader to Munich.** Put compressed credit, limits and receipts before the thread; after the cash-versus-ore comparison, add a closing turn in substance: **“Spain had a Non-Intervention Agreement signed and broken in practice; the next chapter follows the same governments to Munich, where another agreement tries to purchase peace.”** Then retain the required final line, “Now look up what your textbook calls it.” The existing `.inherits` metadata says “Feeds: Munich,” but the prose never performs the transition.

**Verdict:** This chapter is an exhibit split by a rival essay; joining the Guernica naming ladder to the price hidden inside the word “aid” would help it most.
