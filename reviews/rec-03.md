# Recommendations for Chapter 3

1. **Give the chapter a title with a point of view.** “Russo-Japanese War, 1905” sounds like a filing label and is also less exact than the dates used everywhere else in the chapter. Retitle the display heading **“The War on Somebody Else’s Ground”** and put “Russo-Japanese War, 1904–05” in a subtitle or deck. That makes the chapter’s actual discovery—not merely the event—what pulls a pleasure reader in.

2. **Keep the Port Arthur opening, but replace its formulaic last sentence.** Cut “What six classrooms do with that is the subject of this chapter.” Chapters 2 and 4 make nearly the same apparatus-announcing move, so a reader can already hear the template. Rewrite the end of the lede to turn the chapter sequence: **“Chapter 2 watched imperial powers divide a continent. Here two of them go to war on land leased from a third—and most classrooms let the third country disappear.”** This makes the thematic, non-chronological order feel designed rather than merely indexed.

3. **Shorten “The boring facts” from eight bullets to six without losing facts.** Merge the 1895 Triple Intervention with the 1898 lease; merge the two 1905 aftermath bullets; move the sentence about abolition of the Chinese examination system to immediately before the NCERT/Chinese comparison in “The tell”; move the casualty-range sentence to “Where this stops being true.” The present list makes the reader retain two separate aftermaths before knowing which one the chapter will use.

4. **Turn “The two closest classrooms—and why they are off the ladder” into one clean missing-column exhibit.** Keep China directly after the table because its absence is the chapter’s central evidence, but cut the search diary beginning “Stems searched in the original” from this position and move it intact to “Receipts.” Move the Russia scope-boundary paragraph to “Where this stops being true.” A reader should meet the dramatic fact—five of six books narrate the war; the Chinese volume does not—in ten lines, not excavate it from a box of false-positive search results.

5. **Open “The tell” on the parenthesis, not on another grammar survey.** Cut “Every book here agrees on the result…” and begin with NCERT’s sacred sentence, “In 1905, just after the Russo-Japanese war (a war fought on Chinese soil and over Chinese territory)…” Then ask why the chapter’s largest fact is parenthetical. Chapter 2 has just spent pages on who occupies the subject position, and Chapter 4 will again read agency through verbs; beginning Chapter 3 with “the disagreement starts…in the sentence” makes three consecutive chapters feel machine-made.

6. **Compress the Belarus/Lowe/OpenStax/Sabbatucci discussion into one paragraph aimed strictly at the ground.** Preserve all quoted textbook wording, but reduce the current four-paragraph detour through who starts the war to a progression such as: Lowe supplies “South Manchuria”; OpenStax says Russia leased land “from China”; Sabbatucci–Vidotto show an empire being carved up; only NCERT calls it “Chinese soil.” The attack-without-a-declaration material belongs as a brief secondary finding, not as a rival ladder about first movers immediately before a chapter entirely devoted to first moves in July 1914.

7. **Move the Chinese/Indian examination-system comparison forward and make it the end of “The tell.”** The paragraph beginning “The clearest way to see the shape of the gap…” is the chapter’s strongest controlled comparison: same reform, same year, same historical ground, one book dates it by the war and the other does not. Place it immediately after the paragraph that ends “the classroom standing on it is the one that omits it,” then stop the section there. The current order buries the proof beneath the “white race,” “yellow peril,” and casualty digressions.

8. **Cut the casualty-count paragraph from “The tell.”** “One more count, because it sorts the books the same way the ground does” does not in fact sort them—none of the five prints casualties—and Chapter 2 has just made the sharper version of this point by showing whose dead get counted. If the zero matters methodologically, reduce it to one sentence in “Where this stops being true.” Do not ask a repeated device to impersonate a new finding.

9. **Choose one thread essay; the chapter currently contains two.** Keep the Tsushima → all-big-gun battleship → dreadnought race → Scapa Flow low-background steel line. Cut the second “landing” from “Now the other obsolescence…” through “the Persian Gulf has framed every decade since,” including the coal-to-oil excursus. That material is interesting, but after Chapter 2’s quinine story it repeats the same underlying essay—an imperial system discovers that logistics and technology determine reach—and it leaves the chapter’s Chinese-ground thesis out of sight for too long.

10. **Correct the surviving thread’s causal pitch in the prose, not merely in a fact-check comment.** Rewrite “Tsushima settled a war in about four hours and made two things obsolete at once: every battleship then afloat, and the fuel all of them burned.” The chapter later concedes that all-big-gun designs and Fisher’s oil advocacy predated the battle. Use the more defensible claim already implicit in the notes: **Tsushima spectacularly confirmed the all-big-gun argument; HMS _Dreadnought_ institutionalised it; the naval race followed.** Likewise replace “a design read off the Korea Strait” with “a design whose case the Korea Strait made impossible to ignore.” This strengthens the facts and removes the need for the familiar “the punchy version is false” self-correction that Chapter 4 repeats almost verbatim.

11. **Let the Scapa Flow object close “The thread.”** Move “Now the count, both barrels” to before the Scapa Flow paragraph and rewrite that phrase without the gun joke; then end on “It has never seen a bomb.” That is a memorable science-popular landing. The present statistical paragraph after it explains the magic away and closes on an insecure page reference to “Anglo-Persian oil interests.”

12. **Halve “Credit where due.”** NCERT’s paragraph repeats the chapter thesis almost word for word, and the Sabbatucci–Vidotto paragraph inventories points already made in “The tell.” Keep two compact awards: NCERT for naming the ground and Sabbatucci–Vidotto for supplying an actual campaign. Fold the Belarus domestic-motive credit into the compressed tell paragraph and the OpenStax continuity point into receipts. Four installments here feel less like fairness than a second conclusion.

13. **Make the handoff to Chapter 4 accurate and singular.** Cut “The arms race…is the subject of the next chapter”; it is not—the next chapter’s main exhibit is responsibility for the July Crisis and its thread is passports. Add one short final bridge before “Credit where due”: **Russia’s 1905 defeat sent its ambitions back toward Europe and left German planners treating it as the slow power; Chapter 4 begins inside those assumptions.** Do not develop the point here, because Chapter 4 already does; just make the reader feel the hinge.

14. **Reduce “Where this stops being true” to three jobs: corpus scope, the Chinese zero, and textual errors.** The first paragraph repeats the ladder box; the second restates the chapter’s whole argument; the fourth explains translation practice that the footer and receipts already cover; the fifth gives a generic “none of these books is a history” disclaimer. Merge scope and method into one paragraph, retain the precise limits on the Chinese zero, retain the OpenStax/Sabbatucci–Vidotto errors, and cut the generic closing disclaimer. This chapter is roughly half again as long as Chapter 2; most of the excess is apparatus explaining evidence the reader has already understood.

**Verdict:** This is an exhibit trapped inside an essay; foregrounding the NCERT/Chinese same-reform comparison and cutting the coal-and-oil second thread would help it most.
