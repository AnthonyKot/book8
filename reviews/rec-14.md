# Recommendations — Chapter 14, “The 1945–1949 order”

1. **Retitle the chapter around its actual discovery.** “The 1945–1949 order” sounds like a survey of the UN, Bretton Woods, decolonisation, Germany, China and the new security system; the chapter is really about where textbooks place the Cold War’s first move. Use **“The Cold War’s First Sentence”** or **“Where the Cold War Begins.”** This also differentiates Chapter 14 from Chapter 15: this chapter should ask where a narrative begins, while “Korea” asks who began a particular war.

2. **Make the lede a handoff from Hiroshima instead of a jump to Berlin followed by a rewind.** The current opening—“On 24 June 1948 the road, rail and canal links…”—creates a vivid blockade scene, but the facts immediately send the reader back three years, and nothing in the chapter collects Chapter 13’s claim that the bomb could also have been a message to Stalin. Begin in substance: **“In the previous chapter, one British textbook gave the bomb a second audience: Stalin. Eighteen pages later, the same book makes that warning an opening move in the Cold War.”** Then introduce Berlin as the place where rival opening stories become a physical division. Keep all the blockade facts, but stop making the reader enter 1948 only to be marched back to Yalta.

3. **Rewrite the question so Chapter 15 does not repeat it.** Replace “Who started the Cold War — and which event does your textbook put first?” with **“Where does your textbook begin the Cold War—with a speech, a new president, a Soviet design, or no first move at all?”** The present “Who started?” formulation is immediately followed by Korea’s “Who started it?”; two blame questions in succession make the later, sharper chapter feel like a rerun. A question about narrative sequencing fits this chapter’s ladder, title and best grammatical analysis.

4. **Rename and compress “The boring facts.”** Delete the house sentence “Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement,” which appears verbatim in both neighboring chapters, and call the section **“Fixed points”** or **“The common timeline.”** Reduce eight bullets to five by merging Yalta with Potsdam, Truman with Marshall, and Berlin with NATO/the two Germanies. Keep the UN, the People’s Republic of China and the Soviet atomic test visible: they are the facts Chapter 15 will actually inherit.

5. **Turn the ladder from a dubious ranking into three explicit kinds of beginning.** “Ordered from the thinnest account of how it froze” does not describe an intelligible scale: inevitability, reaction, presidential change and disputed blame are different causal models, not thicker and thinner versions of one thing. Keep every textbook quotation intact, but group the rows as **“No first mover”** (Russia, Ukraine), **“Speech and reaction”** (Belarus, OpenStax), and **“Plans, people and disputed blame”** (Italy, Lowe), with a one-sentence hinge between groups. Put the Russian edition caveat beside its row rather than waiting until Limits to reveal that the proper post-1945 companion volume is missing.

6. **Replace the opening of “The tell”; it is both formulaic and contradicted later.** “Nothing on this ladder is a disagreement about what happened” repeats Chapter 13’s “Nobody in this set disputes…” and anticipates Chapter 15’s “Every book here agrees on the calendar.” It also sits badly with the later three-zones/four-zones collision. Rewrite it as: **“The timelines overlap; their causal openings do not. Each book has to decide whether 1945–49 is a sequence of choices, a chain of reactions, or a structure that was already waiting to harden.”** That gives the section an argument rather than another announcement of consensus.

7. **Rebuild “The tell” by causal model, not by another country roll call.** The ladder has just taken the reader Russia → Ukraine → Belarus → United States → Italy → Britain; the prose then largely walks the countries again. Merge Russia and Ukraine into one passage on inevitability, Belarus and OpenStax into one on speech/reaction, and Italy and Lowe into one on deliberate policy and reopened blame. Preserve the fine readings of *predetermined*, *as is considered* and *systematically*, but let those words argue with one another in the same paragraphs. The chapter will become comparative prose rather than six miniature book reports.

8. **Keep the Marshall Plan convergence, but make it the tell’s single counterfinding.** The paragraph beginning “Then the convergence…” is excellent because four unlike publication systems unexpectedly agree about American economic benefit. Move it directly after the three causal-model passages and trim only the surrounding commentary, not any retained quotation. End on “What they disagree about is whether that is an indictment.” Cut the separate airlift-metaphor paragraph from the tell; move its intact linguistic examples to the start of the thread, where “air bridge” can lead naturally to the other piece of physical machinery, the iron curtain.

9. **Move the Ukrainian NATO pull quote later and correct the imagined reader.** The sentence “it ends in 2018, in the present tense, with the reader’s own country on the waiting list” is false for the adult reader of this book; it is the textbook’s Ukrainian student whose country is named. Rewrite it as **“with the country of the student holding that textbook on the waiting list.”** Place the intact pull quote after the causal comparison and Marshall convergence as the final proof that a starting point is chosen from a present, not from nowhere. Do not wedge it between the Lowe analysis and the Marshall Plan finding.

10. **Make China and India the forward turn into Korea; remove Sudan from this narrative passage.** The paragraph beginning “Finally, the space where the order is not built” contains the chapter’s best challenge to its Eurocentric title: China’s years are filled by civil war and state founding, while India reaches Yalta and Potsdam through Korean petitioners. Keep those two examples and rewrite the conclusion around them: **the same years that one shelf calls the making of a European order are, on another shelf, a civil war and a set of locked doors.** Cut the Sudan sentence here and leave it in the receipts; the Limits section admits that its underlying line is the chapter’s weakest evidence. Ending the tell on China and Korea also makes the next chapter feel caused rather than merely next.

11. **Let “The thread” be one essay—the iron curtain—not two naming histories plus a corpus audit.** Keep the fire-door origin, Snowden, the separate Goebbels usage, Churchill’s May telegram and Fulton. Reduce the Orwell–Baruch–Lippmann paragraph to two orienting sentences, or move its textbook quotations intact to the receipts; “Cold War” has its own etymology and currently starts a second essay just as the safety-curtain story catches fire. Likewise move the counts in “Who teaches the pedigree” to receipts. The thread should travel cleanly from theatre machinery to political metaphor to the working curtain a reader can still see today.

12. **Remove the unsupported transmission implied by “the phrase reached Fulton by way of a Nazi propaganda minister.”** The evidence presented establishes earlier and independent uses; it does not establish that Churchill acquired the phrase from Goebbels. Rewrite the claim as: **“Goebbels had used the same object for Soviet conquest months before Fulton; Churchill had used it privately in May. Fulton did not invent the phrase. It made one prior metaphor the name of an age.”** This is more precise and loses none of the disturbing historical proximity.

13. **End the thread on the living object, and replace or relocate the second image accordingly.** Cut the Brecht search-result flourish and the repeated zero counts from the final paragraph, then make “Anyone who has sat in an old theatre and watched a great iron slab go up has watched the Cold War’s name lift out of the way” the section’s last sentence. The Fulton screenshot currently appears after that essay, when its work is already done; move it beside the Fulton paragraph or replace it with a safety curtain in a working theatre. The latter would give the reader the chapter’s distinctive image rather than another lectern document.

14. **Collapse “Credit where due” and stop awarding the same podium three chapters running.** Chapters 13, 14 and 15 repeatedly give Lowe and OpenStax the lead and second nod, with Ukraine close behind; by the middle chapter, “earns the credit line / takes the second nod / depth lead” sounds like a recurring prize ceremony whose winners have already been announced. Reduce this section to one paragraph crediting practices rather than ranking books: Lowe for printing competing schools, OpenStax for testing its own doctrine in Greece, Ukraine for letting both sides state purposes, Italy for building a causal sequence. Cut all four mini-essays’ repeated plot summaries.

15. **Cut “Where this stops being true” by at least half and use the saved space for a clean exit to Korea.** Keep three things in the main narrative: the corpus/syllabus boundaries, the Russian-volume mismatch, and the Yalta three-zone/four-zone collision. Move page-number quality, the NCERT date error, inflation conversions, airlift tonnage, Sudanese OCR and archive desiderata to receipts or research notes; they do not qualify the central first-move argument. Before the receipts, add one brief handoff: **by 1949 the European line had hardened, China’s new government held no UN seat, and Korea had become two states—the arrangements Chapter 15 will turn into a war.** Preserve the required final line exactly: “Now look up what your textbook calls it.”

**Verdict:** This is an essay buried inside an overstocked exhibit; making the iron curtain’s physical and political journey the spine would help it most.
