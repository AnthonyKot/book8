# Recommendations for Chapter 5, “Versailles, 1919”

1. **Rewrite the lede as a direct handoff from Sarajevo, and take the delegations out of the same vaguely located scene.** The reader has just left Chapter 4, yet the excellent fact that the treaty was signed “five years to the day after Sarajevo” is buried in “The boring facts.” Open instead: “Five years to the day after the Sarajevo killings, two German ministers entered the Hall of Mirrors and signed a treaty they had been allowed to answer only in writing.” Then widen to Chinese delegates elsewhere in Paris, the rejected Japanese proposal, and Soviet Russia’s empty chair. This preserves the facts while avoiding the misleading impression that all three encounters occurred in “the same suburb.”

2. **Make the chapter’s promise less administrative and more human.** “What each classroom does with that room is the subject of this chapter” sounds like apparatus announcing itself. Rewrite the last lede sentence around the people: “Eight textbooks remember the closed door. They disagree about who was left standing outside it.” That is also cleaner than repeating the full premise in the question box and again at the start of “The tell.”

3. **Rebuild the ladder as three groups, because grievance is not a scalar quantity.** The announced order—from “inside the palace but outside the room” to “a continent’s worth of people”—does not explain why OpenStax’s Japanese and African cast precedes the China-only books, or why Russia occupies a middle rung. Keep every quotation intact, but add internal group headings: defeated states (UK, BY, IT); excluded powers and petitioners (RU, US); and the settlement viewed from China and the Arab world (IN, CN, SD). The reader can then compare kinds of exclusion without being asked to believe one is “more” excluded than another.

4. **Merge the first two paragraphs of “The tell.”** “Nobody on this ladder pretends the conference was open” and “Watch the slot” make the same discovery twice. Begin with the three matched grammatical constructions—Germans “not allowed,” the defeated “not represented,” Soviet Russia “not represented”—then land on “A bracket is a measurement.” That sharp line deserves to close the opening movement, not arrive after a rehearsal of the thesis.

5. **Cut the paragraphs beginning “The money sorts the books” and “Then there is the arithmetic of the men at the front” from “The tell.”** Reparations figures and the Big Three/Big Four crop are interesting findings, but neither answers whose exclusion a textbook treats as the injustice. The essential facts already remain in the scaffolding and receipts. These two excursions delay the far stronger sequence about Germany, China, the Arab world, and unnamed colonial petitioners.

6. **Use the Italian book’s two “humiliations” as the hinge of the section.** Move “One phrase in that manual does something none of the other seven books do” directly after the opening grammar comparison, and rewrite its first sentence as “The Italian manual gives the same word to two injured parties.” Its repeated running head—German humiliation, then Chinese humiliation—is the chapter’s best proof that the closed room can be seen from both sides. From there, move naturally into the Chinese and Indian accounts before returning to Lowe’s German courtroom.

7. **Tighten the China–India comparison by cutting audit language and letting the vocabulary carry it.** In “Search it: 凡尔赛 — Versailles — appears zero times in the volume,” retain the zero because it matters, but cut the ensuing inventory of every indemnity and the later single League mention. Pair the unnamed Chinese “peace treaty” with NCERT’s unnamed “post-war peace conference,” then stop on “Led by Britain.” The current paragraphs prove the point and then continue proving it.

8. **End “The tell” with the people in the building, not another textbook roundup.** The paragraph beginning “Which leaves the paragraph only one classroom prints” contains the section’s most physical scene: unnamed representatives arrive, receive no attention, and leave disappointed. Remove the scoreboard opening and use this as the culmination of the door image. Finish on “Nobody here writes the conference as one room with everybody’s absence in it.” That line gathers Germany, Russia, China, Japan, Africa, and the Arab delegations without flattening their claims into equivalence.

9. **Give “The thread” one afterlife, not two.** Keep the surprising Part XIII/ILO/eight-hour-day essay. Move the intact Sudanese mandate quotation and the minimum needed explanation into a short sidebar beside the Sudan passage in “The tell,” then cut the thread’s second run from “The second survival is one word” through the final mandate-system count. The mandate material repeats the preceding discussion and Chapter 2’s border argument; here it turns a brisk modern connection into a second essay competing with the first.

10. **Correct the thread’s rhetorical overclaim without losing its Tuesday.** “The eight-hour day, and the weekend on the far side of it, is a term of the settlement” and “the oldest clause of it still in force” blur the difference the preceding paragraph carefully establishes between Part XIII, the ILO, and Convention No. 1. Rewrite the payoff as: “The settlement created the institution that made the eight-hour day an international standard. For many readers, that standard still shapes an ordinary Tuesday—and the weekend on its far side.” This is more exact and more intimate.

11. **Move the corpus audit out of the thread’s ending.** Relocate “Zero of eleven classrooms connect the eight-hour day to the Treaty of Versailles” and the ILO stem-search detail to “Where this stops being true” or the receipts. End the thread on the revised Tuesday sentence. Chapter 4 also closes its thread with zero-counting; doing it again makes two consecutive chapters feel like reports rather than discoveries.

12. **Compress “Credit where due” to three brief judgments without rearguing the chapter.** The OpenStax paragraph repeats the racial-equality clause, African losses, and Ōkuma; the Sudan paragraph repeats the classroom activity; Lowe repeats the German case and mandate language. Retain all quoted textbook wording intact, but cut the surrounding recaps to two sentences per book: what the book risks, and what that risk lets a student see. At present this section is a second conclusion to “The tell.”

13. **Strip production housekeeping from “Where this stops being true.”** Move “Page conventions differ by book” wholly into the receipts, and put the repeated Arabic-extraction warning beside the Sudan citations rather than in another full paragraph; the reader encountered essentially the same warning in Chapter 4. Keep the substantive cautions—the eight-edition base, the mandate-table argument rather than “error,” and the attribution distinctions—but reduce the long San Remo/Smuts catalogue to the errors that actually affect this chapter’s interpretation.

14. **Write an explicit bridge into the book’s deliberate chronological backstep.** Immediately before the fixed coda, add a short paragraph such as: “One of the empty chairs in Paris belonged to a state that had not existed when the war began. The next chapter turns the clock back two years, to the two revolutions that put it there.” This makes the move from 1919 to 1917 feel designed rather than like a sequencing mistake, and gives the next chapter a stronger entrance than the navigation link alone.

**Verdict:** This is an essay, and cutting the mandate half of “The thread” would help it most by leaving one closed room and one startling modern afterlife in the reader’s mind.
