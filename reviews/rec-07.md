1. Rewrite the lede around one image, not two endpoints. Keep Wuchang—the engineering battalion turning its rifles on its officers is alive—but cut the coy “a man stands on a gate tower” and “the other government’s aircraft land in Taipei.” Name Mao Zedong, the People’s Republic, Chiang Kai-shek and the Republic of China when they first matter, then end the lede on the thirty-eight-year struggle over who could claim to be China. The current two-date construction is repeated immediately by Chapter 8’s Dandi/independence lede, where it works better.

2. Make the handoff from Chapter 6 explicit. Change “Inherits: Versailles and May Fourth (ch. 5)” to include 1917, and add a brief bridge before the facts or at the start of “The tell”: the previous chapter showed the Chinese textbook using October as the moment Marxism enters China; this chapter follows that imported vocabulary into the party founded in 1921. Without that bridge, the book’s two consecutive revolution chapters feel like separate dossiers, and the “Inherits” line oddly jumps over the chapter the reader has just finished.

3. Make 1911 earn its place in the title. After the Wuchang scene, state the through-line: the Qing collapse did not merely begin a sequence of events; it opened the unresolved contest over who could found, represent and name the republic. Return once to that contest when the Nationalists move to Taiwan. At present, 1911 is vivid in the lede and facts but disappears from the chapter’s actual argument, which is overwhelmingly about 1927–49.

4. Cut the final “boring facts” bullet—“All eight in-scope books in this corpus cover the period. That is a curriculum norm, not a finding.”—and leave that methodological point in “Limits.” Also shorten the ladder preface by removing its second recital of why Germany, Sudan and Kazakhstan are absent. The same exclusions appear again in “Limits” and in “Receipts”; three announcements before the evidence is bureaucracy, not orientation.

5. Rebuild the ladder so its comparison is visible without reading eight long blocks as prose. Keep every textbook quotation intact, but divide each row internally into “who starts it / what it is called / how it ends,” using a labelled break wherever the row joins passages from different pages. The present claim that the rows form a single scale “from the classroom where nobody starts the war” to the Chinese sentence is strained because several rows pair an origin sentence with a distant outcome sentence. A grammatical map will make the Chinese comma the culmination rather than simply the longest final row.

6. Correct the chapter’s two factual compressions before polishing. “Eight books, one date: on 1 October 1949 the Communists have the mainland and the Nationalists have an island” collapses a military process that was not complete on 1 October; distinguish the proclamation that day from the Nationalist government’s December move to Taipei. Later, “Five of the eleven narrate the Long March at all” contradicts “every covering book except Belarus and Ukraine” and the receipts, which identify six treatments: China, Britain, the United States, Italy, India and Russia. Recount once and use the same denominator throughout.

7. Cut “The tell” by roughly a third and give it one job: naming and grammatical agency. Retain the Chinese comma, the twenty uses of 内战, the Korea comparison, and the OpenStax pairing of “People’s Liberation Army” with the quoted “liberation of Tibet.” Move the Long March material wholesale into “The thread”; reduce the Russia and Belarus discussions to one short paragraph about endings hidden in biographies and subordinate clauses; remove the warlord-etymology paragraph from the main flow. At 6,471 words, this chapter is about 25 percent longer than the already substantial India chapter and nearly three times the unfinished 1917 chapter; the reader should not reach Chapter 8 feeling that every lexical curiosity must become a new exhibit.

8. Rewrite the universal claim “This is the distance rule of the whole project” as a finding limited to this comparison. Likewise, replace “proximity buys detail, and distance buys the count” with language that describes what these particular rows do. Chapter 8 immediately reuses “Distance again buys candour” while also presenting counterexamples; stating the rule as settled here makes the next chapter sound repetitive when it should complicate the pattern.

9. Delete the repeated Long March inventory from one of its two locations. “The tell” already lists Britain’s 20,000, Italy’s fewer than 10,000, India’s 6,000 miles, the US zero and China’s partial count; “The thread” opens by counting the same march again and later performs the full count a third time. Put the complete comparison only at the top of “The thread,” then move directly to Edgar Snow crossing the cordon with the invisible-ink letter. That is the chapter’s most human scene after Wuchang.

10. Verify or qualify the thread’s central source claim: “the itinerary in both of them … comes out of a single reporter’s notebook.” Lowe names Snow, but an Italian bibliography containing *Red Star Over China* does not by itself prove that Sabbatucci–Vidotto took its particular figures directly from Snow. If the research notes establish textual dependence, show the matching evidence; if they do not, rewrite this as a transparent inference. The chapter can argue that one account structured Western knowledge without claiming a provenance it has not demonstrated.

11. Cut the Halliday coincidence at the end of the Italian-bibliography paragraph: “One bibliography, both men, a quarter-century before one of them came for the other’s book.” It is clever but does not establish transmission, and “under Japan” makes the connection more accidental than revelatory. Use that space to explain how a pupil could—or could not—trace the displayed Long March numbers back to a source.

12. Keep the thread’s final turn—“The numbers never settled. The name did”—and the closing “off the map and into orbit,” but audit the sentences leading to it. “Every Chinese launch a reader has watched on the news since has ridden a rocket with the march’s name” is an unnecessary absolute, and “most phones sold in the last few years” will date quickly. Replace both with verified, durable formulations about the Long March launch family and BeiDou-capable devices. The facts stay strong; the prose stops gambling them on “every” and “most.”

13. Rewrite “Credit where due” so it does not summarize the two essays the reader has just finished. The Lowe paragraph repeats the 1927 count, contingency heading and Snow dispute; OpenStax repeats the liberation/Tibet comparison; the Chinese paragraph repeats the force numbers and “Left” errors. Keep the section but make it brisk—one concrete teaching choice per book—or credit different, source-supported virtues. Three reprises after such a long tell/thread feel like the chapter explaining its own explanation.

14. Reduce “Where this stops being true” to the interpretive limits: pinned editions, the Russian and Ukrainian chronological edges, and the absence of lived Chinese experience. Move page-offset conventions, machine-translation workflow, search stems and the list of individual textbook errors into “Receipts” or a shared method appendix. Preserve the errors and qualifications exactly as data, but stop making the pleasure reader traverse production notes before reaching the fixed coda.

15. Let the thread provide the chapter’s real ending. Either move “Credit,” “Limits” and “Receipts” ahead of “The thread,” or visually demote them as end matter so that “The name outran every one of them: off the map and into orbit” leads directly into “Now look up what your textbook calls it.” In the present order, the best landing is followed by roughly sixty lines of assessment and audit material. Also amend “Feeds” to name the immediate Chapter 8 handoff—agency in the verb—alongside the later Korea seam, so the thematic reading order feels chosen rather than merely non-chronological.

Verdict: This is an essay buried inside an overgrown exhibit; the single change that would help it most is to cut “The tell” back to the one-war/two-names argument and let the Snow-to-orbit thread carry the chapter home.
