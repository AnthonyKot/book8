# Recommendations — Chapter 15, “Korea”

1. **Cut the lede’s self-announcement and make the previous chapter cause this one.** “This is the chapter the whole book has been walking toward” tells the reader to feel a culmination the structure has not yet delivered. Replace that sentence with a handoff from Chapter 14: **“The postwar order of the previous chapter is about to become an army, a flag and a border that still divides families.”** The existing date, line and fallen-capital details are strong; the final sentence should turn the inherited order into consequences, not advertise the chapter’s importance.

2. **Rename and compress “The boring facts.”** Delete “Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement,” which the reader has just encountered verbatim in Chapter 14, and use **“The common timeline”** or **“Fixed points.”** Reduce eight bullets to five by merging Japanese rule with the 1945 division; the rival states with troop withdrawals; Incheon, Chinese entry and MacArthur into one campaign-turn bullet; and the armistice with the continuing absence of a treaty. Keep every fact, but stop making the final main chapter sound as though the house template has restarted.

3. **Replace the ladder’s “thinnest to thickest agency” ranking with three answers.** The same agency scale drove Chapter 14, so a reader arriving directly from it meets the same machine with new nouns. Keep every textbook quotation intact, but group the rows as **“No starter named”** (Belarus, India), **“Civil war, then American invasion”** (China), and **“North Korea crosses first”** (Ukraine, Italy, Lowe, OpenStax). The real comparison is not rhetorical thickness; it is the incompatible answer each framing gives to the chapter’s question.

4. **Move the Russian poster exhibit intact out of the ladder section.** After seven long rows, the wall-chart sidebar creates a second ending to the table and immediately launches the same grade-10/grade-11 caveat that returns in Limits—and that Chapter 14 has already explained. Put the complete poster quotation and its warning in Limits or Receipts under **“Non-peer exhibit.”** In the main comparison, one sentence is enough: the corpus lacks the relevant Russian narrative textbook. Do not let a source explicitly declared non-comparable stall the chapter’s central comparison.

5. **Replace the opening of “The tell,” which is virtually Chapter 14’s opening again.** “Every book here agrees on the calendar… The disagreement is entirely in one sentence” repeats the previous chapter’s “Nothing on this ladder is a disagreement about what happened… What does not line up is the first entry.” Rewrite as: **“At 25 June 1950, a textbook has three choices: leave the outbreak authorless, call it a civil war, or give the crossing an army and a verb. Each choice changes what the rest of the war can mean.”** This chapter then escalates the prior chapter’s grammar argument instead of replaying it.

6. **Rebuild “The tell” by those three choices, not by another country roll call.** The prose currently walks Belarus → India → China → Ukraine → Italy/UK/US immediately after the table has walked the same route. Merge Belarus and India into one passage on the authorless outbreak; use China as the hinge between internal war and external invasion; then compare the four books that name the northern crossing, distinguishing “began,” “invaded,” “without warning” and “confident of welcome.” Preserve all quoted textbook language verbatim, but make the words confront one another in the same paragraphs.

7. **Correct the overstatement in “the war’s Chinese name.”** The chapter’s own evidence distinguishes **朝鲜战争** (“Korean War”) from **抗美援朝战争** (“War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea”); the latter is the Chinese campaign/intervention name, not simply China’s alternate name for the entire war. Rewrite “And the war’s Chinese name is a verdict” as **“And the lesson’s name for China’s war is already a verdict.”** That makes the chronology’s use of both names more revealing, not less.

8. **Narrow the claim that Peng Dehuai points to one “specific earlier event.”** His “several hundred years” and cannon on an eastern coast evoke the longer history of Western gunboat power; the quotation does not itself identify the Opium War. Keep the pull quote completely intact, but rewrite “That is the Opium War (ch. 1)” as **“For this book’s reader, the image recalls the Opium War of Chapter 1.”** The backward connection remains powerful without making the textbook source say more than it says.

9. **Move “The dead are counted…” before the scare-quotes paragraph, and give it a humane landing.** Its current last line—“Belarus gives nothing, because Belarus has no room”—is glib beside millions of deaths and treats page space as the climax. End instead on the shared fact already established in the scaffold: despite the national differences in counting, most of the dead were Korean civilians. Then let “One habit is shared by everybody…” close the tell on the unresolved question of how the UN flag got over one army. The thread can answer the question in the very next line.

10. **Open “The thread” with its discovery, not a five-book thicket.** Move “Each classroom prints one gear. None of them assembles the machine” to the top. Then split the current first paragraph into three short evidence movements: OpenStax and Italy on the absent Soviet delegate; China on a manipulated Council without an empty chair; Ukraine and Lowe on China’s excluded seat and eventual stature. Keep every quoted passage and translation intact. The reader should see the machine before being handed all five loose parts.

11. **Make the empty chair the thread’s governing image and remove the false sweep of the newsfeed claim.** “Whenever a Security Council resolution ‘fails’… it fails because a permanent member voted no” is not true as written; a draft can also fail to secure the required nine votes. “The reason none of them ever leaves is a peninsula in 1950” also turns a lesson into an unprovable single cause. Rewrite the ending in substance: **when a draft has nine votes but a permanent member votes no, the chair is occupied; Korea taught the permanent members that absence does not veto.** End on that live institutional consequence, not on another corpus count.

12. **Make the photographs carry people, not merely decorate apparatus.** The opening refugee image is the chapter’s only immediate human scene, but the prose never looks at it; the second image arrives after the empty-chair essay with the meaningless caption “Korean War 03.” Move the second image beside the armistice/no-peace-treaty material or the lede, verify what the source actually depicts, and replace the file-title caption with that identification. Tie one sentence of surrounding prose to displacement or divided families. The chapter currently has more feeling for a Security Council chair than for the people the line divided.

13. **Collapse “Credit where due” to one paragraph crediting practices rather than awarding another podium.** Chapter 14 has just praised Lowe first, OpenStax second and Ukraine for balance; Chapter 15 repeats those three, then adds China, while retelling material already analysed in the ladder, tell and thread. Keep the substance—Lowe reopens causation, OpenStax corrects Washington’s suspicion, Ukraine sequences shared ambition before the crossing, China supplies a primary source—but state it once, without “credit lead,” “second nod” or “depth lead.” Move any retained quoted words intact rather than trimming them.

14. **Cut “Where this stops being true” by at least half.** The syllabus boundaries, missing Russian companion volume and poster caveat have already appeared in this chapter and in Chapter 14. Keep one compact paragraph on the seven-book scope and Russian gap, then one on edition/translation limits. Move the Kazakhstan deportation/Samsung excursion to the receipts: it is interesting, but it sends the book sideways just as the final chapter should be closing. Likewise move page-number and OCR cautions into the source notes rather than making them narrative prose.

15. **Give the final main chapter an actual book-ending paragraph before the fixed coda.** At present the strongest prose ends with Security Council procedure, then the chapter drains through four credit paragraphs, five limitations and ten receipts. After the receipts, add a short synthesis that joins the Chapter 1 cannon, the Chapter 14 postwar order and Chapter 15’s empty chair: a national name can carry an old historical account, while an omitted institutional fact can explain the flag under which the new war was fought. Then retain the required final line exactly: **“Now look up what your textbook calls it.”** That will earn the lede’s claim that the book has been walking here.

**Verdict:** This is an essay buried inside a repeated exhibit; making the empty chair the chapter’s spine would help it most.
