1 Three answers

Seven classrooms teach this war, and they agree on that calendar almost to the week. They split three ways on one sentence — the one where the war acquires a subject. Not thicker and thinner rhetoric: three incompatible answers to the question the chapter opened with.

2 The tell

At 25 June 1950, a textbook has three choices: leave the outbreak authorless, call it a civil war, or give the crossing an army and a verb. Each choice changes what the rest of the war can mean.

Two books take the first choice, for two different reasons. Belarus removes the sentence: the war appears once, mid-paragraph, as an item in a list of things that flared up in “the countries of Asia, Africa and Latin America,” alongside Vietnam and the Middle East. No June, no parallel, no Kim, no MacArthur, no dead — and the name is uncapitalised, корейская война, a common noun rather than a proper event. The verb that does the work in that paragraph belongs to the superpowers, and it is a verb of restraint: they “fought each other indirectly rather than directly.” India keeps the intransitive verb — the war “broke out,” the way weather does — and then changes the subject. Its Korea pages sit inside a chapter on paths to modernisation, and what the war is for there is stated plainly: it “caused not only massive losses of life and property, but also a delay in free-market economic development and democratisation.” One classroom has no room for the question; the other asks a different one, about what a war costs a developing economy. Neither student is asked to wonder who fired.

China takes the second choice and the third in the space of two clauses. The outbreak is 朝鲜内战 — the Korean civil war, an internal matter with no foreign author; only Belarus’s one-clause list leaves the start with less of one. Then the grammar turns, and for the rest of the paragraph the United States is the subject of every verb: it 操纵 (manipulated) the Security Council, 越过 (crossed) the parallel, 侵略朝鲜 (invaded Korea), 侵入 (intruded into) the Taiwan Strait, and at the end 不得不 — had no choice but to — sign. The lesson’s summary box on the previous page has already named the thing 美国发动的侵朝战争: “the war of aggression against Korea launched by the United States.” The start is authorless; everything after it has one author.

And the lesson’s name for China’s war is already a verdict. 抗美援朝 — resist America, aid Korea — names the Chinese campaign, not the whole war, and it is two verbs and two objects. The event keeps its neutral name; China’s war inside the event carries the finding in its title. The book prints both, one line apart, in the chronology at the back: 「朝鲜战争结束，中国人民取得抗美援朝战争的胜利」 — “the Korean War ended; the Chinese people won the victory of the War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea.” A student who learns the second name has learned who was resisting and who was aggressing before any paragraph opens.

（抗美援朝战争的胜利）雄辩地证明：西方侵略者几百年来只要在东方一个海岸上架起几尊大炮就可霸占一个国家的时代是一去不复返了。

“(The victory of the War to Resist US Aggression and Aid Korea) proves eloquently that the era is gone for ever in which, for several hundred years, Western aggressors could seize a country merely by mounting a few cannon on a coast in the East.”

CN · 彭德怀, quoted in the 史料阅读 source box · 中外历史纲要 上, p. 175

That source box is the hinge of this chapter, and it points backwards. Peng Dehuai measures 1953 against several hundred years of cannon mounted on an eastern coast; for this book’s reader, the image recalls the Opium War of Chapter 1. Korea is not filed in this textbook under “Cold War.” It is filed under the closing of an account.

Four more books give the crossing an army and a verb, and then disagree about what a crossing is. Ukraine writes it in two verbs: North Korean troops “crossed the 38th parallel and began a war” — розпочали, which does not describe a movement but assigns authorship — after first distributing the motive evenly between “both regimes.” Italy writes an invasion with a run-up and a quartermaster: frontier incidents, rising tension, and forces armate dai sovietici, armed by the Soviets. Lowe writes an adverb, “Without warning,” and then takes it back for two pages, running the origin as an open question with five suspects — Kim’s own idea, Dean Acheson’s speech leaving Korea off the American defence perimeter, the new government in Beijing, Stalin testing Truman, and, on its own line and without irony marks, “the communists claimed that South Korea had started the war.” OpenStax names the unit, the date and the mood — “confident of welcome” — and then removes its own most convenient villain, since Stalin “had in fact warned Kim against it” and “the Soviet Union sent nothing.” One army, four different events: a crossing that assigns responsibility, an invasion with a supplier, a surprise attack whose origin is contested evidence, and an army that expected to be greeted.

The dead are counted the same way — by whose they are. Ukraine gives one number and it is Chinese: Kim Il Sung’s government “was saved at the cost of lives of 1 million Chinese soldiers.” Lowe gives “about four million Korean soldiers and civilians… and five million people were homeless.” OpenStax gives “approximately three million people… most of them Korean civilians,” and calls the war “futile.” India gives “massive losses of life and property.” China, whose volunteers did much of the dying, gives no figure at all; its casualty paragraph is a paragraph about what the victory produced — prestige, standing, unity at home. Belarus, one clause long, gives nothing. Under all five ways of counting sits the same fact, and only the American book prints it: most of the people killed were Korean civilians, in a war that has never been ended by a treaty. Ukraine is the only classroom that tells a student so, in the present tense, in a 2019 schoolbook: «Хоча його вдалося зупинити, сторони досі перебувають у стані війни» — “although it was possible to stop it, the parties are still in a state of war.” Families separated by the fighting were separated by an armistice line afterwards, and stayed that way.

One habit is shared by everybody, in mirror image: the scare quotes. China quarantines “联合国军” — the so-called “United Nations Force.” Lowe quarantines the Chinese troops “described as ‘volunteers’.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto do the same with “volontari”. Ukraine sends its southern coalition in «під прапором ООН», “under the flag of the UN,” a phrase doing the work of a quotation mark. Nobody here lets the other side keep its own word for its own soldiers. And no book stops to say what put a UN flag over one of the two armies in the first place.

3 Credit where due

The last accounting

Lowe writes “Without warning” and then spends two pages unwriting it — five competing explanations for the attack, the Acheson speech that may have invited it, and, printed without irony marks, the communist claim that the South started it: a textbook aimed at exam candidates telling them that the origin of a war is contested evidence rather than a fact to memorise. OpenStax subtracts the convenient answer, recording that Washington suspected a Kremlin operation and was wrong — Stalin “warned Kim against it,” told him to ask Beijing rather than Moscow, “sent nothing” — and it is the book that spells out how the Council could act at all. Гісем–Мартинюк writes the harder sentence: both governments given the same ambition before anyone is named as having moved, and then the naming happens anyway. 纲要 puts a primary source on the page where nobody else does — Peng Dehuai’s own words, the commander named, the campaigns counted, the Seventh Fleet in the Taiwan Strait explaining why Korea mattered to the state teaching it — in a lesson whose title has already returned the verdict. Candour and verdict are not distributed by side; they turn up in the same books, sometimes on the same page.

4 Where this stops being true

Limits

Seven books teach this war; four never reach it, and none of the four is avoiding it. Russia’s volume here is the grade-10 unified course covering 1914–1945, and the grade-11 companion that would carry June 1950 is not on our shelf; Germany’s is a federal civic booklet on National Socialism; Sudan’s secondary syllabus ends in 1948; Kazakhstan’s is a national-history course, not a world-history one. Each of those four stops before 1950 or was built to tell another kind of history; a silence like that is a syllabus boundary, not a verdict. The Russian gap is the sharpest limitation here, and the revision poster quoted at the end does not close it: a wall-chart answer key and a narrative textbook are different objects. The honest statement is that we do not know how the current Russian classroom narrates June 1950, only how its revision poster labels it.

The editions are pinned and they are not contemporaries: Italy 2004 is a university manual, the British book is 2013, Ukraine 2019 — and that book’s 2018 Trump–Kim paragraph reads differently now than it did when it was printed. None of these books is a military history; for the Pusan perimeter or the air war over the Yalu, every column here will disappoint.
