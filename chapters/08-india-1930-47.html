India 1930–1947

1 The rice that could not be bought

On the morning of 6 April 1930, at Dandi on the Gujarat coast, a sixty-year-old lawyer walks into the shallows and lifts a lump of natural salt out of the mud. He set out twenty-four days earlier with seventy-eight others; the road behind him is now full. Picking up that salt is a criminal offence, because in British India salt may be produced and sold by one party only. The road stays full, on and off, for seventeen more years.

The salt law was about access: a thing nobody can live without, and one party permitted to supply it. Thirteen years later the same question came back to the same empire with no monopoly to blame it on.

A nine-year-old at a school in Santiniketan, a hundred miles north of Calcutta, spent the autumn of 1943 watching people walk in from the villages of Bengal to die. Nothing visible explained it. The rice harvest had not collapsed, no army had cut the province off, and rice was on sale a district or two away at prices the arrivals could not pay. Amartya Sen spent his working life on that gap, and the answer he arrived at is now the standard way any food crisis is read.

Here is the machine. Japan takes Rangoon in March 1942 and then Burma — the world’s largest rice exporter, and the source of a modest but decisive share of what eastern India ate. With the Japanese now at Bengal’s frontier, the colonial administration applies a denial policy to the coastal districts: country boats are confiscated or destroyed by the tens of thousands, and rice stocks above a household allowance are removed, so that an invader would find neither transport nor food.

Those boats were also how Bengali fishermen fished and how rice moved between districts. At the same time the war has its own queue — Calcutta’s industries, the army and the theatres outside India are provisioned first, grain prices are left to the market, and shipping is allocated elsewhere. Prices go up several times over and stay up. Refugees arrive from Burma; a cyclone hits the delta in October 1942.

What kills is none of these singly. A labourer, a fisherman or a barber holding money in a rising market can no longer buy rice that physically exists a district away. Between two and three million people die, most of them in 1943, most of them not of hunger alone but of what follows hunger. It was not a harvest failure, and it was not a planned massacre. It was a priority hierarchy operating as designed.

Sen’s answer to what he watched was that famines are failures of entitlement: of what a person can command, rather than of what exists. The idea took the Nobel in economics in 1998. It is why governments now keep their food position the way a treasury keeps reserves: months of consumption in store, a procurement price, an export ban when the number falls. The machinery exists so that a crisis shows up on a ledger before it shows up in a death rate.

Entitlement sounds abstract until it is put back into that Bengal market. The rice has not vanished. What has vanished is the practical claim that a day’s work, a catch of fish or a haircut once placed on it. The failure happens between a full sack and an empty hand. That is why counting grain alone could not explain what the schoolboy at Santiniketan saw.

Rationing reached Calcutta in 1943 and never left. If you grew up in India, the ration card in the kitchen drawer and the fair-price shop it is spent in are the working remains of that wartime apparatus: the Public Distribution System issues entitlements to several hundred million people, out of buffer stocks, at a fixed price.

Everywhere else the same machinery sits one step back, in the price on the bag. When India stopped most of its rice exports in July 2023, the world rice price hit a fifteen-year high, and importing shelves followed. The prices that did the killing in Bengal were wartime prices in a world market. Salt made control visible in 1930 because Gandhi could hold it up. Rice made the same problem harder to see in 1943 because the food was still there.

2 The dates, briefly

  • 13 April 1919: troops fire on a penned crowd at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar.
  • 12 March – 6 April 1930: the Salt March runs from Sabarmati to Dandi.
  • August 1942: the Quit India resolution; Congress leaders are jailed.
  • 1943: famine in Bengal kills between two and three million people.
  • 16 August 1946: “Direct Action Day” and the Calcutta killings.
  • 15 August 1947: British rule ends; India and Pakistan are independent.
  • 17 August 1947: the Radcliffe boundary award is published.

3 Seven classrooms, two missing stories

Now put this history in front of a fifteen-year-old. Seven books in this comparison reach India. Four make Britain the grammatical giver of independence: the United States says Britain “granted” it; Russia says Britain “took the decision to grant” it; Ukraine has the colonial administration announce the granting; Britain has Bevin and Attlee decide to give it. Belarus says India and Pakistan “attained” independence. India’s own timeline says they “become independent.” Only the Italian book reverses the movement: Congress wrests the promise from English hands.

The British book sees the argument inside its own grammar. Norman Lowe first gives the official account, then prints the reply: “Some Indian historians, including Sumit Sarkar and Anita Inder Singh, have challenged this view, arguing that Indian independence was never a long-term goal of the British… Independence was not a gift from the British, it was ‘the hard-won fruit of struggle and sacrifice’.” UK · Norman Lowe, Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed. (2013), p. 534 Yet the same book has no Amritsar, Rowlatt Act, Salt March or Quit India. It names the struggle and removes the road that would let a pupil see it.

Each of the others leaves a different shape. Russia describes Amritsar most directly and follows the methods of civil resistance, then hands the outcome back to London and ends by saying England left India democracy. OpenStax explains the salt monopoly, the march, the arrests, the wartime famine and the 1946 naval mutiny, yet still makes Britain the giver. Italy gives Amritsar its own heading and Congress the active verb. Ukraine counts about 100 thousand dead in the population exchange; Belarus gives neither the massacre’s place nor the partition toll. Four of the seven books give no partition death toll. Only OpenStax and Lowe mention the Bengal famine, and neither places it inside the independence story.

The most startling blank is openly marked. India’s NCERT volume is a world-history book whose front matter tells pupils that South Asian history belongs to the following year. Its timeline compresses the movement into two cells and omits Amritsar, the Salt March, Jinnah, Mountbatten, partition and the Bengal famine. But it also supplies the best instruction for reading every book here: “So we need to read each timeline critically, see what it tells us and what it does not. Timelines frame history in particular ways.” IN · NCERT, Themes in World History, Class XI, post-2022 rationalised, Reprint 2026–27, p. viii The ladder’s verbs tell one story about who ended empire. The missing roads, rifles, borders and rice tell another.

4 Credit and limits

NCERT deserves credit for declaring its boundary and teaching the pupil to treat selection as evidence; OpenStax is the only book here that shows how the movement worked. But none of the blanks is automatically a refusal. Four corpus books are outside Indian history altogether, and only two of the seven in-scope books mention the famine, making that omission a curriculum norm rather than a national avoidance signal. None is a history of India, and no classroom can settle the partition toll that scholars themselves dispute.
