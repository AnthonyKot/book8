Front matter · Introduction

Eleven classrooms, one world

Put eleven school physics textbooks on a table — Minsk, Moscow, Almaty, Kyiv, Beijing, Delhi, Khartoum, Berlin, Rome, London, Houston — and open them all to falling bodies. They are near-interchangeable. The same constant to the same number of decimal places, the same diagram of a ball and a feather in a vacuum tube, the same worked example with the same arrows. A student trained on any one of them could sit the exam written for any other and pass it. Now open the history books to the same morning — to a beach near Guangzhou in 1839, to the last week of July 1914, to the twenty-fifth of June 1950 — and the agreement stops. Not slightly. Completely, and in a patterned way, so that you can predict from the cover which sentences will go missing inside.

That gap is what this book is about. It is worth saying at the outset what it is not: it is not eleven degrees of error around one correct account that a better textbook could reach. Nobody is wrong about who fell. The dates hold, the treaties were signed on the days they were signed, the ships sank where they sank. What differs is who gets to be the subject of the sentence, what is left out without being denied, and — hardest to see, because it lives between chapters rather than inside them — which event is allowed to have caused which. A physics textbook has one job. A history textbook has that job and a second one: it has to tell a country's fifteen-year-olds who they are. The second job is where the eleven books part company, and it is the subject here.

One material world

There is a floor under all this, and it is made of matter. The silver actually sailed: it left Europe and the Americas, crossed to Canton, and bought leaves. Nitrogen was actually pulled out of the air over Germany and turned first into explosive and then into fertiliser, and the two are the same industry. Gold actually left the vaults in Madrid on trucks and arrived in Odesa. A hull displaces the same water in every language. A ton of ore weighs the same in the ministry-approved book and in the market paperback. Matter has no nationality — which is exactly why it makes such a good instrument. When eleven accounts of a war differ, one of two things is happening: they are describing different things, or they are describing the same thing differently. Anchor the story to something physical, and only the second remains.

So every chapter in this book follows a thing rather than an argument. A leaf. A bark that made a continent's interior survivable for the people invading it. A railway timetable. A fixed nitrogen atom. A bar of gold with a serial number. A card index. A theatre's fire curtain. The thing holds still, and eleven tellings move around it, and the movement is legible because the thing is not. That is the method, and it is also the reason the book is readable at breakfast: the tea is in the cup before the argument arrives.

How it started: one morning, five books

The first thing I looked up was a single date — 25 June 1950, Korea — and the plan was to note down what each book called the war and move on. Norman Lowe's British paperback opens the account with three words, "Without warning," and North Korean troops invading South Korea. The Ukrainian book has them "crossed the 38th parallel and began a war," which puts the line on the map at the centre of the sentence. The Italian manual has North Korean forces, armed by the Soviets, who "invaded the South" — the supplier arrives in the same clause as the act. The Chinese lesson begins that morning with a country doing something to itself: 朝鲜内战爆发 — "the Korean civil war broke out" — and the crossing of the 38th parallel happens later in the paragraph, done by the Americans, on their way to invading Korea. And the Indian book has nobody crossing anything at all: "In June 1950, the Korean War broke out," the way weather breaks. Five accounts of one sunrise. Not one of them is refutable on the facts it prints. I stopped taking notes on the war and started taking notes on the sentences, and this book is what came back.

The twelfth narrator

You should know who is talking. This book was written by an AI, which read all eleven textbooks and is therefore the twelfth narrator in the room — the only one without a country. No ministry approves these pages. No childhood classroom left me a map that still feels like the shape of the world. That is a stance, and it is worth having, but it is not a claim to neutrality and should not be read as one: a language model is assembled out of human writing, most of it in a few languages, and it carries the tilt of what it was made from. There is no version of me that stands outside the argument.

Which is why the book is built so that you never have to take my word for anything. Every claim about a textbook comes with the passage it rests on, its edition and its page, in the original where the original is the finding and in translation for reading, so you can look from the claim to the evidence and back again. If a sentence here says a book softens something, the softened sentence is printed underneath it and you can decide whether that is what it does. Where a quotation rests on a damaged extraction rather than a clean page, the chapter says so beside it. Trust the page, not the voice describing it.

Three pieces of furniture

Three things repeat in every chapter. The ladder is one event with one row per classroom, ordered by grammar rather than by geography: it runs from the accounts where nothing has a subject — wars that "flared up," treaties that were "concluded" — down to the ones where a commissioner stands on a beach at Humen with twenty thousand chests of opium, or a parliament votes. Read one top to bottom and you are watching agency get handed out. An empty rung usually marks the edge of a syllabus rather than a guilty silence: a book that stops in 1948 is teaching a different course, not avoiding Korea. The receipts come at the end of each chapter, the editions and the pages, because most of these books are findable, several are free, and every one of them is more interesting than any description of it — if a chapter makes you want to argue with it, the thing to argue with is on the page number given. And every chapter closes on the same coda: Now look up what your textbook calls it. The ladders have eleven rows; yours is the twelfth, and nobody else can print it. Whatever you were handed at fifteen has a wording for these events, and that wording is the same kind of evidence as everything else on the page. The line is not an accusation aimed at your schooling — it only works if you still trust your own book enough to go and open it, which is why nothing in these chapters sneers at anybody's.

What you are in for

The tea in a great many kitchens is on the far end of the Opium Wars: Britain fought to keep buying a Chinese leaf and then arranged never to have to buy it again, and the taste of Darjeeling is the evidence. The Iron Curtain was fire-safety equipment for a century and a half before it was a border — a slab of iron that drops in front of a burning stage, standard in Vienna's theatres after a catastrophic night in 1881, and anyone who has sat in an old auditorium and watched it lift has watched the Cold War's name get out of the way. And the flag the Korean War was fought under exists because of an empty chair at a table in New York: the Soviet delegate was boycotting the Security Council over who should hold China's seat, and so the resolutions passed. The chair did not cause the war. It decided what the war was called, and under whose colours it was fought — a distinction this book will make more than once, because promoting a precondition into a cause is the most common way an honest sentence goes wrong.

Next, the eleven themselves. It is worth meeting them as voices before they walk into a chapter and start arguing with each other over a beach, a border, a morning.
