2 The ladder

Five classrooms enter Petrograd and tell you what happened there. Three never enter it at all, and meet 1917 only after it has travelled abroad. The second group is not giving a low-agency account of the night; it is giving no account of the night, because the night is not what 1917 is for in those classrooms.

3 The tell

Put the two loudest sentences side by side. Minsk: “The October Revolution of 1917 became the most important event in world history.” London: “It was almost a bloodless coup.” They are describing the same hours, and neither is exaggerating its own evidence — the Belarusian book says on the page before that the Red Guards met no resistance, and Lowe says the plans “went off without a hitch,” which is a compliment to the planners and a demotion of everyone else. The gap between “most important event in world history” and “almost a bloodless coup” is not a gap about facts. It is the gap this chapter is about: an event too small to look like its own consequences, and five classrooms choosing a noun to bridge it.

Each noun hands the steering wheel to somebody different. Revolution gives it to a population: something a society does. Insurrection gives it to an organised minority acting against a standing authority — an operation with a commander, which is why the Italian book's very next move is to name one. Coup gives it to a small group at the top and, by implication, takes it away from everyone else in the country; a coup is something done to a state, not by a people. Three nouns, one night, and the difference between them is the whole argument about what the Soviet state was allowed to claim afterwards.

Russia's volume declines the choice by not making October a separate event. February and October sit under one numbered heading — “The Great Russian Revolution of 1917” — so there is no second thing to name. The syntax then does something more precise than hiding: the actors are on the page, but never in the driver's seat of the sentence. The Provisional Government is the grammatical subject of its own overthrow; the left-radical socialists arrive in a by-phrase; the transfer of power to the Soviets is not taken, it “was proclaimed,” with nobody proclaiming it; and in the sentence that makes Lenin head of government, the office comes first and the man arrives at the end, after his job title. Read it twice and you can name everyone involved. Read it once, at the speed a student reads, and what you remember is a government to which things happened.

Belarus does the opposite and does more work. Its § 14 runs the whole year as one teaching sequence, and closes on a world-historical verdict and Bernard Shaw's admiration for the Soviet system after his 1931 visit. Notice what that sequence has to carry on the way. It prints an election in which the Bolsheviks came second — Socialist Revolutionaries 40.4 per cent, Bolsheviks 24 per cent, liberals 7.5 per cent — and it concludes on the same page that the October Revolution “reflected the interests of broad strata of the population.” The evidence and the verdict are four lines apart, and a student is expected to hold both.

Italy and the Anglophone books change register from history to operations. Sabbatucci–Vidotto's Trotsky is “the organizer and the military mind”; OpenStax's Lenin “led a coup and seized power from the other political factions in Petrograd” — from the other factions, not from the country, which is a smaller and more accurate claim than it looks. The American book alone attaches a foreign hand to the story before the night: “Germany helped him return.” No book on this ladder denies any of the others' facts. They are choosing which verb the facts hang from.

And then there are the three books that never come to Petrograd. In Khartoum the revolution is an impact “calling for freedom and calling for struggle and liberation,” one item in a list of things that pushed the Sudanese national movement into a new phase; it has no city, no date beyond the year, no Lenin. In Beijing it is a timestamp on the arrival of an idea: Marxism spreads in China “after the October Revolution,” and three years later Comintern representatives sit down with Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao. In Delhi it is a cell in a timeline and, forty-one pages away, a “Russian success” that “exercised a powerful influence around the world.” For all three, 1917 is the thing it did to them. What none of the three needs is the night itself. The image of the revolution travelled to places the event never reached — and for three of these eight classrooms, that travelling is the whole of 1917.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Koshelev (BY) takes it, from the column with the strongest verdict in the corpus, and takes it for what is printed after the verdict. Two lines below “The October Revolution of 1917 became the most important event in world history,” on the same page, the book sets its class this exercise: «Почему это событие описывают и оценивают разные люди совершенно по-разному?» — “Why do different people describe and evaluate this event completely differently?” A boxed note on the same page then tells students that 7 November was the state holiday of the USSR, that the Russian Federation has marked 4 November instead since 2005, that Russian media and the public are of two minds about the change, and asks: «Как вы считаете, является ли день 7 ноября достойным памяти?» — “In your view, is the day of 7 November worthy of memory?” A pupil can see both of those on one page: a verdict, and the book's own admission that the verdict is contested and that the calendar has already moved. Whatever else this column is doing, it hands a fifteen-year-old the question this book is made of, and does not answer it for them.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight named editions, not eight countries, and the eight are not doing the same job. Five narrate 1917 from inside; three carry the year in a sentence or a timeline cell, and putting Sudan's two lines beside Belarus's six-page section as if they were rival accounts of the same night would be a category error. That is why they are in separate movements above.

Two volumes cannot reach 1917 at all: Ukraine's 11th-grade textbook begins in 1945, and Germany's bpb booklet is about National Socialism and the war. Their silence is a syllabus boundary, not a choice about Petrograd.

Kazakhstan's 11th-grade История Казахстана is a national history rather than a world history, so it is not laddered beside the others — but what it does with 1917 is worth reading, and it is quoted below.

The last section of this chapter is the part of it not built from textbooks. Nikolai Evreinov's 1920 re-enactment on Palace Square and Sergei Eisenstein's 1928 film are general history, marked for checking, and the claim made about them is deliberately narrow: they produced the surviving imagery, not the outcome. How much of any individual reader's mental image comes from Eisenstein rather than from illustrations, later films or nothing in particular is not measurable, and this chapter does not pretend to measure it.

One quoted error stands as printed: NCERT dates the founding of the Comintern to March 1918. The Third International was founded in Moscow in March 1919. And the Sudanese sentence is reconstructed from an Arabic extraction whose ligatures do not survive machine reading; the printed page, not the file, is the authority for it.

Page numbers follow each book's own printing where it has one, and the file's where it does not — which is why Lowe is cited by section and PDF page.
