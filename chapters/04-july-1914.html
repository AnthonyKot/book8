Who started 1914

1 The crossing

On 28 June 1914 a car takes a wrong turn in Sarajevo, reverses, and stops in front of a nineteen-year-old with a pistol. That accident explains the murder. It does not explain how the pistols reached Sarajevo, what the frontier was looking for, or why a traveller’s face would soon become an object for governments to file.

Gavrilo Princip and the other conspirators were Bosnian Serbs, and therefore subjects of the empire whose heir they meant to kill. The weapons came from Serbian military stocks. Sympathetic Serbian frontier officers helped move the young men, their pistols and their bombs over the Drina at night, through an underground channel. The weapons had to be smuggled. The travellers did not have to evade a passport check, because the border asked arriving travellers for no papers.

That sounds like a hole in an otherwise modern border. It was closer to the European norm. Across much of western and central Europe, customs officers routinely inspected goods while ordinary travellers crossed without passports; the requirement had lapsed during the nineteenth century, and the conspicuous exceptions were the Russian and Ottoman empires: papers were needed to leave them and, in Russia, to move about inside. The frontier read cargo. It did not read people.

The shooting set off a different kind of crossing. Berlin assured Vienna of support on 5–6 July. Austria-Hungary sent Serbia an ultimatum on 23 July and gave it forty-eight hours; Serbia accepted most of its demands and qualified the rest. Austria-Hungary declared war on 28 July. Russia’s mobilisation orders followed, then Germany’s declarations on Russia and France, the crossing into Belgium, and Britain’s declaration on Germany. In thirty-eight days a murder had become a war among Europe’s great powers.

The first lasting change at the border was not a better search for bombs. It was that ordinary travellers became security subjects. Britain introduced the Aliens Restriction Act, carried it through every stage and gave it royal assent on 5 August 1914, the first full day of its war. The government took power over foreigners’ entry, departure, registration and residence.

The document issued under the wartime rules from 1915 added two things a Victorian passport—a letter requesting courtesy for a named gentleman—had never carried. One was a photograph. The other was a physical description arranged as a table: forehead, eyes, nose, shape of face. A person had become a set of readable fields.

The war did not invent the identity document. Safe-conducts are medieval, and the empire struck at Sarajevo had neighbours that had been checking papers for a century. What changed was the paper’s purpose. The old letter asked a foreign government to extend courtesy. The new document made no request; it described. A description could be compared, filed and refused.

The distinction matters. The assassins’ route did not cause the passport system, and a missed inspection did not cause the war. The route shows what movement across much of Europe looked like at the moment war began. The new rules changed ordinary travel, not merely the work of frontier guards.

In October 1920 the League of Nations convened a conference in Paris on passports and frontier formalities. It agreed the shape: the photograph inside the front, the remaining pages blank for other countries’ stamps. Two years later the same body issued the Nansen passport for people whose state would no longer describe them. Statelessness had become common enough to need its own stationery.

In June 1914, the conspirators crossed with weapons hidden in their bags and nobody asked who they were. A year later, an ordinary traveller crossed with a photograph and a table describing their face. The queue at the airport—shoes off, passport open at the picture page—is that table still being read. The shot in Sarajevo did not invent the passport. The war that followed changed what a passport was for.

There was another machine in the story. Railway timetables helped turn mobilisation into a sequence that could not easily be paused. The trains ran for three more years, hauling men and shells ahead of grain, until the queue that mattered was in Petrograd and it was for bread.

2 The dates, dry

  • 28 June 1914: Franz Ferdinand and Sophie are shot dead in Sarajevo.
  • 5–6 July: Berlin assures Vienna of support.
  • 23–25 July: Austria-Hungary’s ultimatum and Serbia’s reply.
  • 28 July: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
  • 29–31 July: Russia’s partial, cancelled and general mobilisation orders; Germany demands a halt.
  • 1–6 August: declarations of war widen the conflict to Russia, France and Britain.

3 What the classroom makes of thirty-eight days

Five textbooks take a fifteen-year-old inside the July Crisis. They agree on the main pieces: Princip, the Austrian ultimatum, war on Serbia, Russian mobilisation, German declarations on Russia and France, the invasion of Belgium and Britain’s entry. They even print the same German war plan. The difference is the amount of choice their grammar gives the people making those moves.

OpenStax turns the alliances into an automatic mechanism and Germany into the object of events. The Belarusian book first lists Austrian and German pressure, then spreads culpability among all the major participants. Chubaryan also shares the guilt before calling one bloc’s actions unprovoked. Sabbatucci and Vidotto number chosen moves and give German initiative the decisive one. Lowe teaches the disagreement itself—alliances, mobilisation, Germany, even British ambiguity—then sides with the case for deliberate German provocation.

A tiny disagreement shows how that framing works. The British book (UK · Lowe 2013) says, “The Russians, anxious not to let the Serbs down again, ordered a general mobilization (29 July).” Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed. · §1.3(g), p. 11 The American book (US · OpenStax 2022) says, “Russia ordered mobilization of its troops on July 30 in response.” World History, Vol. 2 · §11.2, pp. 436–437

The record contains both dates: partial mobilisation was ordered on the 29th and cancelled; general mobilisation was signed on the 30th and effective from the 31st. Germany demanded that Russia stop on 31 July. Each classroom has compressed a defensible sequence, but the compression changes the apparent order. One also treats mobilisation as the necessary premise of war; another says troops could still have been halted. Two days is small on a revision sheet. In July 1914 it was the unit of decision.

Three other classrooms reach 1914 without opening the crisis at all. China uses the European war as the opportunity Japan seized to present the Twenty-One Demands. Sudan begins with a conflict among colonial and European powers and follows what it did to the Arab provinces. India gives the war a timeline box with no first mover. Ukraine begins in 1945, while the German booklet is about National Socialism, so neither silence belongs in this comparison.

The omission is the twist. The five books that narrate the crisis leave out the border transformation. The three that skip Sarajevo are not silent about the war; they begin the world elsewhere. Only two of eleven books say how the pistols entered Bosnia, and none stops at the paperless crossing. The object that survived those thirty-eight days is now in the traveller’s hand.

4 Credit and limits

Chubaryan’s Russian book earns the credit: it prints its own government’s aims—control of the Bosphorus and Dardanelles and annexation of Slav-populated territory—and calls the war predatory on both sides, giving a reader material with which to challenge its final verdict. The limit is the sample. Five books narrate the crisis, not five countries; three of the eight in-scope books begin the war elsewhere, too many silences to call evasion. None is a history of July 1914. These short accounts show what classrooms do with thirty-eight days, not everything that happened inside them.
