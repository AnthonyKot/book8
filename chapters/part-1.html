Part I · Chapters 1–3

Imperialism

It begins as an accounting problem. One country grew something the rest of the world had learned to want every afternoon, and wanted almost nothing back except metal — so the metal went, decade after decade, in one direction, into Canton. I read these three chapters back to back, and what carries from one to the next is not an argument. It is a cargo, and each chapter is the previous chapter's freight arriving somewhere new.

Chapter 1 is what happens when a state decides that balance is intolerable and finds a commodity to reverse it. The reversal is grown in a colony, sold into a country that has banned it, and defended with a navy. But a war can force open a port; it cannot make a plant grow somewhere else. That took botany, patience and theft, and it is why the fixing of the ledger ends with a Chinese bush on an Indian ridge — and why the leaf, once it belonged to the empire that drank it, stopped being worth a war.

The same colonies were growing something else in those decades, and it was bitter. Bark from the Andes, ground and swallowed daily, did not cause anybody to partition Africa; ambition and iron steamers did that. What it removed was the reason Europeans had spent four hundred years trading on the coast and staying there. Chapter 2 is about a continent's interior becoming survivable for the people invading it, and about which of these classrooms thinks that is worth a sentence.

Then the machinery that carried all of it — coal, boilers, plate steel, the yards that built the hulls — learns to carry armour. By 1904 the argument has moved to a leased Chinese harbour, and two empires fight over ground belonging to a third. Chapter 3 ends in the Korea Strait, where a European great power that had spent the century dividing other people's coastlines is destroyed at sea by a fleet whose battleships were built in British yards, by British firms, for a navy Britain had helped train. Nobody in Europe read that afternoon as a local result.

What Part I hands forward is not a lesson. It is equipment and paperwork: fleets that have made every previous fleet second-hand, a race any industrial state can now enter, alliances signed to cover the gaps the race opened — London with Tokyo in 1902, London with Paris in 1904, London with St Petersburg in 1907 — and an assumption, held in Berlin and expensive later, about which of the great powers is slow. Part II opens with all of it already built, in the last week of July 1914, when the timetables start.
