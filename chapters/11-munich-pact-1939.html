1 The paper that became a warning

On 30 September 1938, at Heston aerodrome west of London, a sixty-nine-year-old prime minister comes down the steps of an aeroplane and holds a sheet of paper above his head so the cameras can see it. It is not the agreement he signed in Munich the night before. It is a second, one-page declaration he had asked Hitler to sign that morning. By evening Neville Chamberlain is reading it aloud from a window in Downing Street, with the line that outlived him: “peace for our time.”

The larger agreement was made by Chamberlain, Daladier, Hitler and Mussolini. It transferred the Sudetenland to Germany. Czechoslovakia was not at the table; the USSR was not invited. The transfer ran from 1 to 10 October. The paper carried the decision out of the room, but it did not settle what the decision meant. It could be read as peace purchased under pressure, as a betrayal of a country the buyers were meant to defend, or as the moment Moscow learned that collective security would not hold.

Events kept changing the price. German troops occupied Bohemia and Moravia on 15 March 1939, and Slovakia became a separate state under German protection. On 31 March Britain publicly guaranteed Poland; France followed. British, French and Soviet representatives talked in Moscow through August and reached no agreement. On 23 August Ribbentrop and Molotov signed a non-aggression treaty with a secret additional protocol dividing spheres of interest in eastern Europe.

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September. Britain and France declared war on Germany two days later, but not on the USSR. On 17 September the Red Army crossed into eastern Poland. A German–Soviet treaty on 28 September fixed the line between the two powers, and organised Polish resistance ended in early October. The chain from one paper to the next was complete: Munich, the failed talks, the Pact, two armies entering Poland.

But Munich did not remain only one link in that chain. It became a word that could be carried into the next crisis. In June 1950, flying back to Washington after the first reports from Korea, Harry Truman was thinking about the 1930s: Manchuria, Ethiopia, Austria, and what had followed from letting each one pass. He wrote the memory down six years later in the second volume of his memoirs, Years of Trial and Hope.

From there, “no more Munichs” kept appearing in the mouths of people who wanted opposite things: over Suez in 1956, over Vietnam through the 1960s, and in every decade since. The analogy turns a comparison into an instruction. If the present aggressor is another Hitler, delay is appeasement; if the resemblance is wrong, the word can push a government toward a war that the old event does not explain.

An analogy can do that because it travels lighter than the event. The Munich that enters a speech need not carry Czechoslovakia’s empty chair, Moscow’s exclusion, the lost fortifications or the agreement signed in secret eleven months later. It needs only a threatening state, a concession and the known ending. Once those parts are matched, the rest of the past can seem to have matched as well. A complicated choice begins to look like a repeat performance with one correct line.

That is how a three-day meeting in Bavaria still enters an argument without its guest list, borders or protocol attached. Anyone who follows a present war will hear someone called an appeaser and someone else answer that Munich is the wrong analogy. The word arrives already loaded. Its history does not decide whether it fits a new crisis. It explains why saying it can make negotiation look like surrender before the negotiation has even begun.

The little-known afterlife is therefore not a monument or an anniversary. It is a shortcut in ordinary political language. The photograph shows one paper held over a man’s head. The word made from the meeting has proved easier to carry: from Europe to Korea, Suez and Vietnam, then onward into arguments whose circumstances may share only a few pieces with 1938. The agreement failed to secure the peace Chamberlain announced. Its name survived as advice about how peace should be defended.

2 What reaches the classroom

Now enter six classrooms. They teach the same sequence, but they do not hand over the same chain of cause. The Belarusian and Russian books point backward from the Pact to Munich: exclusion, failed collective security and Western appeasement make Stalin’s agreement with Germany a consequence. The German and American accounts point forward, from the secret protocol into Poland. The British and Italian books keep both sides in view: exclusion before the Pact, secret clauses after it. All six include 23 August. None treats it as an accident.

«Мюнхенский сговор стал синонимом предательства и позора, венцом беспринципных уступок со стороны Англии и Франции.»

“The Munich collusion became a synonym for betrayal and disgrace, the crown of unprincipled concessions on the part of England and France.”

RU · Чубарьян, Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл. (Просвещение 2023) · extract p. 108

The omissions make the arrows possible. Britain alone prices Munich in territory, population, industry and fortifications, then stages four rival answers to the question of blame. Germany alone prints the abandoned plan by German officers to overthrow Hitler if war came over the Sudetenland. Italy records that the supposedly Italian proposal followed German demands almost word for word. Belarus alone reproduces the 23 August spheres with Lithuania on the German side. Each exact detail makes one connection visible and leaves another to a different book.

The sharpest split comes on 17 September. Belarus has a liberation campaign that begins and reunites peoples. Russia names troops crossing a border and an order, but not who gave it. Britain puts the invasion in a subordinate clause. Italy makes the Russians the subject of a seizure. America says Poland was crushed from two sides. Germany names the Soviet leadership, the order and annexation. The grammar grows steadily more agentive as the narrator moves away from Moscow.

“The lessons learned from Hitler’s violation of the Munich Pact spurred Britain and France to take action to protect Poland. They have also been invoked by world leaders ever since, whenever the aggression of one nation threatens the sovereignty or the territorial integrity of another. Using the example of Munich to warn against the perils of allowing one nation to invade another without opposition, whether it be Hitler’s Germany or Putin’s Russia, is known as invoking the Munich Analogy.” US · OpenStax, World History, Volume 2: from 1400 (2022) · pp. 531–532 (the sentence runs across the page break)

Only the American book names the instrument and hands it to the present. The British book shows it working later over Korea and Suez without naming it. The other four stop the word in 1939. That is the twist: the classrooms do not merely disagree about what Munich caused. Some teach a verdict on the past; two teach a tool that can help cause the next decision.

3 Credit, with limits

Lowe (UK) earns the credit for giving a student four argued answers instead of one verdict, while Koshelev (BY), Чубарьян (RU) and bpb (DE) each print an inconvenient detail their own framing could have omitted. The award is narrow. These are six pinned editions, not six countries: four other books stop outside this syllabus, while India’s world-history course reaches the period but leaves this European sequence out. None is a military history of September or a legal history of the protocol, and three printed discrepancies remain part of the evidence rather than mistakes silently repaired.

4 The dates, briefly

  • 12 March 1938: German troops enter Austria; the Anschluss is announced the next day.
  • 29–30 September 1938: Britain, France, Germany and Italy meet at Munich; Czechoslovakia is not at the table and the USSR is not invited.
  • 15 March 1939: German troops occupy Bohemia and Moravia.
  • 23 August 1939: Germany and the USSR sign the non-aggression treaty and its secret additional protocol.
  • 1 September: Germany invades Poland; Britain and France declare war on Germany on 3 September.
  • 17 September: the Red Army crosses into eastern Poland.
  • 28 September: a German–Soviet treaty fixes the line between them.
