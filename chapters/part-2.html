Part II · Chapters 4–6

The Great War

Part I ends with the equipment built and the paperwork signed. Part II is what the equipment consumed. Reading these three chapters in order, the thing that carries from one to the next is not an argument about blame — the classrooms will supply plenty of that — but a stock being drawn down: men, grain, a mineral, and finally hours, each one running short in a way somebody eventually has to sign for.

It starts with a schedule. Every great power kept, in a safe, the answer to a problem of pure logistics: which reservist reports to which depot on which morning, which train leaves which siding at which hour, how many horses per battalion and how much fodder per horse. None of that was secret and none of it was a crime; it was the arithmetic of moving millions of men who had jobs last week. But a schedule has a property an argument does not. Once it begins, stopping it costs more than continuing, and the cost is paid by whoever stops. Chapter 4 is thirty-eight days in which several governments discover that they have built a thing that runs, and that five classrooms will spend a century disagreeing about which hand was on it.

What the trains delivered then had to be fed, and the same wagons could not do both jobs. By the winter of 1916–17 the shortfall had reached the shops of the capital of the largest of the belligerents: on 23 February by the calendar Petrograd was using — 8 March by yours — women queuing for bread in the Vyborg district went on strike instead, and eight days later there was no Tsar. Be exact about what that produced: the queue did not make the state that followed, and chapter 5 is largely about how small the night in October actually was. What the queue produced was the end of the monarchy, in under a fortnight, in the country that had put the most men on the trains.

The other shortage was chemical, and it is the one still in the reader's kitchen. Explosives and fertiliser are the same problem — both need nitrogen taken out of the air and fixed into something a plant or a shell can use — and in 1914 the world's supply came largely as mineral saltpetre from the Atacama, along sea lanes the Royal Navy could close, and did. Germany had a laboratory answer that had been running as a factory since the previous September: a plant at Oppau pulling nitrogen straight out of ordinary air. Built for farms, it was turned to nitrates for shells. It did not lengthen the war by itself — governments did that — but it removed the ceiling that would otherwise have brought the shooting down to what the mines could carry. Then the war ended and the plants went back to their first job, which is why roughly half the nitrogen in the body of a person alive today has been through that reaction, and why bread is cheap. The blockade's answer outlived every border it was drawn to defend.

The signing is in a room built to make one king look like forty. Chapter 6 is about who was let into it, and the accounting is longer than the famous number: the document has fifteen parts, and the parts nobody argues about have quietly done more work than the ones everybody does. One of the fifteen is not about Germany at all. It is about the length of a working day, and it is why that chapter ends on a payslip.

Part II hands forward two things it could not settle. In Paris a delegation from a country that had sent labourers to the Western Front asks for a province back and is refused, and the refusal is on the streets of Beijing within five weeks — which is where Part III opens, with the treaty system from Part I finally coming due. And on the other side of Europe, a government made on a quiet October night is the one power not invited at all, sitting outside an order it will spend the next twenty years being blamed for and bargaining with.
