Part II · Chapters 4–6

The Great War

Part I ends with the equipment built and the paperwork signed: a naval race that a British battleship had restarted, and a defeat in Manchuria that Europe filed as proof of how slowly Russia moved. Part II is what the equipment consumed. What carries from one chapter to the next is not an argument about blame — the classrooms will supply plenty of that — but a stock being drawn down: men, grain, a mineral, and finally hours, each one running short in a way somebody eventually has to sign for.

It starts with a schedule. Every great power kept, in a safe, the answer to a problem of pure logistics: which reservist reports to which depot on which morning, which train leaves which siding at which hour, how many horses per battalion and how much fodder per horse. None of that was secret and none of it was a crime; it was the arithmetic of moving millions of men who had jobs last week. But a schedule has a property an argument does not. Once it begins, stopping it costs more than continuing, and the cost is paid by whoever stops. It took thirty-eight days for several governments to discover that they had built a thing that runs, and five classrooms have spent a century disagreeing about which hand was on it.

What the trains delivered then had to be fed, and the same wagons could not do both jobs. By the winter of 1916–17 the shortfall had reached the shops of the capital of the largest of the belligerents: on 23 February by the calendar Petrograd was using — 8 March by yours — women queuing for bread in the Vyborg district went on strike instead, and a week later there was no Tsar. What the queue produced was the end of the monarchy, in under a fortnight, in the country that had put the most men on the trains. It did not produce the state that followed. That came in October, on a night smaller than the word revolution suggests.

The other shortage was chemical, and it is the one still in the reader's kitchen. Explosives and fertiliser are the same problem — both need nitrogen taken out of the air and fixed into something a plant or a shell can use — and in 1914 the world's supply came largely as mineral saltpetre from the Atacama, along sea lanes the Royal Navy could close, and did. Germany had been running the laboratory answer as a factory since the previous September: a plant at Oppau pulling nitrogen straight out of ordinary air. Built for farms, it was turned to nitrates for shells; it did not lengthen the war by itself — governments did that — but it removed the ceiling the mines would otherwise have set. Then the plants went back to their first job, which is why roughly half the nitrogen in the body of a person alive today has been through that reaction, and why bread is cheap.

The hours are signed for in a room built to make one king look like forty, and who was let into that room is the shorter half of the accounting. The document has fifteen parts, and the parts nobody argues about have quietly done more work than the ones everybody does. One of the fifteen is not about Germany at all. It is about the length of a working day — which is how the last of the shortages ends up printed on a payslip.

Two arguments leave the room unfinished. A delegation from a country that had sent labourers to the Western Front asks for a province back and is refused, and the refusal is on the streets of Beijing within five weeks — which is where Part III opens, with the treaty system from Part I finally coming due. And the government made on that quiet October night is the one power not invited at all, sitting outside an order it will spend the next twenty years being blamed for and bargaining with. Both of those are years ahead. What exists now is a schedule in a safe, and one summer morning that will make it worth more open than closed.
