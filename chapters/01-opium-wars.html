Opium Wars

In 1839, on the beach at Humen near Guangzhou, officials destroy more than twenty thousand chests of opium. Within a year, British warships are in the approaches. By 1842 there is a treaty. What each classroom does with those three facts is the subject of this chapter.

The question · agency

Who started it — and what word does your textbook need for the side that moved first?

1 The boring facts

Dry scaffolding. These lines are the ruler; the ladder is the measurement.

  • Britain’s China trade ran, among other goods, on opium grown largely in British India and sold into Qing ports against Qing bans.
  • 1839: Qing commissioner Lin Zexu oversees destruction of surrendered opium at Humen (Tiger Gate).
  • 1839–42: First Opium War. Treaty of Nanking (Nanjing), 29 August 1842: Hong Kong Island ceded, indemnity, five treaty ports, tariff terms later called unequal.
  • 1856–60: Second Opium War (Britain and France among the principals). Further treaties; 1860 occupation of Beijing and destruction of the Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan) by Anglo-French forces.
  • Later Chinese historiography often folds the wars into a longer “century of humiliation.” Western school surveys often treat them as the opening of China to Western commerce.

2 The ladder

Six narrators who actually cover the wars, ordered from thinnest agency (war as backdrop) to thickest (named parliament, named commissioner, named aggression). Books outside the period — Russia 1914–45, Ukraine 1945–, Germany’s NS booklet, Sudan’s national syllabus — are absent here on purpose. That absence is curriculum, not a verdict.

3 The tell

Nobody in this set denies that Britain fought China in the early 1840s, or that Hong Kong changed hands. The disagreement is about the sentence that makes the war start.

Lowe does not need a start. Britain is “first on the scene,” already fighting and defeating. Opium is in the war’s name; it is not in the grammar of the opening. The chapter is about China after 1900. The wars are furniture moved into place so the next century can be arranged.

Belarus keeps the scare quotes on «опиумной» — the “Opium” War — and a result-clause: the English achieved the opening of China. Capture of Hong Kong sits next to Singapore in the same breath. Singapore was already a British foothold decades earlier; whether that is compression or a slip, the classroom effect is the same. The wars are ticks on a colonial timeline, paired elsewhere with the Crimean War as proof that “Western civilization became global.”

Italy and the United States share a two-beat story: Chinese seizure, British force. The beats differ in moral weight. OpenStax names opium in the warehouses and gives the expedition orders as “impose penalties” and “protect British merchants” — a police action with a navy. Sabbatucci–Vidotto keep an anonymous “imperial functionary” and a government that “decided to intervene”; later they call the second war “improperly” an opium war, a rare moment when a textbook doubts its own label.

这个条约是中国近代史上第一个丧权辱国的不平等条约

“This treaty was the first unequal treaty in modern Chinese history that humiliated the nation and forfeited its sovereignty.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 · on the Treaty of Nanking, 1842

China’s lesson is built to carry a different cargo. Lin Zexu is named. The beach is named. Parliament is named as approving war. The Treaty of Nanking is not a commercial settlement; it is the first of the unequal treaties that “humiliated the nation.” The war is “aggressive” from the British side and a “counterattack” from the Qing. That is not a small edit to OpenStax. It is a different subject: the birth of modern Chinese history as national injury with a paper trail.

India’s NCERT page is short and, for this chapter, load-bearing. The East India Company grows opium in India, sells it in China, and uses the silver for tea and silk to Britain — a “triangular trade.” Force expands a “lucrative trade in opium.” No Lin Zexu, almost no battlefield, but the map of empire includes a vertex the UK chapter does not need: the colony that produced the drug.

4 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

NCERT (IN) earns the credit line for this event. Not because it is the longest account — it is not — but because it does a job the others skip: it puts India on the board as the place the opium came from, and it keeps the British use of force tied to that trade in one sentence. Lowe can open China without India. OpenStax can open the war without naming the Indian crop. The Indian classroom cannot tell “themes in world history” and leave the triangle invisible.

OpenStax (US) deserves a second nod for sequence honesty: seizure of opium first, British military orders second, “unequal treaties” later framed as a Chinese name for imposed peace. That is still a Western survey voice. It is clearer about the commodity than Lowe’s arrival narrative.

纲要 (CN) is the depth lead, not the fairness lead. It alone builds a full lesson with commissioner, chests, parliament, and the humiliation vocabulary. Depth is not the same as distance. The chapter needs CN for space and names; it needs IN and US for angles CN does not take.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is six books that bother with the 1840s. It is not “the world.” Russian and Ukrainian grade volumes in this corpus begin too late; the German booklet is a National Socialism primer; Sudan’s secondary history stays regional. Those zeros are not avoidance of the Opium Wars. They are other syllabuses.

Machine-translated discovery files led us to the pages. The Chinese, Russian, and Italian lines above are taken from the original extractions and rendered in English here for the reader. Before any of them becomes a hard claim in a later rewrite, the printed PDF page should be opened again. Belarus’s “Hong Kong, then Singapore” line is especially worth a second look: the finding may be a textbook error, and errors are data too.

None of these classrooms is a full military history of the campaigns. If you want ships and casualties, you will leave every column unsatisfied.
