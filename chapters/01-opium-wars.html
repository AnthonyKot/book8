Opium Wars

On the beach at Humen in the summer of 1839, Lin Zexu’s officials destroy more than twenty thousand chests of opium surrendered by British and American merchants. Within a year British warships are off the Guangdong coast. By 1842 Hong Kong Island has been ceded and five ports opened. Six classrooms agree on that sequence. They do not agree on which act made it a war — and only one of them says what the drug was there to buy.

The question · where the clock starts

Where does the story begin: with the opium trade, with the Chinese seizure, or with the British decision to use force?

1 What happened

  • Britain bought Chinese tea in quantity and paid for it in silver. From the late eighteenth century the East India Company covered that bill by selling opium, grown under its monopoly in Bengal and Bihar, into Qing ports against Qing bans.
  • 1839: Qing commissioner Lin Zexu oversees the destruction of surrendered opium at Humen (Tiger Gate), near Guangzhou.
  • 1839–42: the First Opium War. Treaty of Nanking (Nanjing), 29 August 1842: Hong Kong Island ceded, an indemnity paid, five treaty ports opened, tariffs fixed by agreement — terms later called unequal.
  • 1856–60: the Second Opium War, Britain and France among the principals. Further treaties; in October 1860 Anglo-French forces enter Beijing and burn the Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan).

2 The ladder

Six narrators who actually reach the 1840s, ordered from thinnest agency — war as furniture — to thickest: named commissioner, named parliament, named aggression. Books outside the period are absent on purpose; that absence is curriculum, not a verdict. Read the column down, one classroom at a time, and watch where each of them decides the story starts.

3 The tell

Nobody here denies that Britain fought China in the early 1840s, or that Hong Kong changed hands. The disagreement is about where to start the clock — and every book that starts it later than the drug gets a shorter, cleaner war.

Lowe starts latest of all: he starts at the end. Britain is “first on the scene,” already fighting, already defeating. Opium is in the war’s name and nowhere in the grammar of the opening. The paragraph is scaffolding for a chapter about China after 1900, and the wars are furniture moved into place so the next century can be arranged around them.

Belarus starts at the result. The English “achieved the opening of China to Europeans”; Hong Kong is seized, then Singapore, in one breath. Singapore was already a British foothold two decades before this war, so that line is either compression or a slip — worth a second look, and either way the classroom effect is the same. The book prints “Opium” in quotation marks each time it uses the phrase, so the label arrives as somebody else’s name for the thing.

Italy and the United States start in the same place, at the seizure, and walk to war in two beats. The beats carry different weights. OpenStax names the commodity in the warehouses and prints the expedition’s orders — “impose penalties” and “protect British merchants” — which is a police action with a navy. Sabbatucci–Vidotto keep an anonymous “imperial functionary” and a government that “decided to intervene”; nobody in the Italian account has a name on either side.

China starts earliest, and starts with arithmetic. The lesson prints the ramp: 4,570 chests of opium into China in 1800, more than 40,000 by 1838, more than ten million taels of silver a year going out. Lin Zexu is named, the beach is named, Parliament is named as the body that approved the war. The Treaty of Nanking is not a commercial settlement but the first of the unequal treaties. That is not an edit to OpenStax; it is a different subject — the birth of modern Chinese history as a national injury with a paper trail. Of the six, it is also the only one that does any counting at all. The other five describe a trade dispute without a quantity in it.

The second war sorts the same books differently, and the title of this chapter is plural because of it. OpenStax gives it a police blotter: it “broke out in 1856 after Chinese soldiers seized a British ship in Chinese waters, crewed by Chinese sailors suspected of piracy.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto give it the same trigger and then doubt the label, calling it “improperly” the second opium war — a rare moment of a textbook arguing with its own index. The Chinese lesson has Britain and France launching it, with the United States and Russia arriving “in the guise of mediators,” and ends it with the Old Summer Palace burning. Belarus files it under something else entirely: paired with the Crimean War, it is proof that “Western civilization became global.” Four classrooms, one war, and only in one of them does anything burn.

That is also where the long labels part company. OpenStax hands “century of humiliation” to the Chinese as their phrase — “what the Chinese have called” it — while the Chinese book uses the vocabulary in its own voice, on its own page, about its own treaty. In this set the difference between quoting a name and owning one is the whole distance between the two accounts.

India’s NCERT page is four sentences and load-bearing. The East India Company grows opium in India, sells it in China, takes the silver — and then the sentence the other five columns do not have: “The Company used the silver to buy tea, silk and porcelain to sell in Britain.” No Lin Zexu, no battlefield, but a complete circuit. It is the one classroom that names the vertex the others can drop, the colony that produced the drug, and the one that says what the drug was buying.

4 Credit where due

NCERT (IN) earns the credit line, and not for length — it is the shortest account here. It earns it for triangulation: it is the only book on the ladder that keeps all three corners in view at once, so that force, drug and cargo stay in the same paragraph instead of being distributed across three chapters. Lowe can open China without India. OpenStax can name the opium and never name the crop’s country. The Indian classroom cannot teach “themes in world history” with the triangle invisible, and the chapter below is built out of the sentence that costs it four extra words.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is six books whose syllabus reaches the 1840s. It is not “the world.” The Russian and Ukrainian volumes in this corpus begin too late, the German booklet is a National Socialism primer, and Sudan’s secondary history stays regional. Those zeros are other syllabuses, not avoidance of the Opium Wars.

None of these classrooms is a military history. If you want ships, tactics and casualty figures, every column here will leave you unsatisfied — the six books between them do not print a single number of dead.

The Belarusian line that puts Singapore inside this war is kept exactly as printed. It may be a textbook error; errors are data, and this one has not been reconciled against the printed page.

7 The thread

Read NCERT’s last clause again: the silver bought tea, silk and porcelain. Only one classroom puts the tea in the sentence at all, and none follows the plant out of China. Britain’s side of this war begins with a leaf. In 1784 Parliament cut the duty on tea from roughly 119 per cent to 12½, smuggling collapsed, legal imports jumped, and tea stopped being a luxury and became the thing a household bought weekly. The Treasury then depended on the duty, the Company depended on the trade, and all of it came from one country, which wanted silver and very little else. Opium was the answer to that: a crop Britain could grow in India and sell to people forbidden to buy it, so that the tea could be paid for in something other than bullion.

A war can open five ports. It cannot make a plant grow somewhere else. Within a decade Britain had gone after that problem too.

The Indian half of it had already started, and it started without any theft. In 1823 Robert Bruce came across tea growing wild in Assam; his brother Charles spent the next fifteen years working out how to make it drinkable; the first eight chests of Assam tea were sold at auction in London on 10 January 1839 — five months before Lin Zexu burned anything. Assam’s bush is a local variety, Camellia sinensis var. assamica, and it is India’s own. Indian tea did not begin with anything taken out of China.

What was taken out of China was the other variety, and the knowledge. In 1848 the East India Company hired the botanist Robert Fortune to go and get both. He shaved his head, wore the dress of a traveller from a distant province, and spent nearly three years in districts closed to foreigners — the green-tea hills of Zhejiang and Anhui, then the Wuyi range in Fujian where black tea was made. He shipped roughly twenty thousand plants and seedlings to the Company’s Himalayan nurseries in sealed glazed boxes, a device patented fifteen years earlier that kept plants alive across an ocean for the first time, and he hired eight Chinese tea makers to come with their tools and show the Indian gardens how it was done. He also settled an argument British botanists had been having for years: green tea and black tea are the same plant, handled differently after picking. He published the trip in 1852 as A Journey to the Tea Countries of China, which is still in print, and reads like what it is — a man describing a theft in the tone of a walking holiday.

The hillside in that photograph was planted in those years. Archibald Campbell, the superintendent at Darjeeling, had put China tea seed in his own garden around 1841; the first commercial gardens opened in the 1850s, while Fortune’s consignments and his eight tea makers were arriving in the same mountains. That is why Darjeeling tastes nothing like Assam. Assam grows the Indian plant; Darjeeling grows the Chinese one, on a ridge in Bengal, because it was carried there.

The circuit closed inside one lifetime. By 1888 Britain was importing more tea from India than from China, and China’s share of the market it had held alone went on falling. India kept both crops: the Company’s opium factory at Ghazipur, which packed chests of the kind burned at Humen, is still working today, still making opiates, now under the Indian government. And the plant kept travelling: tea reached Kenya in 1903 as seed from India, and Kenya is now the largest exporter of black tea on earth.

So the tea in most kitchens — Indian, Sri Lankan, Kenyan, whatever the box says — is on the far end of this chapter. If the box says Darjeeling, it is Chinese stock growing on an Indian slope, and the packet carries the label India registered as its first protected place-name in 2004. The war is in the cup, and it can be described exactly: the fighting did not move the plant. Fortune moved the plant, nine years later, with a shaved head and a set of glass boxes. What the war did was make the terms negotiable — Britain fought to keep buying Chinese tea, and then arranged never to have to buy it again.

Now look up what your textbook calls it.
