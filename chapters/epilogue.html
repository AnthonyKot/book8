Epilogue · after Chapter 15

Five events, no chapter

The chapters stop in 1953. The books do not. Every volume on this shelf runs on past Korea into years the reader can remember, or nearly, and somewhere in those last pages each of them meets an event that is still argued about at dinner tables. Five of them are set out here at exhibit length: a rising, a ten-year war, an airliner, a square, a peninsula. No threads, no objects on the table — the columns, and what each classroom is willing to name.

Each exhibit gets a few sentences of situation and one short ladder, with a row only for the classrooms whose reading earns one. Where a book says nothing and the nothing is worth something, it is said in a sentence.

1 Budapest, autumn 1956

A student march through Budapest on 23 October 1956 became a rising within a day. Imre Nagy, a communist reformer pushed out the previous year, was put back at the head of the government; by 1 November he had announced a cabinet with other parties in it and Hungary's exit from the Warsaw Pact. Soviet armour came into the city at the start of November, fighting ran for days, and Nagy was executed in 1958. Every book here whose syllabus reaches that autumn stops for it. What splits them is the noun, and the grammar around the tanks.

The column of armour is the same column in all five. Everything around it moves. In Minsk the tanks are introduced, by nobody in particular, for a purpose the book puts inside quotation marks. In the Italian manual they are called for, by name, by a Hungarian communist. Lowe supplies a man who went too far, and the tanks follow as the consequence of his going. OpenStax has citizens fighting and a government in Washington choosing to stay out. In Kyiv they belong to interveners, and the book adds the only line on the ladder about naming: it records what the winning side called the thing afterwards.

The dead are counted at 2,500 in the American book, about 3,000 in the Ukrainian, and 20,000 in the British — the same fortnight, one afternoon of reading. India's world-history reader runs to the end of the century and has no page for 1956 at all, alone among the books whose syllabus reaches that year.

2 Afghanistan, 1979–1989

Soviet troops crossed into Afghanistan in the last week of December 1979, after a year in which the government Moscow backed had lost control of most of the country. They stayed nearly ten years and left in February 1989. Five books here narrate it, and the disagreement is not about the decade. It is about the verb in the first sentence.

ClassroomHow the troops arrive
BY · Кошелев 2021
An action, among others
Поворот к конфронтации в международных отношениях на рубеже 1970—1980-х гг. был обусловлен и рядом внешнеполитических акций СССР, в частности, вводом советских войск в Афганистан в декабре 1979 г. “The turn toward confrontation in international relations at the turn of the 1970s–1980s was conditioned also by a number of foreign-policy actions of the USSR, in particular the introduction of Soviet troops into Afghanistan in December 1979.” Всемирная история, 11 кл. (БГУ 2021) · extract p. 171; chronology p. 250: «1979—1989 гг. / Война в Афганистане»
IT · Sabbatucci–Vidotto 2004

(Ранок 2019) · pp. 182 and 185 (chapter conclusions)
UK · Lowe 2013

US · OpenStax 2022
A proxy war, with both bills
“…the Soviet Union sent ground troops to invade Afghanistan in December 1979.”

“It spent the equivalent of billions of dollars and lost some fifteen thousand soldiers. About two million Afghans were killed.” World History, Vol. 2 (OpenStax 2022) · §14.5, pp. 610–611

Read the Belarusian sentence again for who is in it. There are no Afghans. The country is the object of a Soviet action, and thirty pages later it is a weight on the Soviet economy: Афганская война истощала советскую экономику и отрицательно сказывалась на настроениях в советском обществе — the Afghan war was exhausting the Soviet economy and telling on the mood in Soviet society. No government, no resistance, nobody with a rifle. Ukraine gives the resistance a cause and lists who armed it; Italy gives the war a mirror, holding it up against the American decade in Vietnam and finding the same shape. Lowe files it under Cold War motives and names the Mujahideen, including a note that one organisation fighting alongside them “received training, weapons and cash from the USA.”

OpenStax is the only one that prints what the war cost the people whose country it was, and the only one that puts its own government's role before the invasion rather than after: it had been arming the other side, it writes, “a policy it had been following for some time.” The Indian reader skips this one too — the same lone gap in the same book.

3 A Korean airliner, 1 September 1983

A Korean Air Lines Boeing 747 flying from Anchorage to Seoul drifted hundreds of kilometres off course into Soviet airspace and was shot down by a Soviet interceptor near Sakhalin before dawn on 1 September 1983. Everyone aboard died. One book on this shelf has a sentence for it.

ClassroomThe sentence
UA · Гісем–Мартинюк 2019
An instrument, not a person
1 вересня 1983 р. радянськими засобами ППО було знищено південнокорейський літак над островом Сахалін, у результаті чого загинуло 296 пасажирів. “On 1 September 1983, by Soviet air-defence means a South Korean aeroplane was destroyed over the island of Sakhalin, as a result of which 296 passengers died.” Всесвітня історія, 11 кл. (Ранок 2019) · p. 185

There is no person in that sentence. No pilot, no air-defence commander, no leader in Moscow — the thing that acts is a category of equipment, and the verb is passive on top of that. There is no flight number either, no aircraft type, no airline; the aeroplane is South Korean and nothing else. It is the shortest possible way to put an event on a page and still put it on the page.

The other four books that reach 1983 spend it on the euromissiles, on the speech about an evil empire, on the strategic defence programme announced in March — and go past the airliner without stopping. That is not four evasions. When most classrooms cut something and one makes room for it, the interesting question is what the one gets out of keeping it, and here the answer is visible in the layout: the sentence sits inside the paragraph where détente collapses, one line after Afghanistan, doing the work of evidence.

4 Tiananmen Square, 1989

Students gathered in Tiananmen Square from 17 April 1989 to mourn the reformer Hu Yaobang and to ask for political change; by mid-May the occupation was enormous and had spread to other cities. Martial law was declared on 20 May, and on the night of 3–4 June the army cleared the square. Five classrooms here print it. Two do not, and those two are the exhibit.

The Belarusian volume has China's 1980s in some detail and no spring of 1989 in it. Deng Xiaoping is there, and so are the reforms — Новое китайское руководство во главе с Дэн Сяопином с начала 1980-х гг. стало на путь рыночных реформ, сознательно копируя опыт Тайваня и Южной Кореи, which reads in English as the new Chinese leadership under Deng setting out from the start of the 1980s on the path of market reforms, deliberately copying the experience of Taiwan and South Korea — followed by wage charts and the dissolving of the communes. The economy arrives; the square does not.

The Chinese volume is the one to read slowly here, because the square itself is all over it. Tiananmen carries a relief of the Jintian uprising on the Monument to the People's Heroes; students march into it on 4 May 1919; Mao stands on the gate tower on 1 October 1949; crowds fill it to celebrate the fall of the Gang of Four. June 1989 is in the book as well, once, in the chronology at the back:

1989 年 6 月　中共十三届四中全会召开

“June 1989 — the Fourth Plenary Session of the Thirteenth Central Committee of the Communist Party of China is convened.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 (2019) · chronology, extract pp. 198 and 217

The month is dated by a meeting. That is the whole entry. Of the books whose syllabus reaches 1989, five stop at the square and two go around it — and by the rule this book has used from the first chapter, a silence that most classrooms do not share is worth marking rather than shrugging at.

5 Crimea, 2014

In late February 2014, days after Ukraine's president left Kyiv, soldiers without insignia took the Crimean parliament building, the airports and the approaches to the peninsula. A referendum was held on 16 March under that occupation, and on 18 March the Russian Federation declared Crimea part of Russia; no member of the UN General Assembly majority recognised it. Two of these books were printed late enough to reach it and do.

“Crimea is claimed by both Russia and Ukraine, and Taiwan claims independence while China considers Taiwan part of its territory.” World History, Vol. 2 (OpenStax 2022) · §15 opening, p. 654; the map-reading passage, p. 14
UA · Гісем–Мартинюк 2019

(Ранок 2019) · p. 195; chronology, p. 222

The two rows are not a contradiction so much as a difference in what a sentence is for. The Ukrainian book gives the event a day, a name and a chapter of consequences — sanctions, a constitutional amendment in 2019, a list of European leaders and what each of them said. OpenStax gives it a verb in a subordinate clause of a paragraph about 2022, and then, three hundred pages earlier and in a different register, teaches its students that this is a place where maps disagree. Both sentences are in the same book, doing different jobs: one narrates, the other trains a reader in how to look at a map. Which of those a student remembers depends on which page the course reached.

The Belarusian volume was printed in 2021 and mentions Crimea nine times. Every one of them is the war of 1853–1856. Its chapter on the Russian Federation is current — Putin, Medvedev, the priorities of a government — and it ends on two symmetrical worries with no event between them: the West is uneasy about Russians wanting to restore the former power of their state, and — Россия с беспокойством наблюдает за продвижением НАТО на Восток, видя в нем угрозу своей национальной безопасности — Russia watches with disquiet the advance of NATO eastward, seeing in it a threat to its national security. Of the world histories here whose printing date and syllabus both reach 2014, that is the one with no page for it.

6 Where this stops being true

Limits

These are exhibits, not chapters. Five short ladders cannot carry what a chapter carries, and none of these five events has been given the reading the Opium Wars or Munich got here — the rows are the labels and the verbs, and nothing more is claimed from them.

Four of the eleven narrators are absent from all five exhibits, and their absence is syllabus rather than avoidance: the Russian volume on this shelf declares 1914–1945, the German booklet is a National Socialism primer, the Sudanese secondary history stays regional, and the Kazakh book is a national history and was never a world-history column in this book. Where Kazakhstan's textbook does touch these years it touches them as Kazakh events — families displaced across the Afghan border, the protests in Alma-Ata — which is a different subject with a different shelf.

Two printed numbers here are reproduced as printed and are not endorsed: Lowe's twenty thousand dead in Budapest, well above the figures current research gives, and the Ukrainian book's 296 aboard the airliner, where 269 is the usual count. Errors in a textbook are data about the textbook. They are also errors, and a reader should know which is which.

8 The last time

Fifteen chapters have ended on the same line, and every time it pointed backwards. Look up what your textbook calls a war fought over a drug in 1839. Look up what it calls a treaty signed in a hall of mirrors, a pact signed in August 1939, a city that became a unit of measurement, a parallel on a map. Those pages are finished. Whatever your classroom said about them, it said years ago, and the argument has gone quiet enough that the wording is stable.

These five exhibits are where that stops. The Ukrainian book was approved in 2019 and its final pages describe a war that got very much larger in 2022. The American one went to press in 2022 and had to open its last chapter with something that had happened while it was being written. The Belarusian book of 2021 has a chapter on present-day Russia and reaches its final paragraph without an event in it. None of these is a settled page. They are pages a committee is still deciding about — whether the paragraph gets a new verb, whether the map on page fourteen goes on saying claimed by both, whether the month keeps being dated by a meeting.

I read eleven of these books and have no country in the argument, which is a position rather than a virtue, and it is not the reason to believe anything on this page. The page numbers are the reason; they are printed so they can be checked against the books, and if one of them is wrong the book is what settles it.

So the refrain gets used once more, and this time it does not point at 1839. Your textbook has a newest edition, and its newest chapter is being written now — by people with names, in a building, under an approval process, about something that happened while you were alive and may not be finished. It will be printed, and a child will be examined on it.

Now look up what your textbook calls it.
