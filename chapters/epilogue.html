Epilogue · after Chapter 15

Five events, no chapter

1 The last pages

The chapters stop in 1953. History does not. Beyond Korea lie a rising, a ten-year war, an airliner, a square and a peninsula. They do not make a tidy final chapter. They make something stranger: the moving edge where an event becomes a sentence while its consequences are still unfolding.

Begin in Budapest on 23 October 1956. A student march becomes a rising within a day. Imre Nagy, a communist reformer pushed out the previous year, returns to head the government. By 1 November he has announced a cabinet including other parties and Hungary’s exit from the Warsaw Pact. Soviet armour enters the city at the start of November. Fighting runs for days. Nagy is executed in 1958.

The sequence is short enough to fit into a paragraph, but its nouns do not sit still. Is this an autumn, a rising, a revolution or a counter-revolution? Are the tanks introduced, called for or sent in? A person reading the event while it happened would not have known which name would last. The name became part of what survived.

Move forward to the last week of December 1979. Soviet troops cross into Afghanistan after a year in which the government backed by Moscow has lost control of most of the country. They remain for nearly ten years and leave in February 1989. The decade begins with another problem of naming. Troops can be introduced, sent, or said to have invaded. Each verb can carry the same soldiers across the same border and leave a different shape on the page.

Four years into that war, a Boeing 747 travels from Anchorage toward Seoul. It drifts hundreds of kilometres off course into Soviet airspace. Near Sakhalin, before dawn on 1 September 1983, a Soviet interceptor shoots it down. Everyone aboard dies. The event is enormous to the people inside it and compact on a historical timeline: one aircraft, one night, one sentence if it is kept at all.

Then comes a square already crowded with history. Students gather in Tiananmen Square from 17 April 1989 to mourn the reformer Hu Yaobang and ask for political change. By mid-May the occupation is enormous and has spread to other cities. Martial law is declared on 20 May. On the night of 3–4 June, the army clears the square. Yet a chronology can date a month by more than one event. A place can be present on page after page while one night in that place is absent.

The fifth event reaches still closer to the edition in a reader’s hand. In late February 2014, days after Ukraine’s president leaves Kyiv, soldiers without insignia take the Crimean parliament building, the airports and the approaches to the peninsula. A referendum is held on 16 March under that occupation. On 18 March the Russian Federation declares Crimea part of Russia; a majority of the UN General Assembly declined to recognise it.

These five episodes are not one causal chain. Their connection appears after the event, in the making of history. Something happens. Somebody must decide whether it belongs in the available space, which date begins it, which noun contains it and which actor receives the verb. A death toll may stand as printed even when another count has become usual. A disputed map may teach one thing while the narrative three hundred pages later teaches another. An omission may be silence, or it may be the ordinary limit of a book that cannot hold everything.

That makes the object on the table unusually intimate. It is the textbook itself: still in print, still being revised, drafted in a building by people who can be replaced, approved by a committee that meets again next year, and due in front of a child by autumn. On a settled page, wording records an old argument. On the last pages, wording is a recent decision and may change with the next edition.

The old events in this book arrived in classrooms after the argument had quietened enough for their sentences to become stable. These events have not. The Ukrainian volume was approved in 2019, before the war it described became much larger in 2022. The American volume went to press in 2022 and opened its last chapter with something happening while it was being written. The Belarusian volume of 2021 reached present-day Russia. Here the gap between news and history is not a century. Sometimes it is the time between an event, a committee and a school autumn.

A reader can hold that interval. The newest chapter of a textbook is not merely about the recent past; it is a trace of the moment when recent events were turned into examinable prose. People chose the boundary, the name, the verb and the space. Then the page stopped moving long enough to print.

2 The dates, briefly

  • 23 October–early November 1956: a student march becomes the Hungarian rising; Soviet armour enters Budapest.
  • December 1979–February 1989: Soviet troops enter Afghanistan and remain for nearly ten years.
  • 1 September 1983: a Soviet interceptor shoots down a Korean Air Lines Boeing 747 near Sakhalin.
  • 17 April–4 June 1989: the Tiananmen demonstrations grow, martial law follows, and the army clears the square.
  • February–March 2014: soldiers without insignia seize key sites in Crimea; a referendum and Russia’s declaration follow.

3 What reaches a fifteen-year-old

The five comparisons show the decisions one by one. Budapest changes first through nouns and agents. In the Belarusian book, Soviet tanks are introduced by nobody in particular to restore order. The Italian manual names the Hungarian communist who called for intervention. The British account places Nagy’s plans before the Russian response; the American one also notices Washington’s choice to stay out. The Ukrainian book calls the forces interveners and records the winning side’s different name for the rising. The armour does not change. The grammar around it does.

Afghanistan turns on the first verb. The Belarusian account makes the country the object of a Soviet foreign-policy action and later measures the war by its effect on the Soviet economy and public mood. Italy sets it beside the American war in Vietnam. OpenStax prints costs to Soviet soldiers and Afghans and puts American arms to the other side into the story. The British line is the bluntest:

“When the Russians invaded Afghanistan on Christmas Day 1979 and replaced the president with one more favourable to them, all the old western suspicions of Russian motives revived.”

UK · Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed. (2013) · p. 161

The airliner shows the opposite problem: not several accounts, but one. The Ukrainian book keeps the event in a passive sentence whose acting subject is air-defence equipment. It prints 296 passengers; the usual number aboard is 269. The other books pass it. That is a signature choice, not evidence that every omission is avoidance: most books cut the event, while one needs it as evidence in the collapse of détente.

Tiananmen is widely taught, so the two absences sound louder. The Belarusian volume reaches China’s market reforms, wage charts and dissolved communes but not the spring of 1989. The Chinese volume repeatedly visits the square in other years. For June 1989 its chronology says:

“June 1989 — the Fourth Plenary Session of the Thirteenth Central Committee of the Communist Party of China is convened.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 (2019) · chronology, extract pp. 198 and 217

The month remains. The event occupying it changes.

Crimea brings the pattern to the newest pages. Ukraine gives the event a start date, a name and consequences. OpenStax calls it annexation in its 2022 narrative but presents the peninsula elsewhere as a place claimed by both Russia and Ukraine. The Belarusian book, printed in 2021, mentions Crimea nine times, always for the war of 1853–1856. Across all five exhibits, what a classroom leaves out is the final finding: India has no 1956 or Afghan war; almost everyone leaves out the airliner; Belarus leaves out Tiananmen and 2014 Crimea; China keeps June 1989 but not the square’s June story. A teenager receives not only different answers, but different sets of questions.

4 Credit, with limits

Credit belongs to the books that preserve an awkward event, name an actor, count costs outside their own country, or show that maps and narratives do different work. The claim stays narrow. These are five exhibits, not five full chapters, and four books cover other years or another kind of history. Two printed totals are retained because students met them that way, with the usual counts beside them. A missing episode matters most when most comparable books include it; when nearly everyone cuts it, the one book that makes room is the more revealing choice.
