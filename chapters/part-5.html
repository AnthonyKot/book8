Part V · Chapters 14–15

The order that followed

Part IV ends with a city turned into a unit of measurement. Part V is the furniture that was already being carried into the room while the bomb was still being assembled: fifty states signed the United Nations Charter at San Francisco on 26 June 1945, six weeks before Hiroshima, and five of them wrote themselves a permanent seat and a vote that stops things. Two chapters here, and both of them turn on objects that were built for something else.

The first object is a sheet of iron on a counterweight. European theatre codes have required one for well over a century — hung between the stage and the audience, dropped and tested on a schedule, so that a fire in the scenery stays on its own side of the house. It had a plain German name in those codes long before a former prime minister stood at a lectern in Missouri in March 1946 and used it for a line running from the Baltic to the Adriatic. Chapter 14 follows the phrase back through the hands it passed through before Fulton, and finds something worth saying about names in general: a metaphor is a used object, picked up by whoever finds it useful. Five of the classrooms in this book send a student to that speech. Three print the words. One records that they were second-hand.

The second object is a chair, and it is empty. From January 1950 the Soviet delegate stayed away from the Security Council in protest at who held China's seat, and when the Council voted on 27 June 1950 to help stop an army that had crossed the 38th parallel, the veto that could have stopped the vote was not cast, because nobody was there to cast it. Be exact about what that produced. The empty chair did not start the Korean War — artillery did that, before dawn on 25 June. What it produced was the flag: a legal United Nations force, fought against by the state whose seat the boycott had been staged to win. Chapter 15 is the book's last full ladder, and the thing the classrooms fight over hardest is not who fired first but whose soldiers get to keep their own name for themselves.

These two chapters also mark the point where the book's own narrators come into existence. Reading them in order I noticed that the shelf is downstream of its subject: the German source is a federal agency of a state reunified in 1990, out of the two Germanies that 1949 produced; the Chinese volume is compiled by the government founded in October 1949; the Belarusian, Ukrainian and Kazakh books are approved by ministries of states that have been issuing approvals since 1991. Not one edition in this book was printed before 2004. Every classroom the reader has been watching — the ones that disagreed about Canton, about Sarajevo, about Munich — was assembled, staffed and approved inside the settlement these two chapters describe. That is why the same four years are, on one shelf, the making of a European order with conferences and alliances in it, and on another, a civil war ending in a founding, with none of those names present at all.

Then the book runs out of chapters, and the century does not. Hungary in 1956, Afghanistan across the 1980s, a Korean airliner shot down in 1983, a square in Beijing in 1989, a peninsula in 2014: five events these eleven books do reach, in a few pages or a single labelled row or not at all. What a classroom does with an event still inside the working life of the person teaching it is the question the epilogue is for.
