Part V · Chapters 14–15

The order that followed

The four rooms just drawn were themselves wired late, inside the order this part is about. Part V is the furniture that was already being carried in while the bomb was still being assembled: fifty states signed the United Nations Charter at San Francisco on 26 June 1945, six weeks before Hiroshima, and five of them wrote themselves a permanent seat and a vote that stops things. Both of the objects ahead were built for something else.

This is also where the book's own narrators come into existence, because the shelf is downstream of its subject. The German source is a federal agency of a state reunified in 1990, out of the two Germanies that 1949 produced; the Chinese volume is compiled by the government founded in October 1949; the Belarusian, Ukrainian and Kazakh books are approved by ministries of states that have been issuing approvals since 1991. Not one edition in this book was printed before 2004. Every classroom the reader has been watching — the ones that disagreed about Canton, about Sarajevo, about Munich — was assembled, staffed and approved inside the settlement these two chapters describe. That is why the same four years are, on one shelf, the making of a European order with conferences and alliances in it, and on another, a civil war ending in a founding, with none of those names present at all.

The first object is a sheet of iron on a counterweight, hung between the stage and the audience, dropped and tested on a schedule so that a fire in the scenery stays on its own side of the house. It had a plain German name in the theatre codes for decades before a former prime minister, at a lectern in Missouri in March 1946, used it for a line drawn across a continent — and he had not coined that use either. A metaphor is a used object, picked up by whoever finds it useful.

The second object is a chair, and it is empty. From January 1950 the Soviet delegate stayed away from the Security Council in protest at who held China's seat, and when the Council voted on 27 June 1950 to help stop an army that had crossed the 38th parallel, the veto that could have stopped the vote was not cast, because nobody was there to cast it. The empty chair did not start the Korean War — artillery did that, before dawn on 25 June. What it produced was the flag: a legal United Nations force, fought against by the state whose seat the boycott had been staged to win. And what the classrooms fight over hardest there is not who fired first but whose soldiers get to keep their own name for themselves.
