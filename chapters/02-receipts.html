1 The ladder

The rows run from a continent that colonises itself, with nobody taking it, to a named African who leads a country to its liberation.

2 The tell

«ما الفرق بين الغزو والفتح؟ وأيهما يصح أن نطلق على التحرك العسكري التركي – المصري في السودان عام 1821م.»

“What is the difference between ghazw (invasion) and fatḥ (conquest)? And which of the two is it correct to apply to the Turkish–Egyptian military movement in Sudan in the year 1821 CE?”

SD · التاريخ للصف الثالث (2009) · revision lesson, p. 3 — a homework question

The Sudanese book is the only one here that asks the student to pick the verb. It is also the only one in which the country is a subject rather than an object. Unit One — “The Mahdist Revolution and State in Sudan, 1881–1899” — runs from page 1 to page 55 of a 267-page book: eleven lessons, with the Mahdi’s birthplace, his teachers, his emirs, his letters, and a map exercise. Khartoum in 1885 is not captured; it is liberated. The 1898 reconquest is not a reconquest; it is “the invaders.”

Lowe gives the same thirty years three sentences. The United Kingdom holds the largest share of the thing being described, and in a survey that runs past six hundred pages the Scramble for Africa is a paragraph on page 26, inside a list of the causes of the First World War. Nothing in it is dated except “after 1880,” nobody in it has a name, and Britain never appears as a subject: “Most of Africa was taken over by the European states.” One African battle of the period does surface later — Adowa, on page 73, half a century out of place, as the humiliation Mussolini was avenging in 1935. Fifty-five pages against three sentences is not a difference of opinion. It is a difference in what the event is for: for Khartoum the Scramble is the prologue to a national origin story, and for London it is a bullet point on the way to Sarajevo.

The books at the top of the ladder are a passive belt. Belarus writes the strongest version of it: Africa became a continent of classical colonialism, as though colonialism were weather, and the powers arrive one clause later in a by-phrase, after the territory “was divided.” Russia’s single sentence is the same construction with a longer guest list — seven states named, none of them the subject of a verb.

Two books put live verbs in, and one of them spends something to do it. OpenStax names the people who were not consulted: “without input from Africans” is a small phrase doing large work, because it converts an absence into a fact about the room. Sabbatucci–Vidotto supply the fullest European machinery instead — Bismarck convening, Leopold II behind a humanitarian association, the Free State as “a paradoxical euphemism,” the English going up the continent and the French marching across it.

Counting the dead sorts the same books the same way. Belarus reports that at Adwa the Italians “lost about 11 thousand soldiers,” and that subduing the Boer republics “cost them 28 thousand men killed.” OpenStax reports six thousand Italians killed at Adwa and three thousand captured. Different measures — “lost” against “killed” — and the same possession: the number belongs to the European army. Menelik II wins the battle in both books, and his losses are absent from both.

Then the other side of the ledger. The Congo Free State gets OpenStax’s most graphic paragraph — a hand cut from each victim “to maintain a tally of the dead” — and no total at all. Exactly one classroom counts African dead, and they are its own: ten thousand Ansar killed in a single assault at El Obeid, in a chapter whose title calls that campaign a liberation.

One more word behaves strangely across the columns. Fashoda surfaces in two classrooms, Italy’s and the United States’. For both it is the name of an Anglo-French near-war on the Nile, and Sabbatucci–Vidotto add what came of it: France withdrew, the temperature dropped, and the episode “opened the road to a closer understanding between the two powers.” That road runs to the chapter on 1914. Fashoda also appears five times in the Sudanese book — as a town, with a governor, that beaten soldiers ran to. Same six letters: in one classroom a diplomatic incident, in the other a place where people live.

The next chapter leaves the continent and keeps the grammar. Africa was divided as territory, in a room, on a map. In 1904 two empires fight a war across Chinese ground, and the classrooms have to decide whether the ground gets a nationality.

The constraint is still on a kitchen shelf. Quinine is what makes tonic water bitter; the tonic was drunk because the dose had to go down daily, and gin and sugar were added to ease the problem of swallowing it. Take a glass under a black light and it glows a faint blue: that is the quinine, a trace of what once had to go down every morning. The label still says Indian, naming the colony where the habit was formed, and nobody pouring it reads that as a claim about anything. A student in Khartoum is handed two words for one march and asked which is correct; the word on the bottle has been sitting in kitchens since the 1870s without anyone asking it the question.

3 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

MoE Sudan (SD) takes the credit line for depth and agency at once. Fifty-five pages let it do what no other book here attempts: name the Sudanese side of a colonial war down to individual emirs, count its own dead, and hand the student the naming problem as homework — ghazw or fatḥ, invasion or conquest, you decide. It is also the narrator standing closest to the events, writing a national story; distance and depth trade against each other here in the open. Two other books earn a share. OpenStax (US) for naming the drug at all, and for the measurement that makes the thirty years legible — about 10 per cent of the continent under European control at the start, about 90 per cent by the end; Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) for being the only book that stages the partition as a sequence with causes rather than as a result, from Egyptian debt to borders “traced on the geographic map.”

4 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is seven books whose syllabus reaches the 1880s. It is not “the world,” and it is not seven countries — it is seven pinned editions. Ukraine’s volume begins in 1945, China’s is a national history that touches Africa only as background to the Opium War, and Germany’s is a National Socialism booklet; those zeros are syllabus boundaries, not avoidance of the Scramble. The Sudanese quotations are the ones to check hardest: the Arabic in that PDF extracts with its ligatures broken, so every Arabic line here has been reconstructed to standard orthography and must be read against the printed page before it is treated as fixed.

None of these books is a history of Africa. Six of the seven are histories of Europe with an African chapter, and the seventh is a history of one country. If you want the Scramble as it was experienced from Kano, Bulawayo or Kinshasa, every column here will leave you unsatisfied — including the one that runs fifty-five pages.

  • Editor’s note on two dates, kept verbatim because errors are data: (1) The Sudanese book lists “Berlin Conference 1878 CE, Berlin Conference 1884 CE” as the two meetings that divided spheres of influence in Asia and Africa. The partition conference is the one of 1884–85; the 1878 meeting is conventionally the Congress of Berlin, which settled a Russo-Turkish war. OpenStax half-shares the compression: its France section sends the powers to “the 1878 Congress of Berlin to decide the fate of the defeated Ottoman Empire’s possessions in North Africa,” where France “received Tunisia.” (2) The Sudanese lesson heading dates the liberation of El Obeid to July 1882 while its own narrative describes the assault of 8 September 1882 and the siege that followed. Both wobbles are to be checked against the printed page before either is called anything.
