1 The ladder

Six classrooms whose syllabus reaches the 1840s, from war as furniture at the top to a named commissioner and a parliament that voted at the bottom. Read the column down, one classroom at a time, and watch where each of them decides the story starts.

2 The tell

Nobody here denies that Britain fought China in the early 1840s, or that Hong Kong changed hands. The disagreement is about where to start the clock — and every book that starts it later than the drug gets a shorter, cleaner war.

Lowe starts latest of all: he starts at the end. Britain is “first on the scene,” already fighting, already defeating. Opium is in the war’s name and nowhere in the grammar of the opening. The paragraph is scaffolding for a chapter about China after 1900, and the wars are furniture moved into place so the next century can be arranged around them.

Belarus starts at the result. The English “achieved the opening of China to Europeans”; Hong Kong is seized, then Singapore, in one breath. Singapore was already a British foothold two decades before this war, so that line is either compression or a slip; either way the classroom effect is the same. The book prints “Opium” in quotation marks each time it uses the phrase, so the label arrives as somebody else’s name for the thing.

Italy and the United States start in the same place, at the seizure, and walk to war in two beats. The beats carry different weights. OpenStax names the commodity in the warehouses and prints the expedition’s orders — “impose penalties” and “protect British merchants” — which is a police action with a navy. Sabbatucci–Vidotto keep an anonymous “imperial functionary” and a government that “decided to intervene”; nobody in the Italian account has a name on either side.

这个条约是中国近代史上第一个丧权辱国的不平等条约

“This treaty was the first unequal treaty in modern Chinese history that humiliated the nation and forfeited its sovereignty.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 · on the Treaty of Nanking, 1842

China starts earliest, and starts with arithmetic. The lesson prints the ramp: 4,570 chests of opium into China in 1800, more than 40,000 by 1838, more than ten million taels of silver a year going out. Lin Zexu is named, the beach is named, Parliament is named as the body that approved the war. The Treaty of Nanking is not a commercial settlement but the first of the unequal treaties. It is a different subject from the one OpenStax is teaching — the birth of modern Chinese history as a national injury with a paper trail. Of the six, it is also the only one that does any counting at all. The other five describe a trade dispute without a quantity in it.

The second war sorts the same books differently. OpenStax gives it a police blotter: it “broke out in 1856 after Chinese soldiers seized a British ship in Chinese waters, crewed by Chinese sailors suspected of piracy.” Sabbatucci–Vidotto give it the same trigger and then doubt the label, calling it “improperly” the second opium war — a rare moment of a textbook arguing with its own index. The Chinese lesson has Britain and France launching it, with the United States and Russia arriving “in the guise of mediators,” and ends it with the Old Summer Palace burning. Belarus files it under something else entirely: paired with the Crimean War, it is proof that “Western civilization became global.” Four classrooms, one war, and only in one of them does anything burn.

That is also where the long labels part company. OpenStax hands “century of humiliation” to the Chinese as their phrase — “what the Chinese have called” it — while the Chinese book uses the vocabulary in its own voice, on its own page, about its own treaty. In this set the difference between quoting a name and owning one is the whole distance between the two accounts.

India’s NCERT page gives the whole thing four sentences. The East India Company grows opium in India, sells it in China, takes the silver — and then the sentence the other five columns do not have: “The Company used the silver to buy tea, silk and porcelain to sell in Britain.” No Lin Zexu, no battlefield, but a complete circuit. It is the one classroom that names the vertex the others can drop, the colony that produced the drug, and the one that says what the drug was buying.

3 Credit where due

NCERT (IN) earns the credit line, and not for length — it is the shortest account here. It earns it for keeping force, drug and cargo in one paragraph instead of distributing them across three chapters. Lowe can open China without India. OpenStax can name the opium and never name the crop’s country. The Indian classroom cannot teach “themes in world history” with the triangle invisible, and everything that follows here comes out of the sentence that costs it four extra words.

4 Where this stops being true

Limits

Six books is not “the world.” The Russian and Ukrainian volumes begin too late, the German booklet is a National Socialism primer, and Sudan’s secondary history stays regional; for those classrooms the Opium Wars fall outside the syllabus rather than being avoided inside it.

None of these classrooms is a military history. If you want ships, tactics and casualty figures, every column here will leave you unsatisfied — the six books between them do not print a single number of dead.

The Belarusian line that puts Singapore inside this war is kept exactly as printed. It may be an error in the textbook, and it is left standing as one.
