1917

On the night of 25 October 1917 — 7 November on the calendar you use — armed men in Petrograd take the telegraph offices, the railway station, the bridges and the power station. Then they walk into the Winter Palace and arrest the ministers sitting round a table inside. The streetcars are running while they do it. The theatres and restaurants are open. Deaths at the palace that night are counted on one hand. Almost nobody carries that picture. The picture almost everybody carries — a vast crowd pouring over iron gates in the dark, under fire — was made later, on purpose, twice.

1 The picture

The war of 1914 reached Petrograd first as bread queues and then as a garrison that would not fire on them. In February, shortages and strikes grew into mass mutinies. The Tsar ordered force, lost the garrison and abdicated. A Provisional Government then shared power with the Petrograd Soviet and kept Russia in the war.

Lenin returned from exile across Germany in April, promising power to the Soviets and an exit from the fighting. The July Days demonstrations failed, and he went into hiding. In August, General Kornilov's march on Petrograd collapsed after Kerensky released and armed Bolshevik factory workers as Red Guards. The government survived each immediate crisis. By October, the men moving through the city had a practical list: stations, bridges, telegraph, power, palace.

The two dates attached to that night are not rival claims. Russia still used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar used almost everywhere else. The October Revolution therefore happened in November on the calendar now used in a phone.

It was a checklist executed by a few thousand people in a city of over two million. The palace defenders were cadets and a women's battalion. The ministers were arrested in a dining room. The cruiser Aurora fired a blank. Outside, the streetcars ran. Nothing in that description looks large enough to become the founding scene of a state that would organise a third of the planet's politics for seventy years. The outcome was immense. Its opening night had produced almost no spectacle.

So the spectacle was built, in public, at anniversaries. On 7 November 1920, the third anniversary, the director Nikolai Evreinov staged The Storming of the Winter Palace on Palace Square itself. Around eight thousand performers, many of them Red Army soldiers, moved under searchlights to artillery sound, with the Aurora in the river and an audience estimated at a hundred thousand. The government made on the quiet night had held power for three years. Now the night acquired a crowd big enough to resemble what followed.

For the tenth anniversary, the state commissioned Sergei Eisenstein to film it again. October was shot during 1927 in and around the real palace and released in 1928. The crowd swarms the wrought-iron gates, pours through the courtyard and floods the staterooms. Frames from the sequence have been reprinted and rebroadcast as though a camera had been present in 1917. No camera was.

The re-enactment and film did not make the revolution or make it succeed. What the anniversaries produced was the visual record that the small night had not generated for itself: evidence manufactured afterwards at the scale the outcome seemed to require, then absorbed into the common stock of things people have seen. The event was over. The picture was the part still being decided.

A phone now makes this mechanism ordinary. People enter a government building; a few seconds of footage arrive before their meaning does. The caption soon chooses among the same three nouns: revolution, insurrection, coup. The picture is not sufficient. The noun tells the viewer whether a people, an organised minority or a small group at the top is supposed to be acting. That is not an analogy laid over 1917. It is the same gap between an image and the story attached to it, running with better cameras.

Go back, then, to the streetcar. The men taking the city had the power station on their checklist, and the current stayed on. Eleven years later somebody built a more memorable version on the real square with eight thousand extras. The night underneath it remained the size of an ordinary evening: theatres open, restaurants open, trams keeping to their routes while the ministers were arrested indoors. Whatever noun belongs to the event, that is the traffic it happened in.

2 The dates underneath it

  • 23 February / 8 March 1917: bread shortages and strikes grow into mass garrison mutinies in Petrograd.
  • 2 / 15 March: Nicholas II abdicates; the Provisional Government and Petrograd Soviet share power.
  • 3 / 16 April: Lenin returns from exile across Germany and publishes the April Theses.
  • 3–7 / 16–20 July: the July Days fail; the government suppresses the Bolsheviks.
  • 27–30 August / 9–12 September: General Kornilov's march on Petrograd collapses.
  • 24–25 October / 6–7 November: Red Guards and pro-Bolshevik troops take the capital's key installations and the Winter Palace.
  • 3 March 1918: Soviet Russia signs the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk and leaves the war.

3 What reaches the classroom

Eight named editions reach 1917. Five enter Petrograd. Their facts mostly fit together: the key buildings, scant resistance, Trotsky's planning, Lenin's leadership. Their nouns do not. The Russian book binds February and October into one Great Russian Revolution, then makes the Provisional Government the subject of its own overthrow. The Italian book calls the night an insurrection and names its organiser. The American and British accounts narrow the action to a coup. Belarus supplies the widest verdict:

BY · Кошелев 2021

«Октябрьская революция 1917 г. стала важнейшим событием в мировой истории.»

“The October Revolution of 1917 became the most important event in world history.”

same volume · printed p. 109 — the section's closing verdict

At the other end, the British narrative turns the night into an operation whose success belongs largely to its planners:

UK · Lowe 2013

“But it was Leon Trotsky who made most of the plans, which went off without a hitch. During the night of 25–26 October, Bolshevik Red Guards and troops loyal to the Petrograd Soviet took over important buildings, including telegraph offices and the railway station, and surrounded the Winter Palace. Later the provisional government ministers were arrested, except Kerensky, who managed to escape. It was almost a bloodless coup, enabling Lenin to announce that the provisional government had been overthrown.”

same volume · § 16.2, point 8, PDF p. 518 — ten pages after the summary

The grammar places control differently. Revolution lets a society act. Insurrection gives the action to an organised minority. Coup gives it to a small group at the top. Russia avoids a separate October label and lets events happen to a government; Belarus makes a nearly bloodless seizure carry a world-historical meaning; Italy, Britain and the United States move toward commanders, plans and factions. The same small night must bear several different accounts of who made the Soviet state.

Three other classrooms leave the night out altogether. In Sudan, 1917 is an impulse toward national struggle. In China, it marks the spread of Marxism and the route toward the Chinese Communist Party. In India, it is a timeline entry and a success with worldwide influence. Those are not shortened Petrograd narratives. They are stories about what 1917 did after it travelled.

Here is the shared omission. Three books name Eisenstein elsewhere, all in sections on culture; only the British book names October. None connects the later film or Evreinov's re-enactment to the familiar storming imagery. Even the Italian account, alone in noticing that the almost bloodless assault would become a symbol, stops before the symbol is manufactured. The textbooks separate the night from the camera. Put those chapters back together and the apparent contradiction becomes the story: a quiet capture acquired the visual memory of a mass uprising years after it was over.

4 Credit and limits

Koshelev (BY) earns the credit: on the same page as its strongest verdict, it asks pupils why people describe and judge the event differently and whether 7 November deserves remembrance. But this comparison covers eight editions doing different jobs, not eight complete national views. Sudan, China and India carry 1917 abroad rather than narrating Petrograd; the Ukrainian volume begins in 1945, the German booklet in 1939, and Kazakhstan's book is a national history. The claim about the picture is narrow too: Evreinov and Eisenstein produced imagery that survived, not the revolution's outcome, and no one can measure how much of a particular reader's mental picture came from their work.

Now look up what your textbook calls it.
