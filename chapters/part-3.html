Part III · Chapters 7–8

Revolutions against the order

Part I is a trade with two addresses: a drug grown in one country and forced into the other, and a leaf carried the other way and replanted so that nobody would have to fight over it again. Part III is the bill arriving at both addresses. These two chapters run in parallel for forty years, and reading them in order I found the same argument underneath both — not who owns the ports, which was settled by gunboats, but who is allowed to say what the thing that happened next is called.

Chapter 7 begins with the paperwork the guns produced. A treaty port is a legal object before it is a place: it means a state that cannot set its own tariff on its own coast and cannot try a foreigner in its own courts. The dynasty that signed those terms lasted until an engineering battalion mutinied in 1911, and then the terms outlived it — a republic was proclaimed, and the contest over who could speak for it ran for thirty-eight years, through warlord decades, a purge, a retreat, and eight years of invasion. The paper ended before the contest did: Britain and the United States signed away their extraterritorial rights in January 1943, because they needed the country still fighting. What was left to settle by then was not the treaties. It was the name.

Chapter 8 is the other end of the same trade, and its material is a mineral the sea hands out for nothing. British India ran on revenue, and salt is the ideal thing to tax: everybody buys it, nobody can stop. To collect the tax the customs service had grown, in the middle of the nineteenth century, a barrier across the subcontinent — a line of thorn hedge, dead brush and ditch running for over a thousand miles, manned by thousands of officers, whose purpose was to stop salt crossing a fiscal boundary. The hedge was abandoned in 1879; the monopoly it enforced was not. Fifty years later a lawyer walked two hundred and forty miles to the coast and picked up a lump of it. Be exact about what that produced: the salt law did not end an empire. What it produced was an offence that anyone within reach of the sea could commit, in daylight, with wet hands.

Both stories close within two years of each other, and the closing is visible from a harbour wall: 15 August 1947, 1 October 1949, new flags over ports that had been opened by somebody else's navy. Not all of them — one port on that coast kept the old flag for another fifty years, until 1997, which is why the handover was on television within living memory.

What Part III hands forward is money that has stopped doing its job. Both of these endings were fought inside a broken price system — in the Chinese case a currency that lost value faster in the final four years than a wage could be spent, so that a household's savings and a government's authority went together. Part IV winds the clock back to 1929 to watch the break itself: the year the price of nearly everything a farm or a colony had to sell was reset by a fall on a trading floor its growers would never see, and the answer governments reached for was a wall built out of customs schedules.
