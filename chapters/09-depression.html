The Great Depression

1 Coffee in the firebox

In 1931 men in overcoats and hats queue along a Chicago pavement for free food; the kitchen handing it out was paid for by Al Capone. In the same years, on another continent, a government is buying food in order to destroy it. Brazil takes its own coffee crop off the market to keep the price of the rest from falling.

Between the queue and the fire is a fall in share prices. Thirteen million shares change hands on Wall Street on Thursday 24 October 1929, sixteen million the following Tuesday, and by the middle of November quoted prices are roughly half what they were in September. Nothing has been shelled. Nobody has crossed a border. Yet the movement reaches a crop thousands of miles from the exchange.

Coffee was roughly seven-tenths of Brazil’s export earnings in 1929. For a generation, the state had held the world price up by warehousing surplus coffee and borrowing abroad against the stock. Against ordinary harvest swings, the arithmetic worked: buy the surplus, wait for demand to catch up, then repay the loan when the price recovers.

The crash broke both sides of that arrangement. American demand collapsed, while New York banks called in foreign loans. Brazil could neither sell the crop nor keep borrowing against it. So the state bought coffee and destroyed it — burned in the open, dumped at sea, shovelled into locomotive fireboxes. Roughly 78 million sixty-kilo bags were destroyed between 1931 and 1944. The burning was not madness. It was the last available lever in a system built to defend a price.

The political order moved too. In October 1930 the coffee oligarchy’s hold on the presidency broke, and Getúlio Vargas took power on 3 November. In 1933 the Departamento Nacional do Café was created to run the destruction as policy, with a staff and a budget. A country that owned no shares in New York had its national income cut in half by a fall in share prices there, then answered with the one instrument it still controlled: the supply of its own crop.

The crash was eight months old when the United States raised its tariff. Herbert Hoover signed the Smoot–Hawley Act on 17 June 1930 over a public petition from 1,028 economists asking him to veto it. Other states retaliated. The value of world trade fell by roughly two-thirds in three years.

Primary commodities — wheat, cotton, coffee, rubber and sugar — fell further and faster than manufactured goods. Producer countries outside Europe and North America were therefore hit through the price of what they sold, whether or not they had a stock exchange. A full warehouse could now be evidence not of wealth, but of buyers who had vanished.

The tariff did not cause the Depression. Banks failed, the money supply contracted, and the gold standard obliged governments that wanted to keep their currencies convertible to tighten rather than loosen. Countries that left gold early recovered earlier: Britain in September 1931, while France held on until 1936.

What the tariff did was travel. It narrowed the channel through which other countries earned the dollars they used to buy American goods or pay American debts. Retaliation made the same move rational elsewhere. The contraction now passed country by country through customs houses as well as banks. That is how it reached a cotton budget in Khartoum and a coffee warehouse in Santos, neither of which owned a share.

After the war, this spiral became something to engineer against. Twenty-three countries signed the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade in Geneva on 30 October 1947. It became the World Trade Organization in 1995. The postwar arrangement grew from the memory of trade barriers hardening while demand and prices collapsed.

The coffee kept burning into 1944. The fires stopped; the arrangement that lit them is still recognisable. A bag in the kitchen names a country and a roaster, but neither sets the price printed into the chain behind it: that is quoted on an exchange in New York or London, and the people who grew the beans read it the way everyone else reads the weather. That is how a crop became worth more in a firebox than in a cup.

2 The dates, briefly

  • Share prices peak in early September 1929; heavy October selling is followed by a decline that runs until July 1932.
  • American unemployment reaches about a quarter of the labour force by 1933.
  • Around nine thousand US banks suspend operations between 1930 and 1933.
  • Britain suspends gold convertibility on 21 September 1931; France holds on until 1936.
  • Germany’s registered unemployed pass six million in early 1932; Hitler is appointed chancellor on 30 January 1933.
  • Japan’s Kwantung Army seizes Mukden on the night of 18 September 1931.

3 What enters the classroom

Eight pinned textbooks reach 1929, and all eight mention the crisis. Together, they form three bands. India, Belarus and Russia write the crash like weather: an exchange crashes, a fall occurs, a Depression follows. India leaves it as a timeline entry and a passing clause, without Roosevelt, Hoover or the New Deal. Belarus lets “Western democracies” emerge through state regulation but never names the New Deal. Russia finally puts a person in the subject slot, only to say that Hoover did nothing.

Britain, the United States and Italy move from weather to policy. The British book calls the crash a symptom and gives the Republican Party a share of the blame. The American book refuses a single cause, then names Smoot–Hawley and its damage to world trade; Hoover is absent from the entire volume. Italy says the United States defended its own production instead of accepting the responsibilities of an economic hegemon. These books name a party, a statute and a state. None follows the coffee out of the warehouse, and none connects the later GATT lesson to the tariff named earlier.

Sudan and China begin where the financial story lands. Sudan’s 2009 ministry book has no Wall Street crash. It has falling cotton prices and a government cutting employment and salaries. During the Gordon College dispute, its sharpest sentence is:

«لكن الحكومة لم تخفض مرتبات البريطانيين والأجانب.»

“But the government did not reduce the salaries of the British and the foreigners.”

التاريخ للصف الثالث, Ministry of Education 2009 · Gezira scheme, p. 80; Gordon College strike, p. 99

China’s 2019 纲要 上 compresses the crisis into a motive:

1929 年秋，世界性经济危机爆发，严重影响日本。日本统治集团为缓和国内矛盾，摆脱困境，急于发动侵略中国的战争。

“In the autumn of 1929 a worldwide economic crisis broke out, gravely affecting Japan. The Japanese ruling clique, in order to ease internal contradictions and escape its predicament, was impatient to launch a war of aggression against China.”

中外历史纲要 上 · 第22课「局部抗战」· p. 150

There is no unemployment figure, bank, tariff or Roosevelt. In this classroom, the Depression is not an economy. It is a motive.

The ladder is not ideological. It measures distance from the exchange. As the books follow the Depression towards a payroll or an invasion, abstractions give way to officials choosing what happens to identifiable people. Yet all eight leave the machinery thin. They name damage, policy, blame or consequence; not one explains the whole route from monetary contraction and convertibility, through tariffs and retaliation, to the price of cotton or coffee. Three books mention coffee. None says what was done with it.

4 Credit and limits

The Sudanese ministry book deserves the credit: with the fewest pages to spare, it turns the Depression into a choice between named groups rather than an injury to an aggregate population. But this comparison is eight editions whose syllabuses reach 1929, not eight countries in full. Ukraine and Germany begin too late or cover a narrower subject, while Kazakhstan’s volume is a national history. Nor is any of the eight an economic history. Their shared silence about the machinery beneath the slump is real, but it is also a limit of the kind of books being compared.
