Meet the narrators

Eleven books sat on this desk for the length of the writing. They are not a sample of anything and they were never meant to be: one book per position, each the one a fifteen-year-old in that country is most likely to have been handed, or the nearest honest substitute where the school edition is locked behind a paywall. Below is a card for each — who wrote it, who has to approve it before it reaches a classroom, where its pages actually go, what it does better than the other ten, what it cannot see from where it stands, the move it makes when the subject gets difficult, and one sentence of it in its own voice.

Credit comes before critique on every card, and that ordering is deliberate rather than polite: a book that is careless everywhere is uninteresting, and none of these is that. The interesting ones are precise in nine places and go soft in the tenth, and the soft spot is only legible against the precision. Two of the eleven are not school textbooks at all — one is a federal agency's civic booklet, one a university manual — and their cards say so. One is a national history rather than a world history, and it never appears as a peer row when a world event is laid out side by side; its card says why it stayed on the desk anyway.

The order below is the one the book keeps to when it lists them all. Quotes are printed in the original where the original is the finding, with a translation for reading.

1 Belarus · Кошелев 2021

  • The book — В. С. Кошелев и др., «Всемирная история. XIX — начало XXI в.», 11 кл., БГУ 2021. 265 pages in Russian, running from about 1800 to about 2020.
  • Who approves it — the Ministry of Education. School history in Belarus is a state monopoly: one approved line, one publisher of record.
  • Where its pages go — it is a survey with a very long nineteenth century, giving the Russian Empire, the industrial revolution, Napoleon's Europe and the arts chapters of much the same size as the ones the world wars get, and closing with a synchronistic table and a dictionary of concepts.
  • Good for — reach. It is one of the few here whose syllabus starts early enough to carry the Opium Wars, the partition of Africa and the collapse of the colonial system in one volume, and it is the book most willing to end a section with a question it has not answered.
  • Blind zone — the murder of the European Jews. The volume contains no camps, no ghetto, no Babi Yar; it defines «Геноци́д» in its glossary and never attaches the word to an event.
  • Signature move — the result without the actor. Wars are won, countries are opened, territories are seized, and the sentence often reaches its outcome without anyone in it who could be asked why.
«В период немецкой кампании в Польше СССР взял под свою защиту население Западной Украины и Западной Белоруссии. Освободительный поход Красной армии, начавшийся 17 сентября 1939 г., привел к воссоединению белорусского народа в составе БССР и украинского народа в составе УССР… Этот день в Беларуси стал государственным праздником — Днем народного единства.»

“In the period of the German campaign in Poland, the USSR took under its protection the population of Western Ukraine and Western Belorussia. The liberation campaign of the Red Army, which began on 17 September 1939, led to the reunification of the Belarusian people within the BSSR and the Ukrainian people within the Ukrainian SSR… This day in Belarus became a state holiday — Day of National Unity.”

BY · Всемирная история, 11 кл. (БГУ 2021) · extract p. 158

2 Russia · Чубарьян 2023

  • The book — А. О. Чубарьян, «Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945 гг.», 10 кл., Просвещение 2023. 241 pages in Russian, covering thirty-one years and no more.
  • Who approves it — the state. This is the world-history volume of the unified federal course introduced in 2023: one authorised text for the grade, nationwide.
  • Where its pages go — into the world beyond Russia, which is the point of the volume: the Paris peace conference gets more room here than in any other book on this desk, while the Russian revolution of 1917 gets a fraction of what the Belarusian volume gives it, because at home 1917 belongs to the other course.
  • Good for — colonial violence at a distance, told with the actor named and the number printed. Amritsar, the delegations from the colonies who came to Paris and were not received, Hoover waiting for prosperity to come back round the corner: this book is often the most agentive narrator in the room about somebody else's empire.
  • Blind zone — the shooting of the Polish officers in 1940, which the volume disposes of in a subordinate clause about the most anti-Soviet among the prisoners; and everything after 1945, which is a different book.
  • Signature move — the pivot on a single connective. It lists the shared guilt of every party at length, writes однако — however — and lets one adjective decide the paragraph.
«Расстрел английскими властями мирного митинга 13 апреля 1919 г. в Амритсаре»

“The shooting by the English authorities of a peaceful rally on 13 April 1919 in Amritsar”

RU · Чубарьян, Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл. (Просвещение 2023) · p. 123

3 Kazakhstan · «История Казахстана» 2015

  • The book — «История Казахстана», 11 кл., Мектеп 2015. 344 pages in Russian, for Russian-medium schools, arranged thematically from the eighteenth century to the 2010s.
  • Who approves it — the Ministry of Education and Science, through the approved-textbook list.
  • Where its pages go — into what happened to Kazakhstan: the peoples deported into the republic, the Semipalatinsk test site and the movement that closed it, Soviet policy as a republic lived it rather than as Moscow announced it.
  • Not a world-history narrator — and this needs saying plainly, because it changes how the book is used here. This is the national-history course. Setting it beside ten world histories on a world event is a genre mismatch, not a test of candour, and it never appears as a peer row in a side-by-side reading. The Korean War of 1950–53 is not in it at all, and that is a fact about which course it is, not about what it avoids.
  • Why it is still in the room — deportation memory. Where the world-history books have a sentence about Soviet nationalities policy, this one has the arrivals: the Far Eastern Koreans moved in two stages from the autumn of 1937, settled across eight regions, with a Korean theatre in Kzyl-Orda in 1937; the Polish and German deportees; the appeal from Karaul about the test site. That material exists nowhere else on this desk.
  • Signature move — world history arrives as the biography of people who were moved.
«Одной из первых жертв массовой депортации народов в СССР стали дальневосточные корейцы»

“One of the first victims of the mass deportation of peoples in the USSR were the Far Eastern Koreans”

KZ · История Казахстана, 11 кл. (Мектеп 2015) · deportations section

4 Ukraine · Гісем–Мартинюк 2019

  • The book — О. Гісем, О. Мартинюк, «Всесвітня історія», 11 кл., Ранок 2019. 226 pages in Ukrainian, running from 1945 to about 2018.
  • Who approves it — the Ministry of Education and Science, which maintains a list rather than a monopoly: several approved titles compete for the same grade.
  • Where its pages go — it begins where most of the others are winding down, so the postwar world is not an epilogue here but the whole course, and the Korean War gets more of its pages than it gets in any other book on this desk.
  • Good for — stating what both sides wanted before stating who moved first. On Korea it is the only book that gives each government the same ambition in one sentence and then names the army that crossed the line, in that order, without softening either half.
  • Blind zone — everything before 1945, by syllabus; and the murder of the European Jews, which enters mainly as the prologue to the founding of Israel rather than as an event with its own pages.
  • Signature move — the definition box, carrying today's date. Institutions are defined for the student in the margin, and the definition is updated to where the reader's own country currently stands in relation to them.
«При цьому обидва режими прагнули об’єднати Корейський півострів під своєю владою. У червні 1950 р. північнокорейські війська перетнули 38-му паралель і розпочали війну.»

“At the same time both regimes sought to unite the Korean peninsula under their own power. In June 1950 North Korean troops crossed the 38th parallel and began a war.”

UA · Всесвітня історія, 11 кл. (2019) · p. 18

5 China · 中外历史纲要 上 2019

  • The book — 《中外历史纲要（上）》, the state-compiled (统编) national edition, 人民教育出版社 2019. 217 pages in simplified Chinese, from antiquity to roughly 2017.
  • Who approves it — the state compiles it. There is one edition for the country, and the volume above is the Chinese-history half of the pair.
  • Where its pages go — close to a quarter of the volume is the years between the fall of the Qing and 1949, and the outside world mostly enters where it enters China.
  • Good for — arithmetic. On the opium trade it prints the ramp — chests in 1800, chests in 1838, taels of silver leaving per year — where the other accounts of the same war manage a trade dispute with no quantity in it. It also names the body that voted, and reproduces primary sources in boxes with the speaker attached.
  • Blind zone — the partition of Africa, the July crisis of 1914, Munich, the first of September 1939. This is the Chinese-history volume; those belong to its companion, and the zeros are a table of contents rather than a verdict.
  • Signature move — the name as verdict. An event is given a title that contains its judgement, and the title then does the work of an argument every time it is used.
这个条约是中国近代史上第一个丧权辱国的不平等条约

“This treaty was the first unequal treaty in modern Chinese history that humiliated the nation and forfeited its sovereignty.”

CN · 中外历史纲要 上 (2019) · on the Treaty of Nanking, 1842

6 India · NCERT, post-2022

  • The book — NCERT, Themes in World History, Class XI, the post-2022 rationalised edition. About 200 pages in English, reaching from early human history to the end of the twentieth century.
  • Who approves it — the National Council of Educational Research and Training, under the central ministry; states adopt it or publish alongside it.
  • Where its pages go — into themes and timelines rather than a chronicle, and its own South Asia column is nearly empty on purpose, because South Asia is next year's book and the front matter says so on page viii.
  • Good for — triangulation. It is the book that keeps three corners of a trade in the same paragraph, so that the drug, the force and the cargo cannot be filed in separate chapters; and it teaches the student, before any evidence arrives, how to notice what a timeline has left out.
  • Blind zone — itself. The independence movement runs to two cells of a timeline; Jawaharlal Nehru's name occurs in the volume as the name of a university. The vacancy is declared in advance, which makes it a syllabus boundary rather than a silence.
  • Signature move — it hands over the instrument before the exhibit, and the instrument works on the book holding it.

“So we need to read each timeline critically, see what it tells us and what it does not. Timelines frame history in particular ways.”

IN · NCERT, Themes in World History, Class XI · “On Reading World History,” p. viii

7 Sudan · وزارة التربية والتعليم 2009

  • The book — وزارة التربية والتعليم, «التاريخ», third secondary year, revised edition 2009 (Bakht al-Ruda). About 275 pages in Arabic, from 1821 to roughly 1948.
  • Who approves it — the Ministry of Education, which is also the author. One national book.
  • Where its pages go — the opening unit alone, the Mahdist revolution and state, runs for the first fifty-five pages, with the Mahdi's teachers, his emirs, his letters and a map exercise; the world wars arrive late and mostly as things that happened to the Arab world.
  • Good for — resistance told from inside, by the only narrator here whose country is the subject of its own sentences rather than a territory in someone else's chapter. Khartoum in 1885 is not captured in this book; it is liberated.
  • Blind zone — the world outside its region, and everything after independence. The syllabus stops where the national story arrives, and the zeros elsewhere on the shelf are that boundary, not avoidance.
  • Signature move — it puts the vocabulary on trial and makes the student the judge, in a homework question.
«ما الفرق بين الغزو والفتح؟ وأيهما يصح أن نطلق على التحرك العسكري التركي – المصري في السودان عام 1821م.»

“What is the difference between ghazw (invasion) and fatḥ (conquest)? And which of the two is it correct to apply to the Turkish–Egyptian military movement in Sudan in the year 1821 CE?”

SD · التاريخ للصف الثالث (2009) · revision lesson, p. 3 — a homework question

8 Germany · bpb Nr. 316, 2012

  • The book — bpb, «Informationen zur politischen Bildung» Nr. 316: «Nationalsozialismus: Krieg und Holocaust», 2012. 84 pages in German, on 1939–45 and the reckoning after it.
  • Who approves it — the Bundeszentrale für politische Bildung, a federal agency, which publishes it directly. Note the genre: this is civic-education material distributed to schools, not a commercial school textbook — the German classroom editions are behind paywalls, and this booklet stands in for that voice knowing it is not the same thing.
  • Where its pages go — two subjects fill most of the eighty-four: the murder of the European Jews, and the occupation of eastern Europe.
  • Good for — testimony against itself, at a level of specificity no other book here attempts. It names the ordinary army rather than only the special units; it carries the shooting of the Polish officers and the deportations from Soviet-occupied Poland; it prints an eyewitness column on Guernica.
  • Blind zone — everything outside its six years, which is the whole rest of this book's spine; and, because it is an agency booklet, it cannot show what a German fifteen-year-old is actually set to read.
  • Signature move — it argues with the comfortable metaphor. Where a phrase has become the way a horror is remembered, the booklet stops and asks whether the phrase is accurate.
„Deutsche Soldaten beteiligten sich an den Mordtaten gegen die Zivilbevölkerung, sicherten die Erschießungsstätten ab, brannten ganze Dörfer nieder…“

“German soldiers took part in the killings of the civilian population, secured the shooting sites, burned whole villages down…”

DE · Informationen zur politischen Bildung Nr. 316, „Nationalsozialismus: Krieg und Holocaust“ (2012) · pp. 4, 29

9 Italy · Sabbatucci–Vidotto 2004

  • The book — G. Sabbatucci & V. Vidotto, «Il mondo contemporaneo. Dal 1848 a oggi», Laterza 2004. 749 pages in Italian, from 1848 to the early 2000s. A university manual by the authors of the school market leader; the oldest edition on this desk.
  • Who approves it — nobody. A commercial publisher prints it and lecturers choose it.
  • Where its pages go — eight of its chapters are about Italy alone, from unification through Giolitti and fascism to the Second Republic, wrapped around a full narrative of Europe and the wider world.
  • Good for — labels. This is the book that will stop mid-sentence to say that an official name for a thing was a euphemism, that a war is called by the wrong title in its own index, that a symbolic assault was practically bloodless. It is also explicit that outcomes were avoidable, and names the decisions that were taken instead.
  • Blind zone — the world outside Europe gets sections where Europe gets chapters, and the 2004 edition means the last twenty years are not in it at all.
  • Signature move — the cleft sentence, which is Italian's way of pointing: it was X that did it, with the actor placed where the reader cannot walk past.
«…Stato libero del Congo – un paradossale eufemismo per indicare quella che fu, per il trattamento delle popolazioni e lo sfruttamento delle risorse, una delle forme più rapaci e disumane di dominio coloniale…»

“…Free State of the Congo — a paradoxical euphemism for what was, in its treatment of the populations and its exploitation of resources, one of the most rapacious and inhuman forms of colonial rule…”

IT · Il mondo contemporaneo (Laterza 2004) · pp. 142–143

10 United Kingdom · Lowe 2013

  • The book — Norman Lowe, Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed., Palgrave 2013. 999 pages in English, from about 1900 to its own present. The standard revision text for GCSE and A-level.
  • Who approves it — nobody. It sells because teachers buy it, which is a different kind of pressure from a ministry list and produces a different kind of caution.
  • Where its pages go — a thousand pages of the twentieth century arranged as the questions a student will be asked, with the sections titled as those questions.
  • Good for — printing the disagreement instead of resolving it. A section on 1914 answers by naming the historians who have fought over it, with dates, and then takes a side anyway. On India it carries the highest death tolls of any book here, including the ones charged to its own empire, and then prints its own prime minister's defence beside them.
  • Blind zone — the nineteenth century, so the empire's beginnings arrive already finished: Britain is “first on the scene,” fighting and defeating, and the drug is in the war's name and nowhere in the grammar. The shooting of the Polish officers in 1940 is absent.
  • Signature move — the argument itself becomes the lesson, and the student is handed a bibliography and told the professionals are still at it.
“No wonder Bethmann, when asked how it all began, raised his arms to heaven and replied: ‘Oh – if I only knew!’”

UK · Lowe, on the German Chancellor of July 1914 — printed in a school textbook as a serious answer.

Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed. · §1.4, p. 18

11 United States · OpenStax 2022

  • The book — OpenStax, World History, Volume 2: from 1400, Rice University 2022, published under a Creative Commons licence. 723 pages in English, from 1400 to the 2020s.
  • Who approves it — nobody. It is free to download and adopted course by course, which makes it the most easily checkable book on this desk: every page cited here can be opened by the reader in a minute.
  • Where its pages go — into units of near-equal size spread genuinely wide, so that the Islamic world, early modern Africa, the Latin American revolutions and Indian Ocean trade each get about as much room as the Second World War.
  • Good for — mechanism and material means. It will tell you which bark the drug came from and where the plantations were moved to, what an expedition's written orders said, what a tariff did to world trade — and it does not spare its own country when the mechanism is American.
  • Blind zone — the last link in a chain. It names the opium in the warehouses and never the country the crop was grown in; the survey's even hand can leave the connection between two of its own chapters unmade.
  • Signature move — it walks the event forward into the reader's present and says where the equipment is still in service.
“Smoot-Hawley was the highest tariff the U.S. government has ever enacted. It helped stifle world trade, which decreased 30 percent by the early 1930s.” US · OpenStax, World History, Volume 2: from 1400 (2022) · § 12.3 “The Great Depression,” p. 492

12 The twelfth card

There is a twelfth narrator on this page and it is the one writing it: an AI that read all eleven and has no country, no ministry, no childhood classroom, and therefore no map that still feels like the shape of the world. That is a position rather than a claim to neutrality — a language model is assembled out of human writing, most of it in a few languages, and it carries the tilt of what it was made from. So the eleven cards above are not offered on my word: every one of them is built out of passages with an edition and a page attached, and the whole apparatus exists so that you can go and disagree with me on the page number given.
