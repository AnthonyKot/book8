1 The ladder

Seven of these books teach the years 1939–1945. Five of them (UK, US, IN, BY, and the body text of RU itself) contain zero mentions of Katyn, Katyń, or the NKVD officer executions. Only two textbooks—Germany and Italy—name the massacre and assign responsibility to Soviet secret forces. Below, the single passage in the Russian state textbook that touches Polish POWs sits beside the two European accounts that record Katyn, ordered from most agentless to most agentive.

2 The tell

Every book on this shelf that teaches these years records the collapse of Poland in September 1939. The disagreement in the grammar is whether 22,000 captured officers existed as a deliberate liquidation, or whether their disappearance can be absorbed into passive voice without a name, a date, or an executioner.

The single passage in Russia’s unified state textbook (Чубарьян 2023, p. 160) that touches Polish prisoners after September 1939 performs five grammatical operations in twenty-two words:

First, agentless passive constructions remove the actor. Было отпущено (“were released”) and расстреляны (“shot”) describe events that happen to human beings without specifying who performed either action. The NKVD, the Soviet state, Lavrentiy Beria, and the Politburo order of March 5, 1940 do not appear in the syntax.

Second, moral classification is transferred to the victims. Those shot are defined as наиболее антисоветски настроенные (“the most anti-Soviet minded”). The sentence provides a political disposition for the dead, while leaving the executioners unmentioned.

«Большинство из них было отпущено по домам, а некоторые, из наиболее антисоветски настроенных, расстреляны.»

“Most of them were released home, and some, of the most anti-Soviet minded, shot.”

RU · Чубарьян, Всеобщая история 1914–1945, 10 кл. (2023) · p. 160

Third, proportions replace counts. Большинство (“most”) precedes некоторые (“some”). Out of roughly 250,000 Polish servicemen who fell into Soviet hands in September 1939, more than 21,800 officers, border guards, policemen, and educated reservists were systematically executed across five main locations. By placing the release of enlisted men first and framing those killed as “some,” the text presents mass execution as an exceptional administrative measure for a refractory minority.

Fourth, geography and chronology are erased. The word “Katyn” (Катынь) does not appear on page 160 or anywhere else in the 241 pages of the textbook. The year 1940 is omitted. The forest near Smolensk, the Starobelsk camp, and the Kozelsk camp are absent.

Fifth, the silence has company. In five of the seven books that teach these years — Russia's, Belarus's, Britain's, America's and India's — Katyn is absent. In Norman Lowe’s UK standard and OpenStax’s US volume, the Polish campaign of September 1939 ends when Poland is “crushed from two sides,” after which narrative attention shifts immediately to the Western Front and the Battle of Britain. In Belarus’s state volume (Koshelev), the Red Army’s September 17 entry is framed as a “liberation campaign” that reunited Belarusian lands, with zero mention of the captured officer corps.

Only Germany’s federal civic booklet (bpb) and Italy’s university text (Sabbatucci–Vidotto) break the zero-mention pattern. Both treat Katyn as a signature topic. Germany allocates exact figures (15,000 officers shot in April–May 1940, 4,500 in Katyn forest) and links the massacre to the Soviet deportation of 381,000 civilian elites and the 1937–38 NKVD “Polish Operation.” Italy names the 1943 German discovery of mass graves and explicitly attributes the crime per opera dei sovietici (“through the work of the Soviets”). Where five classrooms leave 22,000 dead unmentioned, two set the institutional agent on the page.

3 Credit where due

Fairness, paid in installments

Germany’s bpb booklet earns the credit lead for this exhibit. It does not treat Katyn as an isolated atrocity or a wartime footnote. It places the 15,000 officer executions inside a structural account of Soviet policy in occupied eastern Poland—alongside 381,000 civilian deportations to Siberia and the earlier 1937–38 NKVD "Polish Operation" (111,000 shot)—providing students with the full institutional frame of state violence.

Sabbatucci–Vidotto (IT) merits recognition for historiographical precision regarding the discovery. It explicitly records that the mass graves were uncovered by German forces in 1943 while maintaining clear attribution of guilt to Soviet executioners (per opera dei sovietici), preventing confusion between the discoverers of the graves and the authors of the crime.

4 Where this stops being true

Limits

This comparison evaluates seven in-scope world and modern history textbooks covering the 1939–1945 period. Five of them omit Katyn entirely. That 5-of-7 silence is an avoidance signal in the Russian case, but in the Anglo-American market standards (Lowe, OpenStax) and the Indian curriculum (NCERT), it reflects low syllabus salience: general world-history surveys frequently compress eastern European occupation history into broad campaign overviews.

Four volumes in our wider 11-country corpus are out of scope for this exhibit: Ukraine’s 11th-grade textbook covers 1945–2018; Kazakhstan’s 11th-grade volume is a national history focused on Kazakh land and republic-level deportations; China’s senior history covers domestic Chinese development; and Sudan’s 3rd-secondary text focuses on Arab and regional national movements. Their lack of Katyn coverage is a function of syllabus boundaries rather than topic avoidance.

The quotes cited from the Russian, German, and Italian volumes are verified against the original printed page extractions in corpus-orig/. The line on page 160 of the Russian state textbook remains the sole sentence in that 241-page volume that touches the disposition of Polish prisoners of war captured in September 1939.
