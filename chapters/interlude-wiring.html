Same War, Different Classroom

Interlude · between Parts IV and V

The wiring diagrams

What kept surprising me, reading these books through 1938 and 1939, was how little they disagree about what happened. Give four of these classrooms the same eight dates and they will put them in the same order, and one of them will quietly correct another's arithmetic on a detail. What they do not share is which date is holding up which. So here is the same set of boxes four times, in the same places on the page every time, with nothing changed but the lines between them.

Each drawing is one book's account of the road into the war, redrawn as wiring. A thick line is a claim the book is leaning on; a thin line is a connection made in passing; a dashed line is a connection the book reports as somebody else's. A box with nothing attached is one that book has on its pages but not in this argument, and a box in faint outline is one that book does not have at all. Every sentence behind every line here is quoted, with its page, in chapter 11.

With Munich carrying the load, the Pact stops being a choice and becomes a consequence: «Пакт о ненападении был вынужденным шагом со стороны СССР» — “the non-aggression pact was a forced step on the part of the USSR.” Everything downstream inherits that. September opens as Germany's war, and the second entry into Poland is the one node in the drawing with a line coming in and none going out — troops cross the border «получив приказ», having received the order, in a sentence where orders are received and never given. A student can leave this page able to answer “what caused the war” in one word, with the two entries into Poland sitting downstream of that word, and one of them arriving from nowhere.

Two thick arrows go into the war here, and the book does not tell a student which of them to believe. The section is built as an argument with named adults on each side, and the line the Russian classroom draws in ink is drawn here in someone else's handwriting: “Russian historians justify the pact on the grounds that it gave the USSR time to prepare its defences against a possible German attack.” The second entry into Poland comes in through the pact rather than through a decision — Poland was divided “as agreed in the pact of August 1939” — which is a thin line by construction, since the sentence's real business is that Polish resistance collapsed. One more line here belongs to no other drawing in this set: hundreds of pages later, in his chapter on the slump, Lowe closes with “It was the war that finally put an end to the depression,” and that arrow runs backwards. A classroom on this wiring concludes that the question is open and that serious people answer it differently, which is a conclusion about history rather than about 1939.

One line enters this drawing from outside the frame and does not stop until the war, and it starts in Berlin. The Pact is wired in as an instrument with two heavy lines out of it and none coming in — the protocol provided for «die Zerschlagung Polens und die Aufteilung des Landes zwischen Deutschland und der Sowjetunion», “the smashing of Poland and the division of the country between Germany and the Soviet Union,” and 23 August is marked as «die gemeinsame Aufteilung, Ausbeutung und Unterdrückung Europas», “the joint division, exploitation and oppression of Europe.” Munich, in this wiring, causes nothing: it is a stub that ends in a cross, because the coup the officers around Ludwig Beck had prepared for a war over the Sudetenland was called off once the agreement removed its pretext — «Eine erfolgversprechende Möglichkeit, Hitler zu stürzen, war damit vertan.», “A promising opportunity to overthrow Hitler was thereby squandered.” A classroom on this wiring cannot arrive at “the Western powers brought this about,” because no line from the west is long enough to reach the war. What it can conclude is that the chance to cut the one line that was long enough was German as well, and was let go.

The fourth drawing is mostly empty, and the empty part is the finding: Munich, the Pact and both September entries are nowhere in this volume. The line that does the work comes in from off the left edge, out of a year the other three books put in a different century's chapter — 这个条约是中国近代史上第一个丧权辱国的不平等条约, “this treaty was the first unequal treaty in modern Chinese history that humiliated the nation and forfeited its sovereignty,” written of Nanking in 1842. Versailles sits on that line under another name; the word itself never appears, but the room does: 巴黎和会上，强权战胜了公理，中国遭遇严重外交失败, “at the Paris Peace Conference, power triumphed over justice, and China suffered a serious diplomatic defeat.” And this is the only one of the four that wires the slump into a war at all: 1929 年秋，世界性经济危机爆发，严重影响日本。日本统治集团为缓和国内矛盾，摆脱困境，急于发动侵略中国的战争。 — “In the autumn of 1929 a worldwide economic crisis broke out, gravely affecting Japan. The Japanese ruling clique, in order to ease internal contradictions and escape its predicament, was impatient to launch a war of aggression against China.” Three sentences later the railway near Liutiaohu is blown up. A classroom on this wiring concludes that the war did not begin in 1939 and did not begin in Europe, and that the thing needing explanation is not a signature but a system.

Put the four side by side and the boxes never move. The slump is wired in one drawing as the motive for a war and in another as something a war cured, and in the other two nothing is attached to it. Munich is the cause of everything, an open question, a missed chance, and absent. The 17th of September is an order without a giver, a subordinate clause, an annexation with a stated purpose, and a date that is not on the page. Nobody here is inventing dates; they are all allocating weight, and weight is the part that never has to be footnoted.

Your own classroom had a wiring diagram, and it was never drawn on the board. The last pages of this book are going to ask you to draw it.
