The Cold War’s First Sentence

1 The borrowed object

On the evening of 8 December 1881, shortly before the curtain rose on Offenbach’s Tales of Hoffmann, the Ringtheater in Vienna caught fire. Several hundred people in the auditorium did not get out. Within a few years, Austrian and German building codes required theatres to hang a sheet of iron on a counterweight between the stage and the house. Its ordinary name was eiserner Vorhang: iron curtain. Drury Lane had advertised one since 1794. The Cold War’s great political image began as fire-safety equipment.

An iron curtain had one clear job. A theatre contained two rooms that depended on each other: a stage full of scenery, ropes and lights, and an auditorium full of people. If fire took one, the sheet came down to stop it reaching the other. The object did not explain how the fire began. It drew a hard line through a single building.

On 5 March 1946, out of office, with the President of the United States sitting on the platform behind him, Winston Churchill spoke at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri. He said that an iron curtain had descended across Europe. The borrowed object did exactly what it had done in a theatre: it turned one space into two, put danger on the other side and made separation sound protective.

Two years later, Berlin supplied the physical version. On 24 June 1948, the road, rail and canal links into its western sectors closed, and the power station in the Soviet sector stopped feeding the western grid. Rather more than two million people were inside the line. Two days later, the first transport aircraft landed at Tempelhof. They kept landing for eleven months. The line across Europe was no longer only something said at a lectern. It controlled food, fuel and the routes by which both could arrive.

The rescue acquired another mechanical metaphor. In English, it was an airlift: the cargo goes up. In Ukrainian it is a «повітряний міст», in Italian a «ponte aereo», in German a Luftbrücke — an air bridge. The cargo goes across. A bridge implies two banks and something between them that has to be spanned. Curtain and bridge became a pair: one image drops a barrier; the other crosses it.

Churchill had not invented his phrase. In 1920, Ethel Snowden, a British Labour delegate visiting Soviet Russia, wrote of crossing into it: “We were behind the ‘iron curtain’ at last!” On 25 February 1945, Joseph Goebbels wrote in Das Reich that an iron curtain would fall over territory taken by the Soviet Union if Germany surrendered. On 12 May, four days after the German surrender, Churchill cabled Truman that “an iron curtain is drawn down upon their front. We do not know what is going on behind.” The image had travelled from theatre architecture to travel writing, then into Nazi warning and private diplomacy, before Fulton made it the name of an age.

That journey matters because the phrase appears to explain the history. It does not. No curtain divided Europe. Armies divided it, and treaties wrote the division down. No bridge ended the division either; aircraft kept one part of one city supplied across it. The metaphors made those arrangements sayable in one breath. They also quietly assigned roles: someone lowers a curtain, someone builds a bridge, somebody is kept out and somebody is carried through.

The era’s other name travelled too. George Orwell used cold war in October 1945; Bernard Baruch used it officially in April 1947; Walter Lippmann gave the words further life that year. The name described a conflict that was neither peace nor the war just ended. Like the curtain, it did not settle who made the first move. It made the dispute compact enough to fit in a heading.

The first object is still working. In traditional theatres across Europe the iron curtain is lowered and checked as a matter of licence. At the Vienna State Opera — along the same ring road from where the Ringtheater stood — the panel has carried a different commissioned artwork every season since the late 1990s, raised before the music starts. Anyone who has watched that great slab go up has watched the Cold War’s name lift out of the way.

2 The dates, briefly

  • 4–11 February 1945: Yalta; 17 July–2 August: Potsdam and the occupation zones of Germany and Berlin.
  • 25 April–26 June 1945: San Francisco; the UN Charter enters into force on 24 October.
  • 5 March 1946: Churchill speaks at Fulton, Missouri.
  • 12 March 1947: Truman asks Congress for $400 million for Greece and Turkey; 5 June: Marshall speaks at Harvard.
  • June 1948–May 1949: the Berlin blockade and airlift.
  • 4 April 1949: twelve states sign the North Atlantic Treaty; the two German states follow in May and October.

3 Then the classroom chooses a beginning

Six books on this shelf build the European order from almost the same calendar: Yalta, Potsdam, Fulton, the Marshall Plan, Berlin and NATO. Yet they give a fifteen-year-old three different kinds of beginning. Russia and Ukraine turn confrontation into a condition. Belarus begins with a speech but steps back from owning the claim. The United States arranges a chain of warning and Soviet reaction. Italy puts Truman’s change of manner beside a Stalinist design. Britain makes Soviet action systematic, then opens the blame question to competing schools of historians.

RU · Чубарьян 2023

«Утвердилась решающая роль двух сверхдержав — СССР и США. Противоречия, а впоследствии и противостояние, конфликтность в отношениях между СССР и США предопределялись идеологическими разногласиями.»

“The decisive role of two superpowers — the USSR and the USA — was established. The contradictions, and subsequently also the confrontation, the conflictedness in relations between the USSR and the USA were predetermined by ideological disagreements.”

Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл., Просвещение 2023 · §3, “The disintegration of the anti-Hitler coalition,” p. 205. The grade-10 volume, declared range 1914–1945; its grade-11 companion is not on this shelf — see Limits.

The Russian sentence leaves no first mover because its grammar leaves no opening move. Ideological disagreement does the predetermining. Ukraine names both superpowers and their purposes, then reaches a similar verdict of historical conditioning. Belarus gives Fulton the role of starting gun, but inserts a hedge between speech and consequence. In the American chain, Churchill warns and the Soviet Union reacts; American doctrine, money and aircraft are present, but they do not carry the main causal verbs. Italy leaves out a single culprit by placing an American change and a Soviet plan on the same page.

UK · Lowe 2013

“In the months following Potsdam, the Russians systematically interfered in the countries of eastern Europe to set up pro-communist governments.”

Mastering Modern World History, 5th ed. 2013 · §7.1 “What caused the Cold War?” — subsection “Which side was to blame?”; §7.2(c). Printed index: Yalta 125–6, Potsdam 126, Iron Curtain 129

The British adverb turns events into a plan. But the same book also gives space to Soviet and American revisionist arguments, so its student is asked to weigh a charge rather than inherit one. Across the shelf, the grammar offers different jobs: understand an impersonal process, repeat an attribution, follow a reaction or judge an actor.

The larger omission is geography. China’s volume fills 1945–1949 with civil war and the founding of the People’s Republic; none of this European order’s names appears. India’s world-history book mentions Yalta and Potsdam only as conferences petitioned by Korean independence campaigners. Even among the six European accounts, five lead a student to Fulton, three print the iron-curtain words, and only one says Churchill had used them before. Not one of the eleven classrooms mentions the fire-safety object. They teach the map made by the metaphor and cut the journey by which the metaphor reached the map.

4 Credit and limits

Lowe’s British book earns credit for printing competing schools of interpretation, while OpenStax tests American containment against Greece, where its own account says the policy helped produce the Soviet attention it expected. The comparison remains six books, not a world: the Chinese volume is the Chinese-history half of its course, the German booklet ends with National Socialism, and Sudan’s national and Arab history ends in 1948. The Russian book is also the grade-10 volume declared to cover 1914–1945; its missing grade-11 companion prevents any broad claim about Russian teaching. What can be said is narrower: this edition reaches events in 1955 without using the phrase Cold War.
