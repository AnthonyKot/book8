Spain, 1936–1939: The Bombers and the Bill

1 The bombers and the bill

«Guernica hatte keine Flak, überhaupt gab es keine Geschütze in der Stadt, nicht einmal ein Maschinengewehr wäre zu finden gewesen.»

“Guernica had no anti-aircraft guns; there were no guns in the town at all, not even a machine gun was to be found.”

DE · Padre Alberto de Onaindía, eyewitness account printed in a full column in bpb Nr. 316 (2012), p. 8

That is a priest taking an inventory of what his town did not have. Guernica was a Basque town of a few thousand people about thirty kilometres east of Bilbao. The aircraft came over its centre in the late afternoon of 26 April 1937, a Monday and market day, and kept coming in waves for something over three hours.

The road to that afternoon began with transport aircraft. On 17–18 July 1936, a section of the Spanish army rose against the government, beginning in Spanish Morocco. From late July, German Junkers Ju 52 and Italian Savoia-Marchetti transports carried the Army of Africa across the Strait of Gibraltar to southern Spain. The Condor Legion followed in November. Aircraft first helped move an army into the war; then they became part of the war.

Spain also had a Non-Intervention Agreement. France proposed it in August 1936, and a committee began meeting in London on 9 September. Germany, Italy and the Soviet Union adhered to the agreement, and all three supplied the war. So the question was not only who would send aircraft, tanks, troops and advisers. It was how Spain, split between two sides, would pay for supplies that officially were not supposed to arrive.

The Republic paid first. In 1936 the Bank of Spain held the world’s fourth-largest gold reserve. The country had stayed neutral in the First World War and sold to both sides; now something over 700 tonnes sat in coin, bars and foreign holdings, about 635 tonnes of it fine metal. Under non-intervention, that money bought nothing in Paris or London. The reserve was enormous and the shops were shut.

On the authority of a decree signed by finance minister Juan Negrín on 13 September 1936, roughly 510 tonnes — about three-quarters of the vault — went by truck to the naval base at Cartagena. Four Soviet ships carried it to Odessa in late October. Most of the remainder, some 190 tonnes, had already been sent or sold through France. In Moscow, metal became an account.

Moscow held the pen on the other side of the ledger: the price of the aircraft, the freight and the advisers, and the rate at which pesetas met roubles met dollars. The Republic knew what it had deposited. It could not see what it was being charged. By 1938 the account was spent and deliveries were arriving more slowly. Yet the gold did buy real aircraft. The fighters and tanks it paid for held Madrid through the winter of 1936. The gold did not lose the Republic the war; it made the Republic the customer of one supplier, at prices only that supplier could see.

Franco paid later. The reserve remained in the government’s hands, so he bought on credit. Berlin channelled the trade through two purpose-built companies, HISMA in Spain and ROWAK in Germany. They billed aircraft, fuel and the Condor Legion’s upkeep, then took repayment in materials German rearmament lacked: iron ore from Vizcaya and pyrites from the Huelva mines. Rome extended the larger credit line and sent the larger army against it.

The bills lasted longer than the alliances. Spain was still paying instalments on its civil-war debt to Rome in the 1960s, a generation after the state that had extended the credit ceased to exist. One side had paid cash into an account it could not audit. The other had signed for goods against ore it had not yet dug. Spain’s savings and minerals financed both ends of Spain’s war.

That shape is not exotic. Anyone who has paid a builder up front, or bought a car against next year’s wages, has stood on one side of it: money that cannot be followed once it has been handed over, or a bill secured against work not yet done. The bombing is the part that remains in photographs. Behind the aircraft sat two ordinary financial arrangements, enlarged until a country’s gold, mines and future payments were inside them.

2 The dates, briefly

  • February 1936: the Popular Front coalition wins the general election.
  • 17–18 July 1936: part of the army rises, first in Spanish Morocco and then on the mainland.
  • Late July 1936: German and Italian transports begin carrying the Army of Africa across the Strait of Gibraltar.
  • 26 April 1937: Guernica is bombed; the death toll remains disputed.
  • Autumn 1938: the International Brigades are withdrawn.
  • 28 March–1 April 1939: Madrid surrenders and Franco announces the war’s end.

3 What “help” hides

Now enter the classroom. Six of the eleven books teach this war: Belarusian, Russian, Italian, American, British and German editions. All six describe the foreign intervention as aid, help or support. The word is not false. It is remarkably roomy. It can hold a transport plane, an air raid, a Soviet account and a German mining arrangement without requiring a student to see any of them.

IT · Sabbatucci–Vidotto 2004:

«Meno rilevante quantitativamente, ma ugualmente prezioso, fu l’aiuto della Germania nazista, che inviò soprattutto aerei e piloti e si servì della guerra per sperimentare l’efficienza della sua aviazione.»

“Less significant quantitatively, but equally precious, was the aid of Nazi Germany, which sent above all aircraft and pilots and made use of the war to test the efficiency of its air force.”

Il mondo contemporaneo, Laterza 2004 · § 18.10, pp. 413, 415

The Italian book supplies the purpose: Spain was a place to test an air force. It does not print Guernica or the Condor Legion anywhere in roughly seven hundred and fifty pages. Belarus names neither aircraft nor target, unit, date or sender. Russia brings in the air strikes but never lands them on a named target; Guernica appears only as an artwork, elsewhere in the volume. The American book names Madrid and Guernica, but no unit, date or person who authorised the attack.

Britain and Germany put all five pieces on the page: aircraft, target, unit, date and sender. Yet the two books give Guernica death tolls an order of magnitude apart. The British edition gives 1,600; the German booklet says several hundred. The disagreement has a history, but neither tells the student that the number is disputed. Germany’s booklet goes furthest by placing the commander of its own air force beside the priest who watched the bombing from below. The ladder is not a scale from dishonest to honest. Italy names the experiment and loses the town; the United States names the town and loses the people who sent the aircraft.

Grammar does much of the hiding. At one end, two balanced verbs make German and Italian help to the Nationalists and Soviet support for the Republic look alike, with no cargo attached. In the middle, bombing becomes a noun and the people who ordered or carried it out recede. At the far end, named units and active senders make the act visible. Then the larger omission arrives: none of the six says what the aid cost, who paid for it or what payment took the form of. None of all eleven books mentions the Bank of Spain’s reserve. The classroom story follows the bombers and leaves the bill outside.

4 Credit, with limits

bpb (DE) earns the credit here. A German federal agency gives equal space to its air-force commander explaining the test and to a priest describing the raid from below; it calls Spain a military dress rehearsal without pretending Guernica caused the next war. The award is narrow. These are six pinned editions, not six countries or the world, and five books never undertake the episode. None is a history of Spain from inside its institutions, churches or villages. Even the three printed Italian troop counts disagree sharply: Russia gives 125,000, Britain 70,000 and Italy 50,000.
