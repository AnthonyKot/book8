Part IV · Chapters 9–13

Deals and war

Part III ends with money that has stopped keeping its promises. Part IV is what was signed while it was failing. Under all five of these chapters runs an accounting: in each, something is bought, something is billed, and something is counted. This part is the darkest in the book, so the table it sets is short.

It opens with food destroyed on purpose. Between 1931 and 1944 Brazil burned its own coffee in the open, dumped it at sea and shovelled it into locomotive fireboxes — some seventy-eight million sixty-kilo bags — to hold up the price of what was left, in the same years men queued for free meals on a Chicago pavement. Then the price of everything else was walled in as well: eight months after the crash the United States set the highest tariff it has ever set, and other states answered in kind. The wall did not cause the slump — the deep part of that story is banks and the gold standard. What it taught every government was that the answer to a shortage of customers is to stop being one.

The next war was paid for by both sides, one in advance and one in arrears. In late October 1936 about three-quarters of the Spanish gold reserve was trucked to the naval base at Cartagena and loaded onto four Soviet ships; in Moscow the metal became an account, and only one party to it could see the prices. The other side had no gold, so it bought on credit and settled in iron ore from Vizcaya and pyrites from Huelva: aircraft now, minerals later, out of the ground of the country being fought over. Six books, in four languages, call all of this help. Not one of them shows an invoice.

Then the cheapest item in the part: a signature, made in a room the country being signed away was not in, and then a second signature that the first one is still being blamed for. What came out of Munich and never went back in is a word — appeasement, «умиротворение» in the Russian-language books, loaded in opposite directions depending on the classroom, and now in the mouth of anyone arguing about a current war.

Then a form. A German census form of May 1939 carried a supplementary card asking whether each of a person's four grandparents was Jewish by descent, and the answers were tabulated by machine; a building office in Berlin later pulled names of Jewish tenants out of a card index and handed them over. The machines did not cause the murders and were not required for them — a great deal of that work was done with typed lists and drawers. What the forms removed was anonymity, and a continental total had to be assembled out of records before it could be reached in a meeting.

And at the end of the ledger, a city converted into a unit. Arsenals are still compared in newsfeeds as so many Hiroshimas, a place-name doing arithmetic, carried across languages without a footnote.

After the last of it there are no words for a page, only drawings. The classrooms agree almost entirely on the nodes of these years — Versailles, the slump, Munich, the Pact, the two entries into Poland, the war — and disagree on the arrows. Six boxes, four times over, nothing moved but the lines; and the lines are where the responsibility sits. The account opens in a locomotive firebox.
