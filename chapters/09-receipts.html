The Great Depression — the comparison and receipts

1 The ladder

Economists still argue about the causes, and about how much the New Deal did. None of that chronology is in dispute; the wiring is. What moves from book to book is the sentence that comes after the fall. In some of these classrooms the Depression is weather — it occurs, and afterwards nobody in particular does anything. In others it becomes policy and blame, with an institution named. In the last two it leaves the financial pages for a payroll and an invasion, where somebody decides what happens to identifiable people.

2 The tell

Every narrator here writes the fall itself without a human subject. A fall occurred. A crash occurred. The exchange crashes. A crisis broke out. Buying “began to slow down.” In the whole ladder there is not one sentence of the form “X crashed the market,” and unlike chapters 1, 4 and 8, that empty slot is not a dodge: there is no plausible name to put in it. So the interesting sentence is the next one.

In the first band that sentence lets nobody act, or lets one man act by omission. NCERT gives it a chain of intransitive verbs — the exchange crashes, the Depression follows, twelve million are out of work — and its only other mention of the slump is a subordinate clause in a chapter about the dispossession of Native Americans; the volume has no Roosevelt, no Hoover and no New Deal. Belarus fills the gap with an abstraction: what comes out of the crisis is “the Western democracies,” which had “stepped onto the path of state regulation.” Koshelev’s volume never prints «новый курс» — New Deal — or «Уолл-стрит», and where Roosevelt does appear he is never the man who ended a depression. Russia is the one book in this band that puts a person in the subject slot, and gives him a verb that means the absence of action: Hoover, expecting prosperity “somewhere around the corner,” бездействовал — did nothing. Nobody acts; democracies act; a president acts by omission. Three books, three ways of writing an empty chair.

The second band names an institution, and each names one at home. Lowe blames a party, in a heading — “Who was to blame for the disaster?” — then refuses the easy target, clearing “the unfortunate President Hoover” personally and indicting the governing party instead; he is also the only narrator who takes the famous event off the causal chain, demoting the crash to “just a symptom.” OpenStax names a law. The American survey opens by refusing single agency and then hands the transmission mechanism to its own government — the highest tariff ever enacted, world trade down 30 per cent — while Herbert Hoover does not appear in the book at all. That is a swap of a president for a statute, and the statute is the one with foreign victims. Sabbatucci and Vidotto name a state and a duty it declined: the planetary effects were made worse because the USA, “rather than taking on the responsibilities attached to the role of hegemonic power,” chose to defend its own production. Three narrators, three defendants, none of them a foreigner.

The last two rungs change the shape of the chapter.

Sudan’s ministry textbook never uses the words “Great Depression,” “Wall Street” or “Roosevelt.” It has no crash at all. The crisis enters as a price: the Gezira scheme had made cotton about 40 per cent of government income and two-thirds of exports, demand fell, the price fell, “and the government resorted to strict economic measures, so it reduced employment and salaries.” A government is the subject, and what it does is subtract. Sixteen pages on, the same event is told again from the other end — the 1931 Gordon College strike, led by Makki al-Manna, against a cut in Sudanese graduates’ salaries from eight pounds to five and a half — and the book adds the clause that no other column has any use for: but the government did not reduce the salaries of the British and the foreigners. Most of these classrooms measure the Depression in millions out of work. This one measures it as the gap between two payrolls in the same office.

China gives the crisis its only fully transitive sentence on the ladder. In 中外历史纲要 the world crisis is not a topic; it is a subordinate clause, four characters of scale — 严重影响日本, gravely affecting Japan — and then the main clause arrives with a subject that wants something: the Japanese ruling clique, “in order to ease internal contradictions and escape its predicament, was impatient to launch a war of aggression against China.” Three sentences later the Kwantung Army is blowing up a railway near Liutiaohu. There is no unemployment figure, no bank, no tariff, no Roosevelt. The Depression is not an economy in this classroom. It is a motive.

The bands do not sort by ideology. Belarus and Russia are the two books most willing to call the crisis a crisis of capitalism as a system — «кризис определённого этапа развития капиталистической системы» — and they are the two least willing to let anyone act. What the descent tracks is something narrower: once a textbook follows the Depression out of the financial pages to a payroll or an invasion, the abstractions give way to officials making choices about identifiable people. In the first two bands the victims are aggregates — thirteen million, twelve million, 80 per cent of families with no savings, one in eight farmers. In the third they have addresses: Sudanese graduates as against British and foreign staff, the Chinese Northeast. Both halves are true. Only one half can be argued with, because only one half says who was standing where.

Two books close the story with almost the same sentence. Lowe, at the end of his New Deal section: “It was the war that finally put an end to the depression.” OpenStax: “only the economic stimulation of the onset of World War II brought the world closer to a return of prosperity.” That is chronology, not a verdict on the New Deal — which reduced the pain and rewrote the social contract without closing the output gap, still 14 to 17 per cent unemployment in the late 1930s and under 2 per cent by 1943. And before the wartime spending arrived, the war was rehearsed. Spain is where the aircraft, the credit lines and the paper neutralities of the coming war were tried out — which is chapter 10.

3 Credit where due

Fairness

التاريخ للصف الثالث (SD) is the shortest text on the ladder and the only one that turns the Depression into a decision about named groups of people. Two paragraphs and a strike, no crash, no Wall Street, no Roosevelt. Every other narrator here reports the crisis as something that happened to a population; a ministry textbook, published by the state that succeeded the administration doing the cutting, reports it as something an administration chose to do to one half of its own staff. That is the sentence this whole chapter was looking for, and it is in the book with the fewest pages to spare.

4 Where this stops being true

Limits

This ladder is eight pinned editions whose syllabus reaches 1929, not eight countries and not “the world.” Every one of them that reaches the year has something to say about it, so nobody here is looking away from the crisis. Where it is missing — Ukraine’s volume runs 1945–2018, Germany’s booklet is a National Socialism primer — the silence is a syllabus boundary rather than a decision about 1929. Kazakhstan’s book is a national history rather than a world one, so it does not stand beside these eight; its 1929 is collectivisation and revolt.

None of these eight books is an economic history, and the gap is exactly where this chapter had to go outside them. They name the damage; not one explains the machinery underneath it. The mechanism most economists put near the centre of the story — monetary contraction, and convertibility obliging governments to tighten into a slump — survives on this whole shelf as a single line about the pound in 1931, and the account of it earlier in this chapter came from general history, not from a textbook. If you want to know why the fall went as far as it did, every column here will leave you unsatisfied.

«Почему он не затронул экономику Советского государства?»

“Why did it not affect the economy of the Soviet state?”

BY · Всемирная история, 11 кл. (БГУ 2021), p. 124 — end-of-section exercise 3. The book does not answer it.

Italy supplies the observation the exercise rests on — the recession spread everywhere “with the significant exception of the USSR” — and no book on the ladder supplies the answer, which is that an economy trading little at world prices is not reached by a collapse in world prices. What the exercise also does not say is what those years were in the country it is asking about: the same span is forced collectivisation and, in Kazakhstan alone, a famine usually estimated at 1.5 million dead or more. A question printed beside a table of Western unemployment, with the local answer left off the page.
