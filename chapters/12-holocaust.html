The Holocaust

1 The list and the archive

In November 1940 the Warsaw ghetto was sealed with more than four hundred thousand people inside it. One was a historian. Emanuel Ringelblum began collecting the paper life of the place before that life could be erased.

His clandestine group was called Oyneg Shabes because it met on Saturdays. Its members gathered testimony, ration cards, school essays, deportation notices and reports they wrote themselves. Then they put the collection into metal boxes and two milk cans and buried it.

There was another kind of archive at work. In Berlin, the office of Albert Speer was acting as building inspector for the capital and expecting apartments to fall vacant. It drew up lists of Jewish tenants from its master card index and passed them to the Gestapo. A policy reached a person through an office, a drawer, a name and an address.

The same administrative layer sat behind the scale imagined at Wannsee. On 20 January 1942, fifteen men from ministries, the party and the security police met for about ninety minutes in a requisitioned villa outside Berlin. The shooting of Jewish communities behind the eastern front had already been running for seven months, and the first killings with gas had begun at Chełmno. The meeting did not start the murder. It coordinated it.

The figure put before the meeting was eleven million Jews. Its country list included Ireland, Portugal, Spain, England, Sweden, Switzerland and Turkey — places not under German control. A continental total like that did not appear when the men sat down. It had been assembled beforehand, country by country, from statistics and records. Thirty copies of the meeting’s minutes were typed. One survived in the German Foreign Office files and was found in 1947.

For Germany itself, part of the record was a census. The census of 16 June 1933 recorded religious affiliation. On 17 May 1939, a supplementary card asked whether each of a person’s four grandparents was Jewish by descent. That was a different question, and it made a different list. The answers were tabulated on punch-card machines of the kind Herman Hollerith had patented for the American census of 1890. In Germany the machines were supplied and serviced by Dehomag. Hollerith installations are also documented in the SS economic administration and in the labour-deployment offices of the camp system.

These records did not live in one great machine. The census classified a population; the building office kept tenants; Eichmann’s submissions turned national figures into a continental estimate. Each scale depended on people preparing, moving and reading records. That is what disappears when the history is reduced to a policy at one end and millions at the other: between them stood the forms and files that attached the abstraction to an address.

The tempting version of this story gives the machine too much power. The machines did not cause the murders, and the record here does not show that they were necessary. Much of the work was done with typed lists and card drawers. What the census layer removed was anonymity. After the supplementary card, descent was not something a neighbour might or might not know. It was held by name and address, on a form the person had filled in.

A modern census form still makes a household countable. It fixes categories before anyone filling it in can know every later use of the answers. That is the ordinary object connecting a punch-card machine in a museum to the history: not because a database leads to murder, but because the question on a form decides what can be counted later, and by whom.

Ringelblum’s group built the opposite archive from the same materials. Names, addresses, ration cards and deportation notices were buried so the people in them could not be subtracted quietly. Ringelblum was found outside the ghetto wall in March 1944 and shot. The first cache was dug out of the rubble in 1946, the second in 1950; a third has never been found. The papers, and one of the milk cans, are now at the Jewish Historical Institute in Warsaw. The same paper could help an office find a person or help an archive keep them. Nothing in the form decided which.

2 What reaches the classroom

Six accounts on this shelf describe the systematic murder of the European Jews. All put the Jewish dead at roughly five to six million or a little above. Their difference is not the reality or scale of the crime. It is the size of the group made visible as the perpetrator.

RU · Чубарьян 2023

«После взятия Киева в районе Бабьего Яра было расстреляно около 70 тыс. евреев…»

“After the taking of Kyiv, in the area of Babi Yar about 70 thousand Jews were shot…”

Всеобщая история. Новейшая история 1914–1945, 10 кл. (2023) · pp. 192, 170

The Russian sentence supplies the place, the victims and a total, but no shooter. The Ukrainian book puts the Nazis in a prepositional phrase and moves from the murder to the founding of Israel. The Italian manual puts Hitler and the SS into the plan, then lets the victims disappear in its next sentence. The British book concentrates responsibility at the top: Hitler appears forty times in seven printed pages, while the people below him stay softer in the frame.

The American account does the reverse. Its three-page Holocaust section never uses Hitler’s name. It shows execution squads, ghettos, railway lines, the conference and collaboration across Europe, but leaves the person at the top outside the section. The German booklet is the only account here that holds the top and the widening circle together: Hitler appears sixteen times, alongside at least fifteen other named organisers, and responsibility reaches the ordinary army.

DE · bpb Nr. 316, 2012

„Deutsche Soldaten beteiligten sich an den Mordtaten gegen die Zivilbevölkerung, sicherten die Erschießungsstätten ab, brannten ganze Dörfer nieder…“

“German soldiers took part in the killings of the civilian population, secured the shooting sites, burned whole villages down…”

Informationen zur politischen Bildung Nr. 316, „Nationalsozialismus: Krieg und Holocaust“ (2012) · pp. 4, 29

One book makes the scale into an abstract policy; another makes it chiefly Hitler’s programme; another distributes it through units, offices and collaborators. Two pinned volumes that do cover Europe in this period — the Belarusian world history and India’s Themes in World History — contain no account of the Holocaust at all. Yet the largest shared omission lies elsewhere. Every account teaches the total. Not one of the eleven classroom texts explains how a continental total could be assembled from individual people. The nearest any comes is the German booklet: Eichmann’s statistics behind the eleven million, and a building office drawing tenants’ names from a card index. The classroom can name millions while leaving out the drawer that held one address.

3 Credit and limits

The German booklet takes the credit because it makes the perpetrator group a historical question and follows responsibility from Hitler through ministries, police, offices and soldiers. But it is a Federal Agency for Civic Education booklet, not a school textbook, so its depth cannot be compared with a world survey’s page count. This shelf is narrow too: the Belarusian silence applies only to the pinned world-history volume, and the Holocaust appears in an Indian Class IX book outside this corpus. Even the Babi Yar totals count different windows — two days in some passages, the longer use of the site in another — so grammar, not a verdict on the numbers, carries the comparison.

4 The dates, briefly

  • April 1933: the first German laws excluding Jews from the civil service; 1935: the Nuremberg Laws.
  • 9–10 November 1938: coordinated attacks across Germany and annexed Austria; roughly 30,000 Jewish men are arrested and sent to concentration camps.
  • September 1939: Germany invades Poland; ghettos follow through 1940–41.
  • 22 June 1941: Germany invades the Soviet Union; mass shootings follow behind the front.
  • December 1941: systematic killing with gas begins at Chełmno; 20 January 1942: the Wannsee Conference.
  • During 1942: Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka begin operating; Auschwitz-Birkenau receives transports from across Europe.
  • 27 January 1945: Soviet troops reach Auschwitz; in April, British and American troops reach Bergen-Belsen and Buchenwald.
