What Hiroshima Is For

1 A city in the plural

In February 1989, in the village of Karaul on the Kazakh steppe, a public meeting adopted an appeal about the nuclear test site next door — the meeting that began the Nevada–Semipalatinsk movement, which closed the site two years later. Kazakhstan's eleventh-grade history textbook reproduces the appeal. One of its sentences is this:

«За 40 лет здесь взорвались тысячи Хиросим.»

“Over 40 years, thousands of Hiroshimas have exploded here.”

KZ · История Казахстана, 11 кл. (2015) · extract p. 220 — the Karaul appeal, reproduced in the textbook

Nothing in that sentence is about Japan. Roughly four and a half thousand kilometres of Asia lie between that village and Hiroshima, and forty-four years lie between the meeting and the morning the word acquired its meaning. Nobody on the page stops to explain the unit, because by 1989 nobody needed it explained. A city had become a quantity, and a plural one.

The word first acquired that meaning on 6 August 1945. Japanese cities were already burning before August; the difference was not that civilians had suddenly entered the war. A uranium bomb called Little Boy fell on Hiroshima from the B-29 Enola Gay, releasing the energy of roughly fifteen thousand tons of TNT. On 8 August the Soviet Union declared war on Japan. On 9 August Soviet forces entered Manchuria and a plutonium bomb called Fat Man fell on Nagasaki. The emperor broadcast Japan's surrender on 15 August; the document was signed aboard the USS Missouri on 2 September.

The bomb gave Hiroshima a second life as a ruler. Seven years later the first thermonuclear test in the Pacific released something like seven hundred times as much energy; nine years after that, the largest device ever detonated released about fifty megatons, the equivalent of more than three thousand Little Boys. As weapons grew, their technical units moved further from ordinary experience.

Kilotons and megatons are honest units, but no one can picture a kiloton. A city is different. It has streets, schools and a name. That made Hiroshima useful whenever a speaker needed to turn a quantity of explosive energy into an imaginable loss. By the time the people of Karaul wrote their appeal, the name could cross thousands of kilometres and stand in the plural without explanation.

The same unit still turns up when arsenals are compared in a newsfeed — a new warhead, a stockpile total, a treaty lapsing — as the equivalent of so many Hiroshimas. It travels across languages without translation and usually without a footnote. As popular measures go, it is unusually durable and unusually strange: the name of a place where people were killed, doing arithmetic.

The human count is different. Figures commonly cited today run to roughly 90,000–140,000 at Hiroshima and 60,000–80,000 at Nagasaki by the end of 1945, with further deaths for decades afterwards. Those are ranges, not conversions. A death at the moment of the blast, by the end of 1945 and years later from radiation belongs to a different total. The interval chosen changes the answer.

That makes the familiar comparison less precise than it sounds. One Hiroshima may mean explosive yield, people killed immediately, people dead by the end of the year, or a city's entire destruction. The multiplier can be announced to three significant figures while the thing being multiplied varies by nearly half. The name supplies an image exactly where the number refuses to settle.

The city also became part of a chain of announcements. One interpretation made the bomb a demonstration of American power to the Soviet Union. A Soviet test followed in 1949, then four decades of arsenals priced in cities. The nine days in August do not move. What moves is the arrow drawn through them: bomb to surrender, Soviet entry to surrender, bomb to Cold War.

The sentence from Karaul completes the transformation. Hiroshima begins as a place, becomes an event, then becomes the unit by which another landscape describes what happened to it. The unit works because everyone recognises the city. It remains unstable because recognition is not the same as agreement about what, exactly, is being counted.

2 Eight classrooms, eight destinations

Now enter the classroom. Eight pinned textbooks carry August 1945, and their paragraphs send the same bombs towards different conclusions. Ukraine asks the student to name the bombed cities but never names them in its prose; the section opens on the Missouri. India names the United States in a timeline, then removes the actor from the chapter's argument. Its debate has a purpose and a counterargument, but no person deciding.

China names the United States, then places the bombs beside the Soviet declaration, the Soviet army in Manchuria and a counter-offensive in the Liberated Areas. It gives no casualty figure because its paragraph is moving towards the recovery of Taiwan. Belarus also names the United States, but gives no dead; its sentence turns immediately to the Soviet defeat of the million-strong Kwantung Army. Russia counts about 90,000 dead at Hiroshima, tens of thousands later from radiation sickness and wounds, and about 270,000 across both cities. It makes the American bombing and Soviet entry a compound subject that compelled Japan to surrender. The five books that count one Hiroshima give five totals, from perhaps 84,000 to approximately 120,000.

Italy, Britain and the United States treat the bomb as a decision that must explain itself. The Italian edition makes the two purposes explicit.

IT · Sabbatucci–Vidotto 2004:

«La decisione di Truman serviva innanzitutto ad abbreviare una guerra che si annunciava ancora lunga e sanguinosa, ma aveva anche lo scopo di offrire al mondo (e soprattutto agli alleati rivali sovietici) la dimostrazione della potenza militare americana.»

“Truman’s decision served first of all to shorten a war that still promised to be long and bloody, but it also had the purpose of offering to the world (and above all to the rival Soviet allies) the demonstration of American military power.”

Il mondo contemporaneo (Laterza 2004) · extract ~p. 493

The British edition opens a controversy, separates Truman's stated justification from historians' objections, and later returns to the bomb as a message to Stalin. OpenStax prints the president's diary beside a description of the civilians in the two cities, lists several possible motives and asks students to weigh opposing primary sources. Its omission is not evidence but a settled ranking: it refuses to make one motive safe.

The ladder therefore runs from a question whose actor and cities are missing, through brief chains in which the bomb is one event among armies, to books that name a decision and multiply its purposes. The grammar moves with it: passive constructions can retain a reason while losing the person who held it; compound subjects can make American bombing and Soviet entry share one consequence. What each country leaves out is the finding. Ukraine loses the names in its prose; India the actor in its argument; China and Belarus the dead; Russia alternative motives; Italy the victims; Britain a final verdict; the United States a hierarchy among causes. None can make Hiroshima serve every destination at once.

3 Credit, with limits

OpenStax (US) earns the credit because the narrating state's own classroom prints the primary sources needed to disagree with it, including a president's private promise and the civilian reality on the next page. The award is narrow. These are eight pinned editions, not eight countries in full; Germany and Sudan stop elsewhere, Ukraine begins on the far side of August 1945, and Kazakhstan is a national history rather than a world history. Their casualty figures use different counting windows, and none is a military history of the Pacific war.

4 The dates, briefly

  • Japan had been at war in China since 1937, and in Manchuria since 1931.
  • The incendiary raid on Tokyo of 9–10 March 1945 killed on the order of a hundred thousand people in one night.
  • The Manhattan Project's first test device was detonated at Alamogordo, New Mexico, on 16 July 1945.
  • The Potsdam Declaration of 26 July demanded Japan's unconditional surrender.
  • Hiroshima was bombed on 6 August; the Soviet declaration came on 8 August; Nagasaki and the Soviet entry into Manchuria followed on 9 August.
  • The emperor's broadcast came on 15 August; the surrender was signed on 2 September.
